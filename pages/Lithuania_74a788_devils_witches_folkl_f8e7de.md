## Introduction

[Lithuania]({{ 'where-lithuania-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})’s most distinctive folklore displays do not ask visitors to believe in devils or witches. Instead, they show how supernatural figures have remained culturally alive through collecting, sculpture, storytelling and performance. The country’s best-known examples—the Devils’ Museum in Kaunas and the Hill of Witches on the Curonian Spit—demonstrate two different approaches. One gathers thousands of objects into a curated collection that explores changing ideas about the devil in folk art and popular culture. The other turns a forest into an open-air stage where carved witches, devils and legendary beings invite visitors to walk through a modern landscape of myth. Together they illustrate how Lithuania has transformed folklore into a public cultural experience rather than a relic of vanished belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ciurlionis.lt/activity/permanent-exhibitions/velniu-kolekcija-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ciurlionis.lt">[ciurlionis.lt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ciurlionis.lt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Devil Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NACIONALINIS M. ## The collector behind the Devils' Museum

The Devils' Museum in Kaunas began with an unexpected gift rather than a grand institutional plan. Painter Antanas Žmuidzinavičius received a carved wooden devil in 1906 and continued collecting similar figures for the rest of his life. Friends, artists and travellers added to the collection, and after his death it became part of the M. K. Čiurlionis National Museum of Art. Today the museum contains more than 3,000 representations of devils from Lithuania and dozens of other countries, making it one of the world's most unusual themed collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ciurlionis.lt/activity/permanent-exhibitions/velniu-kolekcija-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ciurlionis.lt">[ciurlionis.lt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ciurlionis.lt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Devil Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NACIONALINIS M. K. ČIURLIONIO DAILĖS MUZIEJUS...</span></span></span>

What makes the collection culturally significant is that Lithuanian folk devils differ from the purely demonic figures familiar from much Western Christian imagery. In folk tales they are often boastful, greedy or easily deceived rather than all-powerful embodiments of evil. Many sculptures portray them as comic tricksters who lose contests with clever farmers or ordinary villagers. This ambiguity allows the museum to display religious carvings beside humorous souvenirs, political satire and contemporary artworks without treating every object as evidence of literal belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ciurlionis.lt/activity/permanent-exhibitions/velniu-kolekcija-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ciurlionis.lt">[ciurlionis.lt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ciurlionis.lt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Devil Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NACIONALINIS M. K. ČIURLIONIO DAILĖS MUZIEJUS...</span></span></span>

Visitors therefore encounter several overlapping traditions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Traditional wooden carvings inspired by village folklore.
* Masks and folk art connected with seasonal customs.
* International depictions showing how different cultures imagine devils and demons.
* Modern souvenirs and satirical works demonstrating how supernatural imagery continues to evolve.

</div>

The museum deliberately blurs the boundary between ethnography, art history and curiosity collection. Rather than preserving a single authentic tradition, it documents how one mythical figure has accumulated new meanings across different periods and societies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lithuania.travel/en/why-lithuania/culture-and-heritage/culture-and-art/museums/family-oriented-museums/the-devils-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lithuania.travel">[Lithuania Travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lithuania.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Travel The Devils’ Museum &#124; Lithuania Travel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Devils’ Museum &#124; Lithuania Travel...</span></span></span>

## How the Hill of Witches creates a modern legend landscape

On the Curonian Spit near Juodkrantė, the Hill of Witches offers almost the opposite experience. Instead of glass cases and catalogued artefacts, visitors follow woodland paths lined with large wooden sculptures of witches, devils, giants and characters from Lithuanian legends.

The attraction was created from 1979 onwards through gatherings of woodcarvers and blacksmiths rather than emerging from an ancient sacred site. The sculptures continue to expand as artists contribute new works, giving the location the character of a living cultural landscape rather than a finished museum. The managing institution describes it as a museum of wooden sculpture, emphasising artistic interpretation over archaeological authenticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.neringosmuziejai.lt/en/hill-of-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: neringosmuziejai.lt">[neringosmuziejai.lt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">neringosmuziejai.lt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hill of Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hill of Witches</span></span></span>

Its setting contributes as much as the carvings themselves. Dense forest, winding paths and changing light encourage visitors to experience folklore as an unfolding narrative instead of a sequence of labelled exhibits. Many sculptures can be climbed through or walked around, making the experience playful as well as atmospheric.

Importantly, the hill is often misunderstood. Although local traditions connected the surrounding landscape with midsummer celebrations and stories of supernatural beings, the present attraction is a modern artistic creation inspired by folklore rather than evidence of an uninterrupted centre of witchcraft. The site's power comes from carefully combining landscape, sculpture and storytelling into an immersive visitor experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.neringosmuziejai.lt/en/hill-of-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: neringosmuziejai.lt">[neringosmuziejai.lt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">neringosmuziejai.lt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hill of Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hill of Witches</span></span></span>

## Witches, landscape and remembered folklore

Lithuanian folklore frequently links supernatural activity with distinctive hills, forests and wetlands. Among the best-known legendary locations is Šatrija Hill, which appears in both folk narratives and historical witchcraft trial material as a place associated with gatherings of witches. Recent folklore scholarship argues that the importance of such places lies not simply in stories about magic but in the way landscapes become repositories of cultural memory. Rather than existing separately from the physical world, legends become attached to recognisable hills and forests that people continue to visit and reinterpret.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kgaa.bokorder.se/en-US/article/5581/arv-nordic-yearbook-of-folklore-vol-81-20/external/48457/the-flight-to-satrija-hill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kgaa.bokorder.se">[kgaa.bokorder.se]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kgaa.bokorder.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 26, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 26, 2025</span></span></span>

This helps explain why modern attractions such as the Hill of Witches resonate with visitors. They do not attempt to reconstruct historical witchcraft. Instead, they build upon an older habit of connecting remarkable landscapes with stories about extraordinary beings.

## Folklore, tourism and the line between belief and performance

Lithuania's folklore displays occupy an unusual position between heritage and entertainment. Neither the Devils' Museum nor the Hill of Witches presents supernatural creatures as historical fact. Instead, both interpret folklore as an important part of national culture while encouraging curiosity, humour and participation.

That balance can be seen in several ways:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Collecting rather than preaching.** The Devils' Museum studies representations of devils instead of promoting religious or occult beliefs.
* **Performance rather than reconstruction.** The Hill of Witches uses artistic installations to evoke legends rather than claiming to preserve an ancient ritual landscape unchanged.
* **Living tradition rather than static heritage.** New sculptures, exhibitions and visitor engagement allow folklore to evolve rather than freezing it in a single historical period.
* **Tourism with cultural depth.** Both attractions are popular visitor destinations, yet each also introduces audiences to distinctive aspects of Lithuanian folk imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ciurlionis.lt/activity/permanent-exhibitions/velniu-kolekcija-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ciurlionis.lt">[ciurlionis.lt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ciurlionis.lt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Devil Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NACIONALINIS M. K. ČIURLIONIO DAILĖS MUZIEJUS...</span></span></span>

</div>

This approach reflects a broader characteristic of Lithuanian curiosity culture. Instead of separating museums, folklore and public art into different worlds, the country often allows them to overlap. A private collector's fascination can become a nationally recognised museum, while a contemporary sculpture park can become the setting through which traditional legends are experienced by new generations.

## Why these displays matter

The enduring appeal of Lithuania's devils and witches lies in their flexibility. They can be humorous without becoming trivial, artistic without abandoning traditional roots, and commercial without entirely losing their cultural significance. Visitors are encouraged to appreciate the craftsmanship of carved figures, recognise familiar folk characters and reflect on how stories survive through objects and places rather than only through books.

Seen together, the Devils' Museum and the Hill of Witches demonstrate two complementary methods of preserving folklore. One preserves belief through collected artefacts; the other through an evolving landscape of sculpture and imagination.
