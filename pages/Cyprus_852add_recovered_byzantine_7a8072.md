## Introduction

The story of [Cyprus]({{ 'where-cyprus-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s recovered Byzantine art is not simply about museum objects returning to display cases. It is a continuing account of war, organised art trafficking, international court battles and the determination to restore sacred objects to a country still divided. Today, visitors to the Byzantine Museum in Nicosia encounter mosaics, icons and wall paintings whose physical beauty is inseparable from the extraordinary journeys that brought them home. Many were hacked from church walls after 1974, sold through the international art market and recovered only after decades of investigation, litigation and diplomatic cooperation. Rather than presenting religious art in isolation, the museum turns each recovered object into evidence of cultural loss, survival and the complex legal challenges of protecting heritage during conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.culture.gov.cy/dmculture/DA/DA.nsf/DMLlooting_add1_en/49DAF5E76419EBAC42257C300035C4BA?OpenDocument=&amp;print=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: culture.gov.cy">[culture.gov.cy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">culture.gov.cy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Antiquities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Looting of Cultural heritage in Occupied Cyprus...</span></span></span>

## The Kanakaria mosaics and their removal

The best-known case centres on the Church of Panagia Kanakaria in the village of Lythrangomi. Its sixth-century apse mosaics were among the very few surviving Early Christian mosaics anywhere in the eastern Mediterranean. They depicted Christ, the Virgin Mary, archangels and the apostles in a remarkably well-preserved composition that had survived centuries of religious upheaval, including the Byzantine period of iconoclasm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.culture.gov.cy/dmculture/DA/DA.nsf/All/5C63072411078AB9C22572750055D67D?OpenDocument=&amp;print=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: culture.gov.cy">[culture.gov.cy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">culture.gov.cy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Antiquities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Looting of Cultural heritage in Occupied Cyprus...</span></span></span>

In the years following the division of Cyprus in 1974, looters entered the abandoned church and violently removed the mosaics from the apse. The figures were sawn into transportable sections, causing irreversible damage before being smuggled abroad. The operation has long been associated with the Turkish art dealer Aydin Dikmen, whose name appears repeatedly in investigations into Cypriot ecclesiastical thefts. The Department of Antiquities describes the removal as part of a broader pattern of systematic looting affecting churches, monasteries and archaeological sites in the island's northern part.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.culture.gov.cy/dmculture/DA/DA.nsf/DMLlooting_add1_en/49DAF5E76419EBAC42257C300035C4BA?OpenDocument=&amp;print=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: culture.gov.cy">[culture.gov.cy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">culture.gov.cy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Antiquities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Looting of Cultural heritage in Occupied Cyprus...</span></span></span>

Unlike many stolen antiquities that disappear permanently into private collections, the Kanakaria mosaics became internationally recognisable because scholars had thoroughly documented them before they vanished. Detailed photographs and archaeological records made it unusually difficult for traffickers to disguise their origin, giving investigators powerful evidence once fragments began appearing on the art market.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.culture.gov.cy/dmculture/DA/DA.nsf/All/5C63072411078AB9C22572750055D67D?OpenDocument=&amp;print=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: culture.gov.cy">[culture.gov.cy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">culture.gov.cy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Antiquities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Looting of Cultural heritage in Occupied Cyprus...</span></span></span>

## How icons entered the international art market

The Kanakaria mosaics illustrate how religious art was transformed into commercial commodities. Instead of remaining intact, sacred images were broken into separate fragments that could be sold individually through dealers and intermediaries in several countries. This fragmentation complicated investigations while increasing potential profits for traffickers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.cgu.edu/cultural-property-disputes-resource/cpdr/church-of-panagia-kanakaria-mosaics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.cgu.edu">[CGU Research Centers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.cgu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Centers Four Mosaics from Church of Panagia Kanakaria in Cyprus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CGU Research CentersFour Mosaics from Church of Panagia Kanakaria in Cyprus - Cultural Property Disputes Resource...</span></span></span>

One of the most significant legal battles arose after several mosaic fragments surfaced in the possession of Indiana art dealer Peg Goldberg in the United States during the late 1980s. The Republic of Cyprus and the Church of Cyprus argued successfully that the mosaics had been stolen property rather than legitimate market acquisitions. In 1990, a United States federal court recognised Cyprus's ownership claims, rejecting arguments that a purchaser acting in apparent good faith could obtain valid title to looted cultural property. The mosaics were returned to Cyprus in 1991, and the case became an influential precedent in international cultural property law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.cgu.edu/cultural-property-disputes-resource/cpdr/church-of-panagia-kanakaria-mosaics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.cgu.edu">[CGU Research Centers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.cgu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Centers Four Mosaics from Church of Panagia Kanakaria in Cyprus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CGU Research CentersFour Mosaics from Church of Panagia Kanakaria in Cyprus - Cultural Property Disputes Resource...</span></span></span>

The recovery effort did not end there. Investigations in Germany uncovered hundreds of additional Cypriot ecclesiastical objects linked to the same trafficking network, while further fragments of the Kanakaria mosaics continued to emerge over subsequent decades. One particularly significant return came in 2018, when the missing mosaic of the Apostle Mark was traced in Monaco with the assistance of Dutch investigator Arthur Brand before being repatriated to Cyprus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.culture.gov.cy/dmculture/DA/DA.nsf/DMLlooting_add1_en/49DAF5E76419EBAC42257C300035C4BA?OpenDocument=&amp;print=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: culture.gov.cy">[culture.gov.cy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">culture.gov.cy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Antiquities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Looting of Cultural heritage in Occupied Cyprus...</span></span></span>

These cases demonstrate that illicit antiquities rarely move directly from looters to collectors. Instead, they often pass through multiple dealers, restorers and intermediaries, making careful provenance research essential for museums, auction houses and buyers.

## Repatriation, provenance and unresolved loss

For visitors to the Byzantine Museum, the recovered objects are displayed not only as masterpieces of Byzantine art but also as evidence in an ongoing campaign to reclaim Cyprus's cultural heritage. Labels frequently emphasise where objects were stolen, how they were identified and the legal processes that enabled their return. The displays therefore tell two parallel stories: one of religious devotion and artistic achievement, the other of theft, investigation and recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/lifestyle/cyprus-museum-showcases-rare-religious-treasures-recovered-after-looting-2025-03-20/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Many of these pieces, including mosaics, icons, and wall frescoes, were retrieved after being looted from northern churches following the...</span></span></span>

Cyprus continues to pursue thousands of missing ecclesiastical objects. Official estimates indicate that more than 20,000 icons disappeared from churches after 1974, alongside wall paintings, manuscripts, altar screens and other religious artefacts. Some have been recovered through court proceedings, police investigations and voluntary returns, but many remain missing or are believed to circulate within private collections whose ownership histories remain opaque.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/lifestyle/cyprus-museum-showcases-rare-religious-treasures-recovered-after-looting-2025-03-20/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Many of these pieces, including mosaics, icons, and wall frescoes, were retrieved after being looted from northern churches following the...</span></span></span>

Provenance—the documented ownership history of an object—has therefore become central to Cyprus's recovery strategy. Before museums or collectors acquire Byzantine works, they increasingly examine export documentation, earlier ownership records and photographic evidence to identify objects that may have been removed illegally. The Kanakaria litigation demonstrated that thorough documentation created before looting can become decisive evidence decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.cgu.edu/cultural-property-disputes-resource/cpdr/church-of-panagia-kanakaria-mosaics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.cgu.edu">[CGU Research Centers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.cgu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Centers Four Mosaics from Church of Panagia Kanakaria in Cyprus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CGU Research CentersFour Mosaics from Church of Panagia Kanakaria in Cyprus - Cultural Property Disputes Resource...</span></span></span>

Despite notable successes, the story remains unfinished. Official announcements continue to record newly recovered mosaics, icons and frescoes as investigations uncover objects in different countries. Each return represents not merely the recovery of an artwork but the restoration of part of a damaged religious monument and a reminder that cultural heritage lost during conflict can remain recoverable long after the original crime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.culture.gov.cy/dmculture/DA/DA.nsf/DMLlooting_add1_en/49DAF5E76419EBAC42257C300035C4BA?OpenDocument=&amp;print=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: culture.gov.cy">[culture.gov.cy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">culture.gov.cy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Antiquities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Looting of Cultural heritage in Occupied Cyprus...</span></span></span>

## Why the museum experience is different

Many museums display Byzantine icons primarily for their artistic or devotional value. The Byzantine Museum in Nicosia adds another layer of interpretation by making provenance itself part of the exhibition.

Visitors see physical traces of violence where mosaics were cut apart, learn how fragments travelled through international trafficking networks and discover how legal systems across several countries eventually helped secure their return. This transforms what might otherwise be a conventional display of sacred art into one of Europe's most compelling museum narratives about cultural crime, restitution and the enduring connection between faith, identity and heritage preservation.
