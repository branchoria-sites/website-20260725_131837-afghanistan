## Introduction

[Tuvalu]({{ 'where-tuvalus-lost-stories-still-survive/' | relative_url }})’s most distinctive museum story is not centred on a large national collection of unusual objects, but on the many Tuvaluan artefacts that travelled beyond the islands and are now held in overseas museums, universities and research collections. These objects include dance skirts, clothing, games, canoe models, tools, ornaments, baskets and everyday household items. Together they preserve evidence of island life that might otherwise be difficult to study, showing how Tuvaluans made things, celebrated, worked, played and adapted to changing influences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tuvalu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

The overseas location of these objects is part of their history. Many entered collections during periods of colonial administration, missionary activity, scientific collecting and later cultural exchange. Some were gifts, some were purchased, and others were gathered by researchers or residents. Their presence abroad creates a mixture of opportunity and debate: museums allow global audiences to encounter Tuvaluan heritage, but questions remain about who should control, interpret and care for culturally important objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/x56113" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## Traditional objects in overseas collections

Overseas museums often hold the kinds of objects that reveal ordinary island life rather than spectacular treasures. This makes Tuvalu’s collections unusual: a woven skirt, a game piece or a small canoe model may appear simple, but each object records knowledge about materials, skills, social organisation and identity.

One of the clearest examples is found at the Museum of New Zealand Te Papa Tongarewa, which has a dedicated digital presentation of Tuvalu collections. Its holdings include dance costumes, clothing, games and models that connect objects with living traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tuvalu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span> A Tuvaluan *titi* dance skirt in the collection demonstrates how clothing can combine pandanus materials, plant fibres, imported materials and decorative techniques. The museum notes that such skirts were worn during dance performances and could express personal choices, community belonging or social identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://collections.tepapa.govt.nz/object/83119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collections.tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collections.tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Collections Titi (dance skirt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Collections Titi (dance skirt</span></span></span>

### Clothing, performance and identity

Dance objects are especially valuable because they preserve traditions that are not only visual but social. A skirt stored in a museum does not represent just an item of clothing; it represents movement, performance, community gatherings and the transmission of skills between generations.

Te Papa’s collections include several Tuvaluan skirts made from pandanus and plant fibres, including examples from the twentieth century. These objects show how makers combined traditional materials with newer influences, creating designs that reflected both continuity and change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://collections.tepapa.govt.nz/object/485942" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collections.tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collections.tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Collections Titi (dance skirt) and bodice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Collections Titi (dance skirt) and bodice</span></span></span>

This is important because museum displays can sometimes make Pacific objects appear frozen in the past. In reality, many Tuvaluan artefacts demonstrate adaptation. Materials, designs and uses changed as island communities encountered Christianity, colonial rule, imported goods and new social settings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tuvalu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

### Games, tools and models of island life

Some overseas collections preserve objects connected with recreation and practical knowledge. Te Papa highlights *te ano*, a traditional Tuvaluan game, through an object in its collection, describing it as a national game played by people of different ages and genders. Such objects show that cultural heritage is not limited to ceremonial items; games can reveal social relationships and community life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tuvalu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

The British Museum also holds a range of objects associated with Tuvalu, including canoe models, skirts, fans, ornaments, baskets, boxes and tools. Its catalogue records dozens of objects connected with Tuvalu, including nineteenth- and twentieth-century items acquired through different collecting histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/x56113" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span></span></span> These collections provide researchers with evidence about craftsmanship, materials and changing patterns of daily life.

## What artefacts reveal about island life

The value of Tuvalu artefacts abroad lies in the stories they allow researchers and communities to reconstruct. Many objects are not famous masterpieces; their importance comes from the details they preserve.

A woven item can show which plants were used and how they were prepared. A canoe model can provide clues about maritime traditions. A household object can reveal patterns of storage, exchange or domestic activity. When grouped together, these objects create a broader picture of island societies built around fishing, navigation, craft production and communal relationships.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/x56113" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Museums also provide preservation facilities that may be difficult to maintain for fragile materials. Organic materials such as plant fibres, shells and textiles require careful conservation because they can deteriorate over time. Collections held overseas therefore function both as exhibitions and as research archives, allowing specialists to compare objects across regions and generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://collections.tepapa.govt.nz/object/83119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collections.tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collections.tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Collections Titi (dance skirt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Collections Titi (dance skirt</span></span></span>

At the same time, an overseas museum object is only part of a larger cultural story. Many Pacific traditions are based on knowledge, performance and relationships rather than physical things alone. A dance skirt without the dance, a tool without the skills behind it, or a model canoe without maritime knowledge provides only a partial view. Modern museums increasingly attempt to address this by adding community voices, recordings and cultural interpretation alongside objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tuvalu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

## Questions of collecting and ownership

The movement of Tuvalu artefacts overseas reflects a wider Pacific history of collecting. During the colonial period, museums in Europe and other countries built large ethnographic collections by acquiring objects from territories under European influence. Tuvalu was part of the Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony until the twentieth century, and objects from this period entered overseas collections through administrative networks, collectors and researchers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tuvalu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tuvalu &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

However, not every overseas-held object has the same history. Some were donated by individuals, some were purchased, and some were collected during periods when local communities had limited influence over how their heritage was removed or interpreted. The challenge for museums is to understand each object’s provenance — its history of ownership, collection and movement — rather than treating all artefacts in the same way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/x56113" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

For Tuvalu, these questions are particularly significant because the country has a relatively small physical land area and limited museum infrastructure. Overseas collections can provide valuable international visibility for Tuvaluan heritage, but they also raise questions about access, representation and cultural authority.

Digital catalogues have become one way to reconnect communities with dispersed collections. Online access allows people in Tuvalu and elsewhere to see objects, study records and contribute knowledge about their meanings. The British Museum and Te Papa both provide online collection information that makes many Tuvalu-related objects accessible beyond the museum building.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/search?keyword=Tuvalu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Collections Search &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Collections Search &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## Why these collections matter in Tuvalu’s unusual heritage story


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Tuvalu’s overseas artefact collections are unusual because they turn ordinary objects into historical evidence. They do not rely on mystery or spectacle; their significance comes from the survival of small traces of island life across distance and time.

</div>

A dance skirt, game object, canoe model or woven item may look modest compared with the famous treasures found in major museums, yet these objects preserve knowledge about identity, creativity and resilience. They show how a small island nation’s culture has travelled through colonial histories, museum networks and modern efforts to preserve heritage.

In the wider landscape of Tuvalu’s curious collections, overseas-held artefacts are therefore less a story of lost objects than of cultural connections: objects that left the islands, entered global institutions, and continue to shape conversations about memory, ownership and the meaning of preservation.
