## Introduction

For travellers interested in unusual collections, the central tension is between spectacle and evidence. Nassau’s pirate attraction turns history into an atmospheric walk-through, while the Bahamas Maritime Museum displays material recovered through licensed marine archaeology. Junkanoo museums preserve objects created to move, sound and disappear into a parade. Elsewhere, small institutions keep together the less glamorous but often stranger fragments of national history: bush remedies, colonial newspapers, cave discoveries, slave-trade objects and tales of spirits. The result is a scattered but distinctive landscape in which the sea, slavery, festival culture and natural disaster all shape what survives.

## The most unusual visitor-facing collections

The experience begins in an atmospheric harbour setting and is designed as an immersive self-guided visit rather than an archaeological museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bahamas.com/plan-your-trip/things-to-do/pirates-of-nassau-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bahamas.com">[bahamas.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bahamas.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It will take about 30 minutes to do a selfguided...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Junkanoo costumes as living curiosities

Junkanoo collections are among the most culturally distinctive displays in The Bahamas. The festival is built around music, movement, elaborate handmade costumes and group competition. Museum presentation therefore creates a paradox: objects intended to surge through crowded streets are frozen behind glass or arranged in quiet rooms.

Several separate visitor experiences preserve this material. Educulture Bahamas, founded in 2000, operates a Junkanoo museum and resource centre displaying costume components, fabrics, instruments and parade paraphernalia. Its rooms occupy a repurposed home, giving the collection the character of a community archive rather than a monumental national institution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://educulturebahamas.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: educulturebahamas.com">[educulturebahamas.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">educulturebahamas.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on educulturebahamas.com.</span></span></span>

Junkanoo World Museum and Arts Centre offers a more participatory experience, with historic costumes, handmade instruments, mosaic art and demonstrations intended to communicate the sound and craft of the parade. The Bahamas Museum of Junkanoo, officially opened in 2023, is another purpose-built attempt to translate the festival into a permanent visitor attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://junkanooworld.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: junkanooworld.org">[junkanooworld.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">junkanooworld.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on junkanooworld.org.</span></span></span>

These places are unusual not because the objects are macabre or mysterious, but because they preserve deliberately temporary art. Junkanoo makers work with paper, cardboard, fabric, wire, feathers and other materials that may be altered, damaged or discarded after performance. Displaying the costumes changes them from competitive street creations into evidence of design, labour, neighbourhood identity and historical memory.

Visitors should also be cautious about any single, overly tidy origin story. Junkanoo is commonly connected with celebrations among enslaved Africans during limited Christmas holidays, but the tradition has developed over generations and its name and precise origins remain debated. The museums are most valuable when they present Junkanoo as a living, changing practice rather than a relic with one simple beginning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://educulturebahamas.com/history-of-junkanoo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: educulturebahamas.com">[educulturebahamas.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">educulturebahamas.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on educulturebahamas.com.</span></span></span>

## A prison converted into a library and museum

One of Nassau’s strangest reused buildings is the Nassau Public Library, Reading Room and Museum. The octagonal structure began as the colonial gaol around the turn of the nineteenth century and was converted into a public library in 1873. Its former cells subsequently held books, charts, newspapers, colonial documents, historic prints and Indigenous artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bahamas.com/plan-your-trip/things-to-do/nassau-public-library-reading-room-and-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bahamas.com">[The Islands of The Bahamas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bahamas.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Islands of The Bahamas Nassau Public Library Reading Room and Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Islands of The Bahamas Nassau Public Library Reading Room and Museum</span></span></span>

The appeal is architectural as much as curatorial. Thick walls, confined cell-like rooms and an unusual plan preserve the physical memory of imprisonment even when the contents concern literature or archaeology. Few libraries make their collections available in spaces built to confine the people who once occupied them.

It should not be treated as a conventional prison museum. The institution remains a working library, and public access to historic interiors or tourist photography may be more restricted than older travel descriptions suggest. Official tourism material still describes the museum holdings, but visitors should confirm present access arrangements rather than assuming every former cell is open for sightseeing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bahamas.com/plan-your-trip/things-to-do/nassau-public-library-reading-room-and-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bahamas.com">[The Islands of The Bahamas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bahamas.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Islands of The Bahamas Nassau Public Library Reading Room and Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Islands of The Bahamas Nassau Public Library Reading Room and Museum</span></span></span>

## Folklore, bush medicine and the supernatural

The Bahamas has no well-documented public museum devoted entirely to witchcraft, Obeah or supernatural beings. Relevant material appears in fragments within wider history, art and online collections.

The Bahamas Historical Society Museum in Nassau includes displays on bush medicine, Indigenous Lucayan archaeology, piracy, slavery, Junkanoo and “ecofacts”, meaning natural remains that help explain human history. This breadth gives the institution the feel of a traditional local cabinet: political events sit alongside remedies, craft, archaeology and environmental evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thebahamashistoricalsociety.org/?page_id=243743" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebahamashistoricalsociety.org">[thebahamashistoricalsociety.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebahamashistoricalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Our Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Our Museum</span></span></span>

Bush medicine is especially relevant to curiosity culture because it sits between practical plant knowledge, oral history, spirituality and modern pharmacological investigation. Grand Bahama Museum’s digital material warns that popular descriptions often omit scientific plant names, preparation methods, dosage and cultural context. That caution matters: a plant listed as a historical remedy is not automatically proven safe or effective, and a museum reference should not be read as medical advice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grandbahamamuseum.org/exhibits/culture/bush-medicine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grandbahamamuseum.org">[grandbahamamuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grandbahamamuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bush Medicine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bush Medicine</span></span></span>

The National Art Gallery of The Bahamas has also addressed public health through exhibitions connecting bush medicine, hospitals, spiritual protection and contemporary ideas of wellbeing. Such displays treat healing traditions as part of social memory rather than reducing them to either superstition or unquestioned cure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nagb.org.bs/exhibitions/medicine-memory-public-health-in-the-bahamas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nagb.org.bs">[National Art Gallery of The Bahamas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nagb.org.bs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nagb.org.bs.</span></span></span>

Obeah, a broad and often contested label applied to African-derived spiritual and healing practices in parts of the Caribbean, appears in Bahamian folklore and historical discussion. Grand Bahama Museum’s online religion material describes Obeah influences in island stories while placing them within the history of enslaved West Africans and Christianity. The presentation is educational rather than a collection of ritual objects, and it should not be confused with a practising religious site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grandbahamamuseum.org/exhibits/culture/religion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grandbahamamuseum.org">[grandbahamamuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grandbahamamuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on grandbahamamuseum.org.</span></span></span>

The same digital museum preserves Bahamian folktales involving devils, spirits and supernatural encounters. These stories are not evidence that uncanny events occurred. They are evidence that Bahamian communities used humour, fear, religion and storytelling to explain misfortune, enforce behaviour and entertain listeners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grandbahamamuseum.org/exhibits/culture/folktale-collection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grandbahamamuseum.org">[grandbahamamuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grandbahamamuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folktales from the Bahamas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folktales from the Bahamas</span></span></span>

## Sacred Space at Clifton

Clifton Heritage National Park on New Providence is not a museum in the normal sense. It is a 208-acre cultural landscape containing evidence of Lucayan occupation, a Loyalist plantation, an African settlement and later historical activity. Its most uncanny installation is Sacred Space, also known as Genesis Garden, created by Bahamian artists Antonius Roberts and Tyrone Ferguson.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cliftonheritageauthority.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cliftonheritageauthority.com">[cliftonheritageauthority.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cliftonheritageauthority.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cliftonheritageauthority.com.</span></span></span>

Beginning in 2005, figures were carved from hurricane-damaged casuarina trees on the former plantation landscape. Their elongated bodies face the sea like witnesses, ancestors or a silent congregation. National Art Gallery interpretation connects Roberts’s work with overlooked Black and Indigenous spatial practices, environmental memory and the creation of decolonial spaces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nagb.org.bs/exhibitions/antonius-roberts-art-ecology-and-sacred-space/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nagb.org.bs">[National Art Gallery of The Bahamas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nagb.org.bs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nagb.org.bs.</span></span></span>

The installation is sometimes described in spiritual language, but it is contemporary memorial art rather than an archaeological group of sacred idols. Its power comes from location. The sculptures stand within a landscape marked by Indigenous presence, enslavement, plantation life, ocean crossings and environmental damage. That makes Clifton one of the country’s most significant places for visitors interested in how objects, landscape and historical trauma can form an open-air collection.

## The lost blue-hole fossil museum

One of the most remarkable Bahamian collections is no longer publicly accessible. Sawmill Sink, a flooded limestone cave on Great Abaco, preserved fossils in unusual underwater conditions. Beneath freshwater and a corrosive layer containing hydrogen sulphide, oxygen-poor saltwater protected bones from extinct tortoises, crocodiles, birds, snakes, bats and other animals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.businessinsider.com/blue-hole-bahamas-ice-age-fossils" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: businessinsider.com">[Business Insider]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">businessinsider.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on businessinsider.com.</span></span></span>

Diver Brian Kakuk’s discoveries from 2005 onwards helped researchers reconstruct environmental change in The Bahamas from the late Ice Age through the arrival of humans. Among the finds was an extinct tortoise later named *Chelonoidis alburyorum*. A national museum branch opened on Abaco in January 2018 with displays on blue holes, wildlife and Lucayan history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.businessinsider.com/blue-hole-bahamas-ice-age-fossils" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: businessinsider.com">[Business Insider]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">businessinsider.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on businessinsider.com.</span></span></span>

Hurricane Dorian destroyed the museum in September 2019. Much of the collection was salvaged, but the building, laboratory and records suffered severe damage, and the curator later said she had no plans to reopen it. Some material was moved into climate-controlled storage at the University of Florida.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.businessinsider.com/blue-hole-bahamas-ice-age-fossils" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: businessinsider.com">[Business Insider]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">businessinsider.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on businessinsider.com.</span></span></span>

This lost museum matters because it reveals a practical weakness in archipelagic collecting. A nationally important fossil archive may depend on a small institution, individual specialists, vulnerable power supplies and buildings exposed to hurricanes. The strange collection here is therefore both natural and institutional: ancient animals preserved in a toxic submerged cave, then rescued into a museum that survived for less than two years.

## Slavery, resistance and difficult objects

The Pompey Museum of Slavery and Emancipation occupies Vendue House in Nassau, a building historically associated with the sale of goods and enslaved people. The museum interprets slavery, resistance, emancipation and their consequences in The Bahamas. Its name commemorates Pompey, an enslaved man associated with resistance on Exuma in 1830.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bahamas.com/experiences/downtown-nassau" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bahamas.com">[The Islands of The Bahamas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bahamas.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bahamas.com.</span></span></span>

This is not an “oddity museum”, and treating shackles or slave-trade material as macabre curios would be inappropriate. Its relevance lies in the disturbing relationship between collection and site: objects and testimony about enslavement are displayed inside a building connected to the trade itself. The physical setting prevents the history from becoming abstract.

Recent visitor reports indicate that the museum was open in early 2026 after earlier periods of closure and restoration. They also suggest a small but substantial display using artefacts, text and digital interpretation. Because the building has suffered fires and closures in the past, current opening arrangements should be checked before a visit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.co.uk/Attraction_Review-g147416-d6415699-Reviews-Pompey_Museum_of_Slavery_Emancipation-Nassau_New_Providence_Island_Bahamas.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.co.uk">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tripadvisor.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## How credible are the collections?

The Bahamas’ unusual attractions fall into several distinct categories, and visitors will get more from them by recognising the difference.

**Archaeological and research-led collections** include the Bahamas Maritime Museum’s documented wreck material and the surviving Sawmill Sink fossils. Their strongest claims rest on excavation records, conservation, scientific analysis and legal oversight, although private funding and salvage rights deserve scrutiny.

**Community and living-culture museums** include Educulture and other Junkanoo centres. Their authority comes substantially from makers, performers and long-term participants. They may be less formal than state museums, but they preserve practical knowledge that conventional object catalogues can miss.

**Commercial interpretation** is represented by Pirates of Nassau. It uses reliable broad history but communicates through reconstruction and entertainment. Wax figures and recreated rooms are illustrations, not authenticated pirate remains.

**Historic-site museums and memorial landscapes** include Pompey, the former gaol library and Clifton. Here, the building or ground is often more important than any single object.

**Digital collections** such as Grand Bahama Museum widen access to folklore, religion, natural history, stamps and maritime history but do not necessarily correspond to a physical gallery that travellers can enter. The institution explicitly describes its mission as digitally preserving Grand Bahamian heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grandbahamamuseum.org/exhibits" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grandbahamamuseum.org">[grandbahamamuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grandbahamamuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on grandbahamamuseum.org.</span></span></span>

## What defines Bahamian curiosity culture?

The most memorable strange collections in The Bahamas are shaped by four forces.

First is the sea. Shipwrecks, piracy, trade and blue holes have preserved objects in places that are dangerous, inaccessible or vulnerable to looting. Second is performance: Junkanoo museums attempt to conserve costume and sound without stripping them of movement and communal meaning. Third is colonial history, visible in an auction house turned slavery museum, a gaol turned library and plantation land turned memorial space. Fourth is fragility. Salt, humidity, hurricanes, development and limited institutional resources can erase a collection almost as quickly as it is assembled.

There may be no single Bahamian museum of monsters or magic, but the country has something more rooted in its own history: treasure recovered from reefs, extinct animals lifted through poisonous cave water, festival giants preserved after the music stops, folk remedies balanced between memory and medicine, and human figures carved from storm-damaged trees to face the Atlantic. Together, these places show that The Bahamas’ strangest collections are not detached cabinets of exotic objects. They are attempts to hold on to material that the sea, colonial power, tourism and natural disaster have repeatedly scattered.
