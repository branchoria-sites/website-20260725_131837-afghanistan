## Introduction

The royal palace at Bafut in north-west [Cameroon]({{ 'where-cameroons-strangest-collections/' | relative_url }}) is one of the country's most unusual heritage sites because it combines a living royal residence with a museum of warfare, kingship and colonial resistance. Unlike collections assembled purely for display, many of its objects remain closely tied to the authority of the Bafut monarchy. Among the most discussed exhibits are reported war trophies associated with the conflicts between the Kingdom of Bafut and German colonial forces at the turn of the twentieth century, including accounts of human skulls attributed to German soldiers killed during the fighting. These objects, together with the palace's recent experience of armed conflict during Cameroon's Anglophone crisis, make Bafut an important case study in how museums preserve difficult histories while confronting modern threats to heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bafut-Kriege" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## When war trophies become museum evidence

The Bafut palace museum is unusual because it is not separated from the institution that created many of the objects it preserves. The palace remains the residence of the Fon (traditional ruler), while sections of the complex present royal regalia, weapons, ceremonial objects and historical material connected with the kingdom's past.

For visitors interested in unusual collections, the museum's fascination lies less in displays designed to shock than in its survival as a functioning royal archive. The collection documents how a Grassfields kingdom resisted German colonial expansion during the Bafut Wars of approximately 1899–1907. Weapons, military objects and stories of resistance are presented alongside the continuing traditions of the palace, giving the museum the character of both a historical exhibition and a living institution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bafut-Kriege" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## The Bafut Wars and the reported skull display

The wars between Bafut and German colonial forces formed part of Germany's campaign to establish military control over the Cameroon protectorate. German expeditions repeatedly attacked Bafut after resistance led by Fon Abumbi I and allied communities. Although German forces eventually imposed colonial authority, the conflicts became central to Bafut historical memory.

One of the museum's most widely repeated claims is that it preserves the skulls of four German soldiers killed during the fighting, reportedly displayed together with weapons and ammunition connected to the Battle of Mankon. This account appears in descriptions of the palace museum and has been repeated in historical summaries of the Bafut Wars. However, detailed modern scholarly documentation of the objects themselves is limited, and visitors should recognise that published descriptions rely heavily on local historical tradition and older accounts rather than recent museum catalogues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bafut-Kriege" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Whether every detail can be independently verified or not, the reported display illustrates an important distinction. In many European museums, colonial campaigns are often represented through documents, uniforms or maps. At Bafut, the narrative is instead told from the perspective of a kingdom that experienced invasion. The reported trophies therefore function less as military curiosities than as symbols of survival and political memory.

## The ethics of displaying human remains

If the reported skulls remain on display, they place Bafut within a wider international debate about exhibiting human remains.

Modern museum ethics increasingly argue that human remains should be displayed only where there is a clear educational purpose, careful interpretation and respectful treatment. Museums are encouraged to consider the beliefs of descendant communities, explain the historical context and avoid presenting remains simply as spectacles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/human-remains" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Bafut adds further complexity because the remains, if correctly identified, would represent enemies killed during colonial warfare rather than ancestors of the local community. They therefore occupy several overlapping roles:

## A heritage site endangered by modern conflict

Bafut's importance is not confined to colonial history. During Cameroon's ongoing Anglophone crisis, the palace itself became part of a different conflict.

Rapid assessments supported by UNESCO in 2020 found significant damage across the Bafut Royal Palace complex. Buildings showed structural cracks and water damage, sacred areas had been desecrated, museum buildings had suffered bullet impacts, collections experienced destruction from both conflict and environmental deterioration, looting had occurred, and parts of the museum library and documentary archive had been lost. These findings highlighted that the danger extended beyond architecture to the historical collections themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/crises-are-affecting-cultural-heritage-cameroon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crises are affecting cultural heritage in Cameroon &#124; UNESCO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crises are affecting cultural heritage in Cameroon &#124; UNESCO...</span></span></span>

The damage illustrates a recurring problem for living heritage sites. Unlike museums in secure urban centres, palace museums in conflict zones continue to serve local communities while also safeguarding irreplaceable collections. The same buildings may function simultaneously as royal residences, ceremonial spaces, museums and symbols of cultural identity, making them especially vulnerable during political violence.

UNESCO's emergency assessments aimed to identify priority conservation measures before further losses became irreversible, recognising that both tangible collections and intangible traditions were under threat as communities were displaced and ceremonial life disrupted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/crises-are-affecting-cultural-heritage-cameroon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crises are affecting cultural heritage in Cameroon &#124; UNESCO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crises are affecting cultural heritage in Cameroon &#124; UNESCO...</span></span></span>

## Why Bafut matters in Cameroon's curiosity landscape

Among Cameroon's unusual heritage collections, Bafut occupies a distinctive position. It is neither a conventional military museum nor a cabinet of curiosities assembled for entertainment. Instead, it preserves objects whose meanings continue to evolve.

The reported war trophies are significant not because they are macabre, but because they embody unresolved questions about colonial violence, historical memory and museum ethics. At the same time, the recent damage suffered during the Anglophone conflict reminds visitors that heritage is not only about preserving the distant past. Collections that survived colonial conquest can still be endangered by contemporary conflict.

For anyone exploring Cameroon's palace museums, Bafut demonstrates that unusual collections often carry responsibilities as well as fascination. Its museum records resistance, raises difficult ethical questions about the display of human remains, and stands as a reminder that cultural heritage itself can become a casualty of war.
