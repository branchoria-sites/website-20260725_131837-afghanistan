## Introduction

[Namibia]({{ 'where-namibia-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s most thought-provoking museum experiences are often not the strangest objects themselves, but the stories attached to them. Colonial medical equipment, mining artefacts, ethnographic collections and displays of Indigenous knowledge all raise the same question: who decided what these objects mean? Visitors quickly discover that many collections were assembled during German colonial rule or South African administration, when museums often reflected European ideas about science, civilisation and race rather than the perspectives of the communities whose lives they documented. Today, the most interesting visit is often one that asks what is missing from the display as much as what is inside the cabinet.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Namibia_bfe79d_contested_museum_int_3c9150-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Who Tells It illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Namibia_bfe79d_contested_museum_int_3c9150-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Namibia_bfe79d_contested_museum_int_3c9150-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Rather than treating unusual collections as neutral curiosities, it helps to read them as evidence of changing ideas about authority, memory and healing. Namibia's museums increasingly sit at the intersection of historical preservation, post-colonial debate and community-led reinterpretation, making them important places for understanding both the country's unusual heritage and its ongoing conversations about justice and identity.

## Medical instruments and living plant knowledge

Colonial-era medical collections can appear reassuringly scientific. Glass syringes, surgical tools, pharmacy bottles and hospital equipment suggest progress, disease control and modern healthcare. Yet these collections rarely explain that colonial medicine existed alongside rich Indigenous systems of healing that were frequently dismissed, regulated or excluded from official institutions.

This absence matters because traditional knowledge in Namibia has always involved more than herbal remedies alone. Plant use is closely connected with environmental knowledge, spiritual practice, seasonal observation and community experience. Ethnobotanical research has documented extensive Indigenous expertise in medicinal plants across Namibia, but museums have historically treated this knowledge as ethnographic curiosity rather than as an evolving body of practical expertise. Modern scholarship increasingly recognises these traditions as living knowledge rather than relics of the past.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tsumebmuseum.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tsumebmuseum.com">[tsumebmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tsumebmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tsumeb Museum...</span></span></span>

For visitors, this changes how displays should be read.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* A cabinet of colonial medical equipment usually reflects the history of state medicine rather than the complete history of healthcare.
* Displays of medicinal plants may represent only selected examples, not the wider cultural systems that give those plants meaning.
* The absence of contemporary Indigenous voices can itself be an important part of the story.

</div>

Instead of asking which treatment was "correct", a more useful question is how museums balance laboratory science, colonial documentation and community knowledge without reducing any of them to stereotypes.

## Mining wealth, labour and colonial memory

Mining museums present another example of contested interpretation. Namibia's extraordinary mineral wealth produced remarkable collections of crystals, ores and mining technology that genuinely fascinate visitors. The minerals from Tsumeb, in particular, are internationally famous for their diversity and beauty, and specialist museums celebrate both their geological significance and the engineering achievements involved in extracting them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museums.com.na/museums/central/tsumeb-mineralogic-mining-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.com.na">[museums.com.na]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.com.na</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tsumeb Mineralogic &amp; Mining Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museums Association of Namibia...</span></span></span>

Yet mining collections can be interpreted in two very different ways.

One approach celebrates discovery, industrial innovation and economic development. Historic machinery, railway construction, smelters and mineral specimens become symbols of technical achievement.

A second interpretation asks who performed the labour, who controlled the mines and who benefited from the wealth. Colonial mining depended on systems of land appropriation, racial segregation and tightly controlled labour that shaped both towns and communities. Without this context, gleaming mineral displays risk presenting extraction as a purely scientific or commercial success rather than part of a wider colonial economy.

The Tsumeb Museum illustrates this tension particularly well. Its collections combine mining history with military artefacts recovered from Lake Otjikoto, including artillery hidden by retreating German colonial forces during the First World War. The recovered weapons are undeniably unusual exhibits, but they also remind visitors that mining towns were deeply connected with colonial expansion, transport infrastructure and military power rather than existing as isolated industrial communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museums.com.na/museums/central/tsumeb-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.com.na">[museums.com.na]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.com.na</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tsumeb Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museums Association of Namibia...</span></span></span>

The question is therefore not whether these objects belong in museums—they clearly do—but whether exhibitions explain the broader systems that produced them.

## Reading inherited labels and missing voices

Many Namibian museums developed during periods when exhibition labels reflected colonial scholarship. Ethnographic collections often classified communities according to fixed categories, describing cultures as static subjects for observation rather than as living societies capable of change.

This does not necessarily mean the collections themselves lack value. Clothing, tools, domestic objects and ceremonial items remain important historical evidence. The challenge lies in interpretation.

Older displays may:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* describe Indigenous communities almost entirely in the past tense;
* prioritise European collectors over the original makers;
* explain objects without recording how descendant communities understand them today;
* separate "nature", "science" and "culture" in ways that do not match Indigenous worldviews.

</div>

The Swakopmund Museum, with its extensive collections covering natural history, archaeology and ethnography, demonstrates both the strengths and limitations of this traditional encyclopaedic museum model. Visitors encounter an impressive range of material, but inherited classifications and older interpretive styles require careful reading, particularly where colonial-era assumptions remain visible in exhibition design or labelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swakopmundnamibia.com/swakopmund-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swakopmundnamibia.com">[Swakopmund Namibia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swakopmundnamibia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Swakopmund Namibia Swakopmund Museum &#124; Swakopmund Namibia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Swakopmund NamibiaSwakopmund Museum &#124; Swakopmund NamibiaFebruary 3, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 3, 2026</span></span></span>

Increasingly, museums worldwide are moving towards shared interpretation, inviting descendant communities to contribute new labels, oral histories and alternative explanations rather than replacing one authoritative narrative with another.

## Colonial relics as evidence rather than celebration

Colonial objects themselves are not inherently celebratory. Uniforms, military equipment, administrative documents and domestic artefacts can either reinforce nostalgic stories about empire or help explain how colonial systems functioned.

The difference lies in interpretation.

For example, artillery recovered from Lake Otjikoto can be displayed simply as rare military hardware. Alternatively, it can be presented within the wider history of German colonial rule, regional conflict and the collapse of colonial authority during the First World War. The object remains unchanged, but its meaning becomes far richer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museums.com.na/museums/central/tsumeb-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.com.na">[museums.com.na]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.com.na</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tsumeb Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museums Association of Namibia...</span></span></span>

Likewise, railway equipment or mining machinery may represent technological achievement while simultaneously illustrating systems of labour control, resource extraction and unequal economic development.

Good interpretation allows visitors to hold both ideas at once.

## Why the debate extends beyond museum walls

Questions about interpretation connect directly with wider debates over colonial memory in Namibia. Discussions surrounding the Herero and Nama genocide, the return of human remains from overseas institutions, memorial landscapes and historical recognition have encouraged museums to reconsider whose perspectives are represented in permanent displays. These debates are not confined to academic circles; they increasingly influence exhibitions, public history projects and heritage tourism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/artanddesign/2026/jun/05/namibia-shark-island-herero-nama-genocide-fractured-lifeworlds-spore-initiative-berlin-forensic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The exhibition “Fractured Lifeworlds,” now in Berlin after premiering in Namibia, investigates this atrocity through forensic and artisti...</span></span></span>

For unusual collections, this has practical consequences.

An object once presented simply as an exotic curiosity may now be interpreted through the experiences of the people who created, used or lost it. Human remains, sacred objects and ethnographic collections are increasingly viewed through ethical questions of consent, ownership and cultural authority rather than only scientific value.

This shift reflects a broader understanding that museums do not merely preserve history—they actively shape public memory.

## What visitors should look for

The most rewarding way to explore Namibia's stranger collections is to pay attention not only to unusual artefacts but also to the decisions behind their presentation.

Useful questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Who collected these objects, and under what historical circumstances?
* Does the exhibition distinguish between colonial records and Indigenous knowledge?
* Are community voices included alongside older museum labels?
* Does the display explain labour, power and historical context as well as technology or science?
* What stories seem absent, and why might they be missing?

</div>

Seen in this way, Namibia's colonial relics, medical collections and mining displays become more than cabinets of curiosities. They reveal how museums themselves change over time, moving from institutions that once claimed a single authoritative voice towards places where interpretation, memory and historical responsibility remain open to discussion.
