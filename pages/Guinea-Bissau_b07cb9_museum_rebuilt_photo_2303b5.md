## Introduction

[Guinea-Bissau]({{ 'where-guinea-bissau-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s National Ethnographic Museum is unusual not because it houses spectacular curiosities, but because much of its modern identity was rebuilt from fragments of its own lost archive. After the 1998–99 civil war devastated the museum, destroying or dispersing collections and thousands of photographs, researchers, museum staff and curators reconstructed its history using surviving contact sheets, damaged objects and the memories of the people who had created the institution. The result is a museum that documents not only the country's cultural diversity but also the destruction and recovery of cultural memory itself. Rather than treating photographs as simple illustrations, the reconstruction project turned them into primary evidence, allowing lost exhibitions, collecting expeditions and forgotten objects to be identified decades after the original museum had effectively disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/mas/article/view/3825" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.le.ac.uk">[le.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.le.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Leicester Open JournalsThe Lives and Deaths of an Ethnographic Museum: History, Violence and Curatorial Collaborations in G...</span></span></span>

## How Photographs Helped Rebuild Bissau's Lost Museum

The National Ethnographic Museum opened in 1988 as Guinea-Bissau's principal museum devoted to the country's many ethnic communities. It collected textiles, tools, ceremonial objects, domestic artefacts and masks, but it also displayed photographs showing these objects being used in everyday life, festivals and religious ceremonies. This emphasis on context distinguished it from museums that present objects as isolated works of art. Instead, visitors could see how material culture functioned within living communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ox.ac.uk/research/research-impact/blow-bissau" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ox.ac.uk">[Oxford University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oxford University Blow-up in Bissau &#124; University of Oxford</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oxford University Blow-up in Bissau &#124; University of Oxford</span></span></span>

Everything changed during the civil war of 1998–99. The museum building was occupied by Senegalese troops supporting the government, while fighting in Bissau caused extensive damage. Much of the collection was destroyed, dispersed or left in poor condition, and the museum's photographic archive—thousands of negatives and prints documenting fieldwork carried out since the mid-1980s—was largely lost. By the early 2000s the institution had effectively ceased to exist as a functioning national museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ox.ac.uk/research/research-impact/blow-bissau" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ox.ac.uk">[ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oxford University Blow-up in Bissau &#124; University of Oxford</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oxford University Blow-up in Bissau &#124; University of Oxford</span></span></span>

What survived was unexpectedly modest: several hundred contact sheets, a small number of damaged artefacts and the recollections of former museum staff. Those fragments became the foundation for rebuilding both the museum's collections and its institutional history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anthro.web.ox.ac.uk/blow-up-in-bissau-images-and-memories-of-a-national-ethnographic-museum-in-bissau" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anthro.web.ox.ac.uk">[Anthropology &amp; Museum Ethnography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anthro.web.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pology &amp; Museum EthnographyBlow-up in Bissau: Images and Memories of a National Ethnographic Museum in Bissau &#124; School of Anthropol...</span></span></span>

## The Museum Before the Civil War

The museum emerged during Guinea-Bissau's first decades of independence, when cultural institutions were expected to strengthen national identity while recognising the country's remarkable linguistic and cultural diversity. Museum staff travelled across the country documenting everyday life, religious practices, craftsmanship and local traditions.

Importantly, the museum's collecting policy was often more restrained than outsiders might expect. Researchers involved in documenting its history note that fieldworkers frequently chose not to remove certain sacred ritual objects from communities. Instead, they photographed ceremonies, recorded information and collected only items whose transfer had been negotiated. This means the photographic archive preserved aspects of cultural life that were never fully represented by the physical collection alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/mas/article/view/3825" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.le.ac.uk">[University of Leicester Open Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.le.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Leicester Open JournalsThe Lives and Deaths of an Ethnographic Museum: History, Violence and Curatorial Collaborations in G...</span></span></span>

Because photographs accompanied many displays, the archive recorded not just individual artefacts but also exhibition design, field expeditions, museum staff, donors and community collaborations. After the war, those images became essential evidence for reconstructing all of these relationships.

## What Survived in the Contact Sheets

The most remarkable survivors were approximately 400–450 contact-print sheets. Contact sheets are proof pages made directly from photographic negatives, allowing photographers to review every frame before producing larger prints. Normally they are temporary working material. In Guinea-Bissau they became the principal surviving visual archive after the negatives themselves were destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anthro.web.ox.ac.uk/blow-up-in-bissau-images-and-memories-of-a-national-ethnographic-museum-in-bissau" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anthro.web.ox.ac.uk">[ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anthro.web.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pology &amp; Museum EthnographyBlow-up in Bissau: Images and Memories of a National Ethnographic Museum in Bissau &#124; School of Anthropol...</span></span></span>

Beginning in 2012, museum director Albano Mendes collaborated with anthropologist Ramon Sarró and curator Ana Temudo to digitise, enlarge and document these fragile sheets. Enlarging the tiny images made it possible to identify:

* former exhibitions inside the museum;
* fieldwork carried out during the 1980s;
* museum staff and community collaborators;
* places where collecting expeditions had taken place;

## Reopening, Access and Incomplete Recovery

The reconstruction project became publicly visible through the exhibition *The National Ethnographic Museum of Bissau: 30 Years of History*, opened in September 2017. Instead of pretending the losses had never happened, the exhibition displayed enlarged versions of the contact-sheet images alongside surviving artefacts, telling visitors both what had once existed and what had been lost. According to the researchers involved, the exhibition attracted official attention and helped secure renewed political support for reopening the museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anthro.web.ox.ac.uk/blow-up-in-bissau-images-and-memories-of-a-national-ethnographic-museum-in-bissau" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anthro.web.ox.ac.uk">[ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anthro.web.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pology &amp; Museum EthnographyBlow-up in Bissau: Images and Memories of a National Ethnographic Museum in Bissau &#124; School of Anthropol...</span></span></span>

The museum therefore reopened not as a perfect reconstruction but as a visibly incomplete institution. Many original artefacts remain missing, and the photographic archive itself survives only in fragmentary form. That incompleteness is now part of the museum's identity rather than something hidden from visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/mas/article/view/3825" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.le.ac.uk">[University of Leicester Open Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.le.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Leicester Open JournalsThe Lives and Deaths of an Ethnographic Museum: History, Violence and Curatorial Collaborations in G...</span></span></span>

Practical information for visitors remains less predictable than at larger national museums elsewhere. Opening arrangements and staffing have varied over time, reflecting Guinea-Bissau's limited cultural funding and the continuing challenges of maintaining museum infrastructure. Travellers should therefore expect a small institution whose greatest significance lies in its history rather than in the scale of its displays.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ox.ac.uk/research/research-impact/blow-bissau" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ox.ac.uk">[Oxford University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oxford University Blow-up in Bissau &#124; University of Oxford</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oxford University Blow-up in Bissau &#124; University of Oxford</span></span></span>

## Why This Museum Matters in Guinea-Bissau's Curiosity Landscape

Within Guinea-Bissau's unusual heritage sites, the National Ethnographic Museum stands apart because its most extraordinary collection is, in effect, a collection of evidence about its own destruction. The damaged contact sheets preserve vanished exhibitions, lost objects and forgotten museum practices while demonstrating how photographs can become historical artefacts in their own right.

That gives the museum a dual role. It remains an ethnographic museum documenting the country's diverse cultures, but it is also a case study in cultural resilience. Visitors encounter not only masks, textiles and everyday objects but also the story of how a nation's museum was painstakingly rebuilt from proof sheets that were never intended to survive.
