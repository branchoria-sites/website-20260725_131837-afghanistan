## Introduction

[Finland]({{ 'where-finland-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s Sámi sacred collections are unusual not because they contain mysterious objects, but because they raise difficult questions about who should care for them, interpret them and decide their future. Ceremonial drums, ritual objects and other culturally significant items are not simply historical artefacts. For many Sámi people they remain part of a living Indigenous tradition whose cultural and spiritual meanings did not end when the objects entered museum stores.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Finland_c909b1_sami_sacred_collecti_9b7da9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Sami Heritage illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Finland_c909b1_sami_sacred_collecti_9b7da9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Finland_c909b1_sami_sacred_collecti_9b7da9-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Modern museum practice in Finland has therefore shifted away from treating these collections as ethnographic curiosities. Instead, the central issue is museum responsibility: recognising how collections were assembled, acknowledging unequal power between collectors and Indigenous communities, and ensuring that Sámi people play the leading role in decisions about their own cultural heritage. This approach has made Finland an important case study in Indigenous stewardship rather than simply another country displaying unusual sacred objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

## What Sámi drums and sacred objects represented

Among the best-known sacred objects are Sámi ceremonial drums. Historically these were used by ritual specialists during ceremonies involving healing, divination and communication with the spiritual world. Their painted surfaces represented relationships between people, animals, landscapes and spiritual beings rather than functioning as decorative art.

The drums were only one part of a wider sacred landscape. Clothing, ritual tools, offerings, carved objects and places of worship all formed part of interconnected cultural practices tied to seasonal movement, reindeer herding, fishing and local environments. Removing a single object from this setting often stripped away much of its original meaning.

For that reason, contemporary museums increasingly avoid presenting drums as exotic "shamanic curiosities". Instead, they explain that these objects belonged to complex systems of knowledge and belief that survived despite centuries of religious pressure and cultural change. The object alone is only part of the story; equally important are the memories, language and community knowledge associated with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

## How confiscation and unequal collecting shaped museum collections

Many surviving sacred objects entered museum collections during periods when Sámi communities had little control over the process.

From the seventeenth century onwards, missionaries, state officials, travellers and researchers collected Sámi material across northern Scandinavia. During Christianisation, ceremonial drums were frequently confiscated because church authorities regarded them as symbols of non-Christian religious practice. Other objects entered museums through scientific expeditions, private collecting or unequal exchanges that reflected the political power of states rather than the wishes of Indigenous communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/releases/sami-museum-objects-returning-home-repatriation-work-with-sami-communities-awarded-a-grant-of-e850000/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

This history matters because it changes how museums understand ownership. A collection catalogue may record when an object was acquired, yet reveal little about whether the original community freely consented to its removal or whether the object had continuing ceremonial significance.

Museums today therefore distinguish between legal possession and ethical responsibility. Even where acquisition complied with the standards of its time, many institutions acknowledge that those standards often ignored Indigenous rights, cultural authority and spiritual values.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/releases/sami-museum-objects-returning-home-repatriation-work-with-sami-communities-awarded-a-grant-of-e850000/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

## Why stewardship matters more than display

For visitors interested in unusual collections, it can be tempting to think of sacred artefacts primarily as exhibits. Finnish museum practice increasingly argues the opposite.

Some sacred items are displayed only selectively, while others remain in collections where access is carefully managed. The decision is not necessarily about secrecy but about respect. Certain objects carry meanings that cannot be fully communicated through a display label or behind glass in a gallery.

Museum responsibility therefore extends beyond conservation. It includes questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Who decides whether an object should be exhibited?
* Which language should describe it?
* Should photographs be taken or published?
* Which traditional knowledge accompanies the object?
* Can community members determine restrictions on access?

</div>

These questions have become central to Indigenous museology internationally, and Finland has increasingly adopted this community-led approach in relation to Sámi heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

## Repatriation and Siida's community role

The clearest example of this change is the work of the Sámi Museum Siida in Inari.

Rather than functioning simply as a regional museum, Siida serves as Finland's national specialist museum for Sámi culture. Its collections are intended to support Sámi identity, language and cultural continuity, not merely preserve historical artefacts for outside audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

A landmark moment came in 2021 when the National Museum of Finland transferred more than 2,200 Sámi objects to Siida following years of cooperation. Although the collections remained within Finland, the move represented a significant shift in authority by placing the material under the stewardship of the Indigenous institution best placed to interpret and care for it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/releases/homecoming-jubilee-celebrates-the-repatriation-of-museum-artefacts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Homecoming Jubilee celebrates the repatriation of museum artefacts – Siida</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 20, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 20, 2021</span></span></span>

Siida's work has not stopped with this transfer. The museum is also engaged in international efforts to encourage the return of Sámi collections held elsewhere in Europe. Estimates suggest that around 50,000 Sámi objects remain in museums and research institutions outside Sámi control, reflecting centuries of collecting across national borders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/releases/sami-museum-objects-returning-home-repatriation-work-with-sami-communities-awarded-a-grant-of-e850000/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

Recent projects have expanded the idea of repatriation beyond simply transporting objects home. Siida's *Ellos min árbi* ("Long Live Our Cultural Heritage") programme works with Sámi communities to reconnect returned artefacts with family histories, traditional knowledge and contemporary cultural practice. Workshops collect memories, identify previous owners where possible and restore cultural meaning alongside physical custody. The museum describes this as both material and cultural restoration rather than merely moving museum property between institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/ellos-min-arbi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ellos min árbi – Siida</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ellos min árbi – Siida</span></span></span>

## Why this changes how visitors should see these collections

For travellers exploring Finland's unusual heritage, Sámi sacred collections require a different mindset from curiosity cabinets or folklore museums.

The objects themselves can certainly appear visually striking, particularly ceremonial drums with their painted symbols. Yet their significance lies less in their appearance than in the responsibilities surrounding them. The most important story is not the survival of an ancient ritual object but the continuing efforts of a living Indigenous community to determine how that object should be interpreted, preserved and, where appropriate, returned.

This makes Finland's Sámi collections distinctive within the country's wider landscape of unusual museums. They demonstrate that museum ethics can be as historically significant as the artefacts themselves. Rather than celebrating rare objects for their strangeness, modern interpretation increasingly asks who has the right to tell their stories, who benefits from their preservation and how museums can move from being collectors of Indigenous culture to partners in its continuation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/releases/sami-museum-objects-returning-home-repatriation-work-with-sami-communities-awarded-a-grant-of-e850000/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
