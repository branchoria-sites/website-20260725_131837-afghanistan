## Introduction

[Sri Lanka]({{ 'where-sri-lanka-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})’s demon masks are unusual because they do not simply represent monsters or characters from folklore: they preserve an older way of explaining illness, where sickness could be understood through relationships between people, spirits, performance and community ritual. In Ambalangoda, the southern coastal centre best known for mask carving, visitors encounter masks linked to healing ceremonies in which named disease-causing demons were represented, confronted and symbolically controlled. The tradition is especially associated with the Sanni Yakuma healing ritual and the group of masks known as Daha Ata Sanniya, or the “eighteen diseases”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1761180/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCSri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 23, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 2006</span></span></span>

Today, Ambalangoda’s mask museums and workshops present these objects as cultural heritage, craft and performance history rather than as medical treatments. The masks are therefore unusual museum objects: they sit between folklore collection, living craft tradition, theatrical costume and evidence of historical ideas about disease. The best-known visitor site is the family-run Ariyapala Mask Museum, which displays traditional masks, explains carving practices and connects the objects with dance traditions including Sanni healing performances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ariyapalamasksmuseum.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ariyapalamasksmuseum.com">[ariyapalamasksmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ariyapalamasksmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ariyapala Mask Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ambalangoda &#124; HomepageSeptember 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 17, 2022</span></span></span>

## The demons linked to particular diseases

The most distinctive feature of Ambalangoda’s healing masks is that each demon has a role within a symbolic classification of illness. Rather than showing a general evil spirit, Sanni masks give individual conditions a visible identity. Historical accounts describe eighteen major Sanni demons, although researchers have found variations in names and details between sources and regional traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1761180/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCSri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 23, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 2006</span></span></span>

The masks transformed invisible suffering into recognisable characters. A patient’s symptoms could be interpreted through this system and represented through a particular masked performer. Examples include:

* a demon associated with blindness, represented through the Kana Sanniya mask;
* a demon associated with deafness, represented through Bihiri Sanniya;
* a demon associated with lameness or paralysis, represented through Kora Sanniya;
* demons linked with stomach disorders, fever, respiratory problems and mental disturbance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://australian.museum/learn/cultures/international-collection/daha-ata-sanniya-masks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: australian.museum">[The Australian Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">australian.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Australian Museum Daha Ata Sanniya Masks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Australian MuseumDaha Ata Sanniya Masks - The Australian Museum...</span></span></span>

This does not mean that traditional practitioners were creating a medical classification in the modern scientific sense. Instead, the masks preserved a cultural map of suffering. The system organised frightening experiences into named forms that could be addressed through ritual action. A modern visitor can understand the masks as a historical record of how one society interpreted the relationship between illness, fear and social order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1761180/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCSri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 23, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 2006</span></span></span>

The most complex figure in this tradition is Maha Kola, a chief demon figure often associated with the complete set of illnesses. Accounts describe the Maha Kola mask as incorporating smaller representations of other demons, visually gathering the whole disease system into one dramatic object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bmj.com/content/333/7582/1327" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bmj.com">[BMJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease &#124; The BMJ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease &#124; The BMJ...</span></span></span>

## How healing ceremonies mixed fear, humour and performance

The power of the masks came not only from their appearance but from how they were used. In the Sanni Yakuma ritual, masks were worn by performers during a long ceremony involving music, dancing, dialogue and interaction with the affected person and their community. The ritual was not a silent display of supernatural fear; it was a staged encounter in which threatening figures could also become comic and controllable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bmj.com/content/333/7582/1327" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bmj.com">[BMJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease &#124; The BMJ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease &#124; The BMJ...</span></span></span>

This mixture of terror and humour is one of the tradition’s most unusual features. The demons may have exaggerated teeth, bulging eyes and distorted expressions, but the performance often reduces their power by mocking them. Anthropological studies of Sanni rituals have highlighted the dramatic nature of these encounters, where demons appear as characters within a social performance rather than simply as frightening beings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/abs/ritual-drama-of-the-sanni-demons-collective-representations-of-disease-in-ceylon/20ACFB351B59DCC77ADDFDA18E9CFDD0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Ritual Drama of the Sanni Demons: Collective Representations of Disease in Ceylon &#124; Comparativ...</span></span></span>

The ritual also worked at a community level. Illness affected not only an individual but families and neighbours who witnessed the ceremony. The performance created a shared explanation for suffering and provided a structured response when people faced conditions that were poorly understood. Scholars have discussed these rituals as having possible psychological and social effects, including providing reassurance, emotional release and a sense of order during illness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1761180/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCSri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 23, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 2006</span></span></span>

For visitors seeing the masks in Ambalangoda today, this context is important. A carved demon face hanging on a wall is only the physical remains of a much larger event: a night-time performance with dancers, drummers, ritual specialists and a community gathered around a person seeking relief. The mask was not originally made as a decoration; it was part of a complete healing system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1761180/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCSri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 23, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 2006</span></span></span>

## What changes when ritual masks become tourist objects

Ambalangoda’s masks occupy an unusual position between sacred object, artistic craft and tourist souvenir. The town remains closely associated with traditional mask carving, and institutions such as the Ariyapala Mask Museum present masks through exhibitions, workshops and craft demonstrations. These displays help preserve knowledge of carving techniques and performance traditions that have become less common in everyday life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ariyapalamasksmuseum.com/about.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ariyapalamasksmuseum.com">[ariyapalamasksmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ariyapalamasksmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ariyapala Mask Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ambalangoda &#124; About US...</span></span></span>

However, museum display changes the meaning of the object. A Sanni mask in a gallery is usually viewed as an example of art history or folklore, while the same type of mask in a healing ceremony was an active participant in a ritual. The shift from ritual space to museum space makes the object easier for outsiders to understand, but it can also remove the sounds, movement, beliefs and community relationships that originally gave it meaning.

This tension is central to Ambalangoda’s strange-collection appeal. The masks are not curiosities because they are bizarre decorations; they are unusual because they preserve a complete worldview in carved wood. They show how a society once connected disease, spirits, performance and healing, while also revealing how living traditions adapt when they become heritage attractions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ariyapalamasksmuseum.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ariyapalamasksmuseum.com">[ariyapalamasksmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ariyapalamasksmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ariyapala Mask Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ambalangoda &#124; HomepageSeptember 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 17, 2022</span></span></span>
