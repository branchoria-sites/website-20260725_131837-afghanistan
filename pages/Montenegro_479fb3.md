## Introduction

The best strange-history experiences are therefore not always the country’s largest museums. They are objects and sites where collecting, devotion, military power, tourism and disputed storytelling overlap. Some are professionally curated and publicly accessible; others are working religious treasures, fragile archaeological objects or neglected historic structures rather than conventional visitor attractions. Understanding that difference is essential. Montenegro rewards curious travellers, but it also requires them to distinguish a genuine collection from tourist folklore, and a preserved historic object from a place that is merely photogenic or abandoned.

## Where Montenegro’s strangest collections are found

The strongest concentration lies between Kotor, Tivat and Cetinje. Kotor supplies the eccentric private collection, Tivat the industrial and military oddity, and Cetinje the country’s densest cluster of historic museums, sacred objects and state symbolism. Northern towns such as Pljevlja add archaeological rarities that are less visible to ordinary tourists.

This geography reflects Montenegro’s history. The Adriatic coast accumulated maritime objects, imported art and cosmopolitan curiosities. Cetinje, the former royal capital, became a storehouse for national memory, diplomatic gifts, religious treasures and royal possessions. Inland regional museums preserve material from prehistoric settlements, Roman burials and local traditions. The result is not a unified “museum of the strange”, but a network of small institutions and singular objects whose unusual qualities emerge once their stories are explained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.narodnimuzej.me/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.narodnimuzej.me">[narodnimuzej.me]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.narodnimuzej.me</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on narodnimuzej.me.</span></span></span>

## A Cold War submarine among the superyachts

At Tivat’s Naval Heritage Collection, the most memorable exhibit is not inside a glass case. It is the former Yugoslav Navy submarine P-821 *Heroj*, a diesel-electric patrol vessel launched in 1967. Visitors can enter its narrow interior, passing crew quarters, machinery, control equipment and torpedo spaces. A smaller submarine, P-912 *Una*, is also associated with the collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://leightontravels.com/2025/05/18/touring-the-hero-p-821-submarine-in-tivat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leightontravels.com">[leightontravels.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leightontravels.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Leighton Travels!Touring the Hero P-821 Submarine in Tivat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 18, 2025 — 18 May 2025 — Hero P-821 was built in 1967 in Split, Croatia, the lead vessel of a series of submarines known as the Hero...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2025</span></span></span>

The setting gives the object much of its strange power. The submarine stands at Porto Montenegro, now known for luxury yachts, restaurants and high-end property. A vessel designed for secrecy, coastal defence and underwater surveillance has become a public exhibit within one of the Adriatic’s most polished leisure developments.

This is a commercial visitor attraction, but the submarine itself is an authentic military artefact rather than a themed reconstruction. Its cramped passages provide a physical understanding of submarine life that photographs and display panels cannot reproduce. Unlike many military museums, which present weapons at a safe distance, the Tivat collection allows visitors to step inside the machine and experience how little space separated sailors from engines, batteries and torpedoes.

## Cetinje’s enormous relief map

One of Montenegro’s most unusual state-owned exhibits is the relief map of Montenegro beside the Biljarda museum complex in Cetinje. Created during the Austro-Hungarian occupation in the First World War, it turned the mountainous country into a three-dimensional military planning object. UNESCO’s description of Cetinje’s historic core identifies the relief as one of the city’s significant monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cetinje Historic Core</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Heritage Centre Cetinje Historic Core</span></span></span>

The map is unusual because it sits somewhere between cartography, sculpture, intelligence tool and war relic. Valleys, lakes, passes and mountain chains are rendered as physical terrain rather than conventional lines on paper. For modern visitors, it resembles an immense model landscape. For the occupying army, the same object represented routes, obstacles and strategic control.

Its location within Cetinje’s museum district adds another interpretive layer. The former royal capital contains institutions built to express Montenegrin sovereignty and identity, yet this particular exhibit was made by a foreign military power occupying the country. It preserves not only geography but the gaze of an army attempting to master that geography.

The relief is frequently described online with inconsistent measurements and technical details. Its broad origin and military purpose are well established, but precise claims about scale, construction personnel or methods should be checked against current museum interpretation rather than repeated from travel blogs or social media.

## Sacred relics between museum and pilgrimage

Cetinje also contains Montenegro’s most powerful collection of objects whose status cannot be reduced to “museum exhibits”. The Cetinje Monastery treasury is associated with the right hand of John the Baptist and a fragment traditionally identified as part of the True Cross. The National Museum holds the icon known as Our Lady of Philermos, an image deeply connected with the Knights Hospitaller and the later Order of Malta.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cetinje Historic Core</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Heritage Centre Cetinje Historic Core</span></span></span>

These objects are unusual not because they are deliberately macabre or eccentric, but because their claimed identities, journeys and forms of custody span centuries of religious and political upheaval. The Philermos icon travelled through Rhodes, Malta, Russia and Yugoslavia before becoming publicly known in Montenegro. The Order of Malta records that it had disappeared during the Second World War and was later found in Montenegro.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orderofmalta.int/news/pilgrimage-to-the-madonna-of-philermo-in-montenegro/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orderofmalta.int">[Sovereign Military Order of Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orderofmalta.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orderofmalta.int.</span></span></span>

The icon’s traditional association with Saint Luke belongs to devotional legend, not secure art-historical proof. Scholarly discussion generally treats the surviving image as a medieval Byzantine work and examines whether it is a fragment of a larger composition. Its fame depends as much on centuries of veneration, military patronage and reputed miracles as on the painted panel itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/36456704/The_Icon_of_the_Madonna_of_Fil%C3%A9rimos_Rethinking_its_History_and_Iconography" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

Its ownership and appropriate location remain culturally sensitive. A 2025 Maltese discussion renewed calls for the icon to return to Malta, at least temporarily, reflecting its central place in the history of the Order of St John. Montenegro, meanwhile, treats it as one of Cetinje’s most important treasures. The disagreement illustrates how sacred collections differ from ordinary museum property: they can simultaneously be artworks, diplomatic objects, national possessions and living devotional symbols.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://timesofmalta.com/article/a-call-return-malta-lost-icon.1114761" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: timesofmalta.com">[Times of Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">timesofmalta.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Times of Malta A call to return Malta&#x27;s lost icon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Times of Malta A call to return Malta&#x27;s lost icon</span></span></span>

Visitors should not assume that every relic is displayed like an archaeological artefact with unrestricted access and neutral labelling. Some remain embedded in worship and pilgrimage. Dress, behaviour, photography rules and opening conditions may reflect religious practice rather than standard museum procedure.

## The Roman glass cup that may not be on display

The Heritage Museum in Pljevlja preserves one of Montenegro’s most remarkable archaeological objects: a late Roman cage cup, commonly called the Pljevlja diatretum. Cage cups were made with an inner glass vessel and an outer openwork shell connected to it by small glass bridges. Their fragility and technical complexity make complete surviving examples exceptionally rare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.montenegro.travel/en/explore-montenegro/culture-and-tours/archaeological-sites" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: montenegro.travel">[Montenegro]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">montenegro.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Montenegro WIld Beauty I Archaeological sitesVisit the Pljevlja Homeland Museum to see over 1,200 items excavated from graves h...</span></span></span>

The Pljevlja cup is made from colourless glass with a cobalt-blue decorative cage and inscription. It was recovered from a Roman-period burial area near Pljevlja alongside a much larger body of grave goods, including jewellery, vessels, coins and weapons. The object turns a regional museum into the guardian of an internationally significant example of luxury Roman glassmaking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.montenegro.travel/en/explore-montenegro/culture-and-tours/archaeological-sites" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: montenegro.travel">[Montenegro]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">montenegro.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Montenegro WIld Beauty I Archaeological sitesVisit the Pljevlja Homeland Museum to see over 1,200 items excavated from graves h...</span></span></span>

The practical complication is access. Several museum summaries and older reports state that the cup is not part of the normal permanent display and may be viewed only by prior arrangement. Public information is inconsistent, with some general listings implying that it is displayed while more detailed accounts describe restricted viewing. Travellers should therefore contact the museum before making a special journey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.instagram.com/p/CHUm9goB9eN/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: instagram.com">[instagram.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">instagram.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fact No 7 Diatreta</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fact No 7 Diatreta</span></span></span>

This uncertainty is itself typical of Montenegro’s curiosity landscape. A country may possess an extraordinary object without presenting it as a heavily promoted attraction. Conservation, security, staffing and limited exhibition facilities can matter more than tourist demand.

## Money as a national curiosity

Cetinje’s Money Museum is less uncanny than a prison or sacred relic, but it belongs in Montenegro’s unusual-collection story because it turns the country’s complicated political history into a sequence of currencies, notes, coins and financial systems. It is operated by the Central Bank of Montenegro and occupies a building associated with the country’s early banking history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cbcg.me/en/currency/money-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cbcg.me">[CBCG]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cbcg.me</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Money Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Money Museum</span></span></span>

The permanent exhibition, “Money in Montenegro”, includes rare denominations from different periods and uses multimedia material to explain how currency changed as states, rulers and economic systems changed. The museum also incorporates historic film, including footage connected with Montenegro’s declaration as a kingdom in 1910 and later socialist-era development.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cbcg.me/en/currency/money-museum/permanent-exhibitions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cbcg.me">[CBCG]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cbcg.me</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Permanent Exhibitions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Permanent Exhibitions</span></span></span>

Its real interest lies in the instability of the category “Montenegrin money”. The territory has passed through royal, Yugoslav, socialist and post-Yugoslav systems, while modern Montenegro uses the euro despite not being a member of the eurozone. The museum therefore presents money not simply as payment, but as evidence of sovereignty, political alignment and national aspiration.

Unlike the private Cats Museum, this is an institutional, educational collection with conventional curatorial authority. It is a good counterweight to Montenegro’s more romantic attractions because it shows how an apparently dry object category can reveal repeated changes of regime and identity.

## Jusovača: a prison ruin, not yet a settled prison museum

Jusovača in Podgorica is often included in lists of unusual or dark places, but it should not be described casually as a functioning prison museum. The complex originated under Ottoman rule and later served as a Montenegrin state prison. It has long stood in a neglected or unstable condition, with visitors reporting ruined cells, overgrowth and uncontrolled access rather than a managed exhibition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wanderella.wordpress.com/2015/10/21/one-abandoned-prison-200-years-of-montenegrin-crime-and-punishment/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wanderella.wordpress.com">[wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wanderella.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

Its appeal is easy to understand. The surviving walls and cells offer a direct encounter with the physical history of punishment, confinement and state authority. Yet the absence of formal interpretation creates problems. An abandoned prison can invite dramatic speculation while saying little about who was held there, under what laws, in which periods, or how conditions changed.

Campaigns and planning exercises have proposed transforming Jusovača into an arts or cultural centre. A public consultation on revitalisation was announced in 2020, and earlier civic initiatives also argued for preservation and adaptive reuse. These proposals demonstrate public recognition of the site’s value, but they do not by themselves make it an established museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wanderella.wordpress.com/2015/10/21/one-abandoned-prison-200-years-of-montenegrin-crime-and-punishment/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wanderella.wordpress.com">[Wanderella]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wanderella.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

For readers interested in dark tourism, Jusovača is a useful example of the boundary between heritage and ruin. Its significance is real; its visitor infrastructure and curatorial status are uncertain. Entering unstable structures without official supervision may also present obvious safety and legal risks.

## Lapidariums, grave goods and stones with afterlives

Several Montenegrin museums preserve collections that become strange through accumulation rather than sensational subject matter. Kotor’s Lapidarium brings together carved stone fragments, inscriptions and architectural pieces collected from the town and surrounding region. The collection began to take shape in the early twentieth century and now occupies a restored historic church space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://muzejikotor.me/en/home/lapidarium/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: muzejikotor.me">[muzejikotor.me]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">muzejikotor.me</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on muzejikotor.me.</span></span></span>

A lapidarium can resemble an archaeological afterlife for buildings. Door surrounds, memorial stones, coats of arms and inscriptions are removed from their original settings and reorganised as evidence. Visitors do not see a reconstructed medieval city; they see fragments that survived demolition, reuse, earthquake damage and urban change.

Regional museums provide similar encounters with grave goods. In Pljevlja, more than a thousand objects from excavated burials document Roman wealth, personal adornment and funerary practice. Elsewhere, archaeological collections in Budva, Kotor, Berane and Nikšić preserve material ranging from prehistoric tools to Roman mosaics and Illyrian equipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.montenegro.travel/en/explore-montenegro/culture-and-tours/the-most-popular-museums" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: montenegro.travel">[Montenegro]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">montenegro.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on montenegro.travel.</span></span></span>

These are conventional archaeological institutions, not mystery museums. Their relevance to curiosity culture lies in the objects themselves: miniature glass vessels, carved funerary stones, extinct-animal remains, ancient jewellery and ritual deposits. Responsible interpretation depends on excavation records and conservation, not claims of unexplained origins.

## What Montenegro largely does not have

Reliable evidence does not support presenting Montenegro as a major destination for dedicated UFO museums, cryptid collections, occult archives, witchcraft museums, medical cabinets or permanent torture exhibitions. Such themes may appear in local legends, temporary events or commercial storytelling, but they do not currently form a well-documented national museum circuit.

The country’s strange-history landscape is therefore object-led rather than genre-led. Its curiosities are embedded inside national museums, monasteries, municipal collections and historic sites rather than gathered under labels such as “the unexplained” or “the macabre”.

## How to visit with realistic expectations

Montenegro’s museums are often small, seasonal or dependent on local staffing. Current opening hours should be checked directly, especially for regional institutions, religious treasuries and specialist exhibits. Objects described in tourism material may be in storage, available only during guided visits or temporarily removed for conservation.

The most rewarding approach is to treat Kotor, Tivat and Cetinje as a connected curiosity route. Kotor offers the private and playful Cats Museum alongside archaeological stone collections. Tivat provides the physical intensity of the submarine. Cetinje combines money, royal history, military cartography and sacred treasures within a compact historic centre. Pljevlja is a more specialised journey for travellers particularly interested in Roman archaeology and the cage cup.

Credibility also varies by setting. The Money Museum, National Museum and municipal archaeological institutions are formal public bodies. The Cats Museum is a private thematic collection whose value lies in its period material and cultural influence rather than academic zoology. Monastery relics are interpreted through faith as well as history. Jusovača remains a historic prison site without the stable interpretation expected of a mature prison museum.

Montenegro’s most unusual collections are compelling precisely because they sit at these boundaries. They show how an animal becomes a city brand, how a weapon becomes heritage, how an occupation map becomes a national monument, how an excavated cup becomes almost too precious to display, and how a sacred image can belong simultaneously to art history, pilgrimage and international dispute.
