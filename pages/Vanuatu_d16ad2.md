## The national collection is Vanuatu’s main cabinet of wonders

The National Museum, part of the Vanuatu Cultural Centre, is the clearest starting point for unusual heritage in the country. Its collections combine archaeology, ethnography and natural history rather than separating them into narrowly themed galleries. Reported holdings include masks, slit gongs, mats, model canoes, pottery, shells, fossils and specimens representing the islands’ wildlife. Archaeological material reaches back to the first settlement of the archipelago more than 3,000 years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museu.ms/museum/details/17339" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museu.ms">[Museu.MS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museu.ms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of VanuatuThe National Museum contains a large section of traditional artefacts, from masks and slit gongs, to mats and s...</span></span></span>

What makes the museum unusual is not simply the appearance of individual objects. Many come from societies in which designs, performances and ritual knowledge could belong to particular families, ranks or communities. A mask or decorated figure may therefore be evidence of political status, mortuary practice or an initiation system, not merely an example of “tribal art”. ## Ancestral effigies are not macabre souvenirs

Among the most arresting objects associated with Vanuatu are large mortuary effigies from Malakula. Three such figures and two modelled skulls were returned to Vanuatu from the United States in August 2024 after an FBI investigation into a deceased collector’s holdings. The figures contained human skulls and had been made to represent named deceased men from the Smol Nambas community. One was about 3.5 metres long and weighed roughly 318 kilograms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. These effigies can easily be misread through the language of horror museums. Their strangeness is real, but it lies in a different cultural logic. According to the Vanuatu Cultural Centre curator involved in identifying them, the dead were once placed on raised platforms before selected bones were removed. A figure was later formed around the skull using mud and plant material, painted to preserve the person’s identity and kept in a restricted ceremonial house. The practice ended decades ago following religious change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. The repatriation also reveals how Vanuatu’s most culturally sensitive material became part of the international curiosities market. Human remains and funerary objects from across the Pacific were collected, exchanged and sold during the colonial era and afterwards. In this case, comparison with objects in overseas museums helped specialists establish the figures’ likely origin. Their journey home was therefore both a recovery of ancestors and an exposure of the collecting system that had transformed them into private art commodities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. ## Archaeology supplies Vanuatu’s strangest natural-history object

One of the National Museum’s most remarkable scientific holdings concerns an extinct giant turtle discovered at Teouma on Efate. Archaeologists found bones of a previously unknown horned turtle in deposits associated with the earliest settlers of Vanuatu, dating to around 3,000 years ago. The animal belonged to a lineage of large land turtles previously thought to have disappeared much earlier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2932593/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The pattern of remains makes the discovery more than a palaeontological curiosity. Most recovered elements were limb bones, with few shell fragments and no skull or tail armour. Researchers interpreted this as evidence that the animals were butchered elsewhere before selected portions were carried to the settlement. The find provides the earliest demonstrated encounter between humans and this particular giant-turtle lineage and suggests that hunting contributed to its extinction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2932593/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

This is precisely the kind of object that complicates the boundary between a natural-history collection and an archaeological one. The bones document an extraordinary lost animal, but also food gathering, colonisation and ecological change. They turn an apparently fantastical creature—a heavily armoured land turtle surviving into the human era—into a closely dated part of Vanuatu’s settlement history.

The wider Teouma site is equally important. It includes an early cemetery associated with the Lapita cultural tradition, whose communities expanded across much of the Pacific. Museum collections linked to the excavation therefore connect unusual animal remains with decorated pottery, burial practices and the beginnings of permanent human life in the islands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Vanuatu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of Vanuatu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of Vanuatu</span></span></span>

## Chief Roi Mata’s Domain is a landscape collection

Chief Roi Mata’s Domain is not a museum in the normal sense. It is a group of places on and around Efate associated with the life, death and mass burial of a remembered chief. UNESCO describes the property as an exceptional example of the continuing relationship between Pacific communities, landscape and oral tradition. Its principal elements include a former residence, Fels Cave and the burial island of Artok.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1280.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

For readers interested in tombs, legends and archaeological mysteries, the domain is one of Vanuatu’s strongest sites. Oral accounts preserved the story of a chief whose death was followed by an elaborate burial involving members of his community. Archaeological investigation found evidence that broadly corresponded with important elements of the remembered tradition. The value of the site lies less in proving every detail of a legend than in showing how places, graves and inherited narratives can reinforce one another over centuries.

It should not be approached as a spooky burial attraction. The domain remains part of a living cultural landscape, with access and interpretation shaped by local custodianship. The December 2024 earthquake also caused a major landslide at Fels Cave, leading to access restrictions and recovery work. Current conditions need to be checked before planning a visit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-supports-post-earthquake-recovery-vanuatu-cultural-centre-and-chief-roi-matas-domain-world" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

## Espiritu Santo preserves a war in objects and wreckage

Espiritu Santo offers Vanuatu’s most recognisable form of modern oddity collecting. During the Second World War, the island became a vast Allied base filled with airfields, hospitals, warehouses, ships and military equipment. The South Pacific World War II Museum in Luganville preserves locally found artefacts, photographs, personal effects and fragments of aircraft connected with this period. It operates a public museum in Unity Park while pursuing a larger long-term museum project. Its published hours list weekday opening and additional opening on cruise-ship days, although arrangements should always be confirmed locally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pacific_WWII_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">South Pacific WWII Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South Pacific WWII Museum</span></span></span>

The collection’s appeal comes from its proximity to the places where objects were lost. Exhibits have included aircraft wreckage, a large naval bank-vault door, military identification tags and material connected with the troopship *SS President Coolidge*. The museum has also researched tags found on Santo in an effort to identify their former owners and reconnect them with surviving relatives. This shifts the material away from anonymous militaria and towards individual biographies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pacific_WWII_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">South Pacific WWII Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South Pacific WWII Museum</span></span></span>

Nearby, Million Dollar Point functions as an underwater junk museum without walls. At the end of the war, large quantities of vehicles, machinery, supplies and other equipment were dumped into the sea. Divers and snorkellers can now encounter a dense artificial reef formed around twisted machinery and military debris. Stories about precisely why everything was discarded are often embellished, but the central fact—a huge concentration of abandoned wartime equipment—is well established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://southpacificwwiimuseum.com/mdp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southpacificwwiimuseum.com">[South Pacific WWII Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southpacificwwiimuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on southpacificwwiimuseum.com.</span></span></span>

The wreck of the *SS President Coolidge* provides a more solemn counterpart. The former luxury liner, converted into a troopship, sank after striking mines near Santo in 1942. It is now a major wreck-diving destination and an archaeological site containing traces of military transport, shipboard life and wartime loss. Together, the museum, wreck and dump site form a dispersed collection in which gallery objects, submerged structures and the altered coastline tell different parts of the same story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pacific_WWII_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">South Pacific WWII Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South Pacific WWII Museum</span></span></span>

## Cultural villages mix interpretation with tourism

Community and commercial cultural villages around Efate and other islands sometimes present demonstrations of hunting, food preparation, medicine, warfare and older social customs. Ekasup Cultural Village, for example, advertises accounts of historical cannibalism alongside demonstrations of traps, fishing knowledge and traditional technologies. Such places can be engaging introductions to everyday skills and oral history, especially when interpretation is led by local participants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vanuatu.travel/en/ekasup-cultural-village-nafonu-tatoka-tours" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vanuatu.travel">[Vanuatu Travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vanuatu.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Travel Ekasup Cultural Village</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Travel Ekasup Cultural Village</span></span></span>

They are not museums in the strict curatorial sense. Displays may use replicas, demonstrations and performance rather than accessioned objects with documented provenance. Tours are also shaped by what visitors expect to find “exotic”, which can encourage an outsized focus on cannibalism, magic or supposedly untouched village life. This does not make the experience false, but it does mean that theatrical presentation, local history and tourism marketing need to be distinguished.

Cannibalism in particular demands care. It is part of the documented and remembered history of parts of Vanuatu, but tourism material can flatten diverse islands and communities into a single lurid stereotype. A responsible visit pays attention to who is telling the story, whether a claim concerns a particular place and period, and whether the demonstration represents current practice, ancestral history or a visitor-facing reconstruction.

## What Vanuatu’s strange collections reveal


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Vanuatu’s curiosity culture is unusual because the most compelling material often resists being treated as a curiosity. An ancestral figure may contain the remains of a known person. A drawing may be culturally important precisely because it is performed and erased. A cave may be at once an archaeological location, a legendary setting and a place under customary authority. A pile of corroded military machinery may be both pollution, reef habitat and historical evidence.

</div>

The country also exposes the unequal history of collecting. Sacred objects and human remains were removed into foreign private collections and museums, while local institutions have had to document, identify and negotiate their return. The 2024 repatriation showed that global museum catalogues can help recover provenance, but it also demonstrated the damage caused when objects are detached from the communities able to explain what they are.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. The relics, including two skulls and three large effigies (rambaramp), were recognized by Vanuat...</span></span></span>

For visitors, the most rewarding approach is therefore not to seek a conventional “museum of the bizarre”. Vanuatu offers something richer: a network of national collections, living demonstrations, ancestral landscapes, scientific discoveries and wartime wrecks in which the distinction between exhibit and living heritage is continually negotiated. Its strangest objects matter not because they are freakish, but because they carry unusually visible connections between bodies, memory, land, belief and ownership.
