## Where Cameroon’s strangest collections are found

## Foumban’s monumental royal collection

The Museum of the Bamoun Kings is the clearest starting point for travellers seeking an unusual but substantial museum experience. Opened in Foumban on 13 April 2024, it stands near the historic royal palace and houses more than 10,000 objects; some reports place the collection at roughly 12,500. These include royal thrones, masks, weapons, pipes, musical instruments, sculptures, regalia and ceremonial possessions accumulated through generations of Bamoun rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.voanews.com/a/cameroon-opens-museum-honoring-oldest-sub-saharan-kingdom/7569526.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: voanews.com">[voanews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">voanews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Voice of America Cameroon opens museum honoring oldest sub-Saharan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Voice of AmericaCameroon opens museum honoring oldest sub-Saharan...April 14, 2024 — 14 Apr 2024 — A traditional masked dancer performs...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 14, 2024</span></span></span>

It is one of the rare cases in which a country’s most visually eccentric museum is also one of its most historically important.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://english.aawsat.com/culture/4965166-cameroon-opens-museum-honoring-oldest-sub-saharan-kingdom" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english.aawsat.com">[Asharq Al-Awsat]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english.aawsat.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">4965166 cameroon opens museum honoring oldest sub saharan kingdom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">4965166 cameroon opens museum honoring oldest sub saharan kingdom</span></span></span>

## Palace museums and the problem of secret objects

Mankon, Bafut, Babungo and Oku contain some of Cameroon’s most intriguing collections because their exhibits were not originally made for unrestricted viewing. Objects in Grassfields royal treasuries could belong to rulers, court societies or regulatory associations whose knowledge and performances were limited by rank, gender, initiation or circumstance.

The Mankon Museum, opened beside the palace in the mid-2000s, contains objects drawn largely from royal holdings. Research on the museum records local unease over the display of things that some community members believed should not have been made visible to everyone. That disagreement is central to understanding the site: the museum does not merely explain a vanished belief system but alters the social life of the objects it displays.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0300c641" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: escholarship.org">[escholarship.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">escholarship.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on escholarship.org.</span></span></span>

Academic studies of Grassfields palace museums have found that they remain closely tied to palace authority. Access may be conditional rather than universal, and museums can continue to serve the interests of rulers and notables alongside educational or tourist purposes. They should not automatically be treated as neutral public institutions modelled on European civic museums.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299738505_Museums_in_the_Palaces_of_the_Cameroon_Grassfields_Concerns_about_accessibility_and_sustainability" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Museums in the Palaces of the Cameroon Grassfields6 Apr 2016 — Findings reveal that these museums are still largely see...</span></span></span>

This tension makes the collections especially compelling, but it also requires care. A carved figure labelled as a “ritual statue” may have had a much narrower audience and a more specific role than the label suggests. A mask may not be a disguise in the everyday sense but one element of a complete performance involving costume, music, movement, status and controlled appearance. Museum display freezes the visible component while leaving much of the event absent.

### Babungo and Oku

Babungo was historically known as an important centre of ironworking and sculpture. Its palace museum presents royal and ceremonial material drawn from a much larger treasury, including metalwork, carved objects and possessions associated with kingship and social institutions. The appeal lies partly in the density of skilled manufacture: objects that might appear fantastical are also demonstrations of technical knowledge and court patronage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.travelocameroon.com/places-to-visit/babungo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: travelocameroon.com">[Travel Cameroon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">travelocameroon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Travel Cameroon Babungo &#124; Book Babungo Tour Packages</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Travel Cameroon Babungo &#124; Book Babungo Tour Packages</span></span></span>

At Oku, the palace collection reportedly grew to more than 800 artefacts made from materials including wood, metal, clay, beads, bamboo, leather, feathers, textiles, animal skin and ivory. Before formal museum arrangements, objects were kept in palace rooms, royal treasuries and the stores of secret societies, sometimes in poor conservation conditions. The museum’s development was therefore both a preservation measure and a major change in access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revues.acaref.net/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2021/11/Victor-BAYENA-NGITIR-.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revues.acaref.net">[REVUES DE L&#x27;ACAREF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revues.acaref.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">REVUES DE L&#x27;ACAREFTHE OKU PALACE MUSEUM&#x27;S JOURNEY TOWARDS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">REVUES DE L&#x27;ACAREFTHE OKU PALACE MUSEUM&#x27;S JOURNEY TOWARDS</span></span></span>

These museums are not “witchcraft museums” in the usual tourist sense. They may contain objects connected with spiritual authority, divination, protection, initiation or ancestral belief, but the collections are generally framed through royal history and community heritage rather than through sensational claims about magic.

## Bafut’s war trophies and endangered palace heritage

The Bafut palace complex is one of Cameroon’s most historically charged museum environments. Its museum has displayed royal objects, weapons and material concerning resistance to German colonial forces. Older accounts also describe a section associated with the Battle of Mankon that included the skulls of four German soldiers alongside arms and ammunition. This particular claim appears repeatedly in historical and reference material, but current display status is difficult to verify independently and should not be assumed without checking locally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bafut_Wars" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bafut Wars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bafut Wars</span></span></span>

Human remains transform a museum visit into an ethical question. Even where remains are understood locally as trophies, evidence or memorial objects, their public presentation involves issues of consent, dignity, historic violence and competing interpretations. The interest is not merely that skulls are macabre. It is that the same objects can be read as proof of resistance, trophies of warfare, ancestral history or human remains requiring restricted treatment.

Bafut also demonstrates how vulnerable Cameroon’s collections have become. Human Rights Watch reported that soldiers attacked and looted the royal palace in September 2019. A later UNESCO assessment found cracks and leaks, desecrated sanctuaries, bullet damage, destroyed collections, looting and damage to museum-library documents at heritage sites affected by the Anglophone crisis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2019/10/11/world-heritage-site-attacked-cameroon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Rights Watch World Heritage Site Attacked in Cameroon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights Watch World Heritage Site Attacked in Cameroon</span></span></span>

For travellers, this has an immediate practical consequence. As of July 2026, the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office advises against all travel to Cameroon’s North-West Region, where Bafut, Mankon, Babungo and several other palace museums are located. Their cultural importance should not be confused with present-day accessibility or safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cameroon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cameroon travel advice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cameroon travel advice</span></span></span>

## Dschang: interpreting the visible and invisible

The Museum of Civilisations in Dschang offers the most accessible broad introduction to the ideas behind Cameroon’s unusual collections. Opened to the public in the early 2010s, it presents the country through major cultural regions: the forest south, the coast, the northern savannah and the western Grassfields. Its displays include masks, sculpture, tools, ceremonial objects, architectural forms, photographs and audiovisual interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_des_civilisations_de_Dschang" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Musée des civilisations de Dschang</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Musée des civilisations de Dschang</span></span></span>

Unlike a palace treasury, Dschang is designed as an interpretation centre. Objects are arranged to explain social organisation, political authority, livelihoods and belief rather than to display the possessions of one dynasty. This makes it a useful place to learn why an elephant mask, carved throne or ceremonial robe cannot be understood purely as decoration.

The architecture contributes to the message. Its forms allude to masks and symbolically important animals, including the spider, elephant and buffalo. These references are not random fantasy motifs. Across parts of the Grassfields, animal imagery can communicate power, intelligence, danger, rank or royal identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_des_civilisations_de_Dschang" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Musée des civilisations de Dschang</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Musée des civilisations de Dschang</span></span></span>

For a curiosity-focused visitor, Dschang’s main value is interpretive rather than sensational. It helps separate several ideas that are often collapsed together by outsiders:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Ritual does not necessarily mean occult.** An object can belong to a ceremony without being presented as evidence of supernatural powers.
* **A mask is not an isolated sculpture.** Its meaning may depend on who owns it, who may wear it and when it appears.
* **Secrecy can be institutional.** Restricted knowledge may regulate authority and initiation rather than conceal a supposed mystery from tourists.
* **Animal forms are symbolic language.** Hybrid or exaggerated creatures often communicate status and social ideas rather than literal monster belief.

</div>

## Yaoundé’s private cabinet of heritage

The Blackitude Museum in Yaoundé is one of Cameroon’s more cabinet-like collections. It grew from royal objects inherited and preserved by its founder, Queen Nana Agnes Fo Nab Ngo I, and was subsequently expanded through donations and purchases. The museum is privately run rather than a conventional state institution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackitude_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Blackitude Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blackitude Museum</span></span></span>

Its holdings encompass masks, statues, vessels, pipes, musical instruments, textiles, architectural elements, royal furniture and ceremonial objects, with particular strengths in material associated with the Grassfields and forest communities. Published descriptions place the wider collection at more than 2,000 works, although only a portion is displayed at one time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackitude_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Blackitude Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blackitude Museum</span></span></span>

What makes Blackitude relevant to strange-museum culture is its personal origin. It is partly a family inheritance, partly a royal treasury and partly an ethnographic museum assembled through the judgement of an individual founder. That combination resembles a traditional cabinet of curiosity more closely than Cameroon’s larger civic museums do.

Visitors should nevertheless approach claims about individual objects carefully. Provenance — the documented history of ownership and movement — is particularly important in private ethnographic collections. Copies, modern works, restored pieces and genuinely old ritual objects can all share the same display space. Age, spiritual significance and authenticity should not be inferred from appearance alone.

## Masks, power and misleading labels

Cameroon’s most visually striking museum objects are often its masks, particularly beaded elephant masks from the Grassfields. Examples now held by museums abroad use cloth, fibre, cowrie shells and elaborate beadwork. They were associated with elite societies and public performances connected with wealth, rank and royal authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1954" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishmuseum.org.</span></span></span>

To call such objects “tribal masks” strips away much of their meaning. Ownership could signal privilege. Performance might commemorate a ruler or elite figure. Certain masks were associated with institutions that exercised judicial, political or ritual functions. The face seen in a display case was therefore only one part of a system involving authority, costume, movement and spectatorship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://umma.umich.edu/objects/mask-1997-1-343/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: umma.umich.edu">[umma.umich.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">umma.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

Museum labels may also use broad words such as “secret society”, “fetish” or “witchcraft”. These can mislead when they flatten very different institutions into a single category of exotic religion. A better visitor question is not “Did people believe this object was magical?” but “Who controlled this object, what could it do socially, and under what conditions was it seen?”

That shift changes the museum experience. The strangest element is often not the form of the object but the decision to display it.

## Missing objects and colonial collections

Any account of Cameroon’s museum culture must include what is absent. More than 40,000 cultural objects from Cameroon are held in German museums, according to a major German provenance-research initiative. Many entered European collections during colonial rule, a period marked by military violence, punitive expeditions, coercion and plunder. Large portions of these holdings have still not been fully researched.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kulturgutverluste.de/en/press/cultural-goods-cameroon-german-museums" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kulturgutverluste.de">[Kulturgutverluste]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kulturgutverluste.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cultural goods cameroon german museums</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cultural goods cameroon german museums</span></span></span>

This creates an unusual museum landscape in which some of the country’s most important royal, ritual and ceremonial objects are better represented in Berlin, Stuttgart or other European collections than in the communities from which they came. Cameroonian museums may preserve related objects, local knowledge and living institutions while conspicuous historical pieces remain overseas.

The resulting debate is not simply about sending art back to a modern state. Palace collections may be claimed by traditional rulers, families, ritual associations, municipalities or national authorities. Determining where an object belongs can require reconstructing exactly how it was taken and who held responsibility for it before colonial removal.

This “museum of absences” is one of the most important features of Cameroon’s curiosity culture. Empty spaces, replicas, photographs and oral accounts can carry as much historical weight as the artefacts still present. They also explain why local museums are closely tied to political memory and why the apparently simple act of placing a mask in a gallery can become a dispute about ownership, violence and authority.

## How to visit responsibly

Cameroon’s unusual collections reward a different approach from novelty tourism. Visitors are more likely to gain something from them by treating palaces and ritual displays as living cultural environments rather than as warehouses of bizarre objects.

Practical expectations should remain flexible. Smaller museums may have irregular hours, limited online information, locally arranged guides and restrictions on photography. Objects may be removed for ceremonies, conservation or security. A museum described in older travel material may be inaccessible because of conflict, damage or changes in local administration.

The strongest presently practical route is through the West Region, particularly Foumban, Dschang and heritage sites around Bafoussam, subject to current local advice. Museums in the North-West require a much more serious safety assessment; official UK advice currently warns against all travel to that region.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cameroon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cameroon travel advice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cameroon travel advice</span></span></span>

Inside the museums, responsible curiosity means distinguishing between several kinds of claim. A guide may explain a community belief without asking visitors to accept it as scientific fact. A ritual object may be genuinely old but incompletely documented. A dramatic story may represent palace tradition rather than an independently verifiable historical account. None of these distinctions makes the collection less interesting. They reveal how memory, belief, authority and tourism are being negotiated in the same room.

## Why Cameroon matters to strange-museum culture

Cameroon expands the idea of what an unusual museum can be. Its most distinctive collections are not built around novelty for its own sake. Their strangeness comes from unresolved boundaries: between treasury and gallery, sacred restriction and public access, living authority and historic interpretation, local ownership and colonial displacement.

Foumban offers monumental royal spectacle. Dschang supplies the interpretive framework. Blackitude shows how private inheritance can become public ethnography. The Grassfields palace museums reveal the consequences of displaying objects that were once controlled by rulers and initiated societies. Bafut adds war memory, human remains and the immediate danger posed by armed conflict.

The result is a museum landscape that is fragmented, sometimes difficult to visit and occasionally poorly documented, but unusually rich in questions. Cameroon’s curiosity collections matter because they do not merely preserve mysterious-looking things. They expose the cultural rules that determine who may see, possess, explain and remember them.

        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
