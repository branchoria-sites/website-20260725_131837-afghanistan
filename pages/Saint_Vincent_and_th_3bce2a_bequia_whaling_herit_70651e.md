## Introduction

Bequia’s whaling heritage is one of the most unusual and most controversial cultural traditions in the Caribbean. Unlike historic whaling displays that describe a vanished industry, Bequia preserves a living maritime practice that still operates on a very small scale under international regulation. This makes the island’s heritage museum and surviving whaleboats far more than maritime curiosities: they represent a tradition that some islanders see as an important part of local identity and food culture, while many conservationists and visitors regard the continued hunting of whales as ethically unacceptable. Understanding Bequia therefore requires holding two ideas together at once. The island preserves remarkable boatbuilding skills and maritime history, yet those same traditions remain at the centre of an international debate about culture, conservation and animal welfare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iwc.int/management-and-conservation/whaling/aboriginal/bequia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iwc.int">[International Whaling Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iwc.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span></span></span>

## Why Bequia's Whaling Museum Still Divides Visitors

The Bequia Heritage Museum, overlooking Friendship Bay, occupies an unusual place within [Saint Vincent and the Grenadines]({{ 'saint-vincent-and-the-grenadines/' | relative_url }})' cultural landscape. Rather than celebrating maritime history in isolation, it interprets the people, tools and boats behind a practice that has never entirely disappeared.

Visitors encounter full-sized traditional whaleboats, locally built wooden vessels, harpoons, navigation equipment, photographs, ship models and woodworking tools. These objects are displayed not simply as antiques but as evidence of specialised craftsmanship that enabled small crews to pursue humpback whales from open sailing boats. The museum also links whaling to wider themes including Indigenous settlement, inter-island navigation and Bequia's long tradition of wooden boatbuilding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iwc.int/management-and-conservation/whaling/aboriginal/bequia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iwc.int">[International Whaling Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iwc.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span></span></span>

The result is a museum experience unlike most maritime collections. Many historic boat museums commemorate extinct industries; Bequia instead asks visitors to consider artefacts connected with a regulated activity that still survives. That reality shapes almost every reaction to the collection. Some visitors admire the seamanship and engineering involved, while others struggle to separate the historic craftsmanship from the killing of whales itself.

## Inside the Bequia Heritage Museum

The museum's displays are strongest when explaining how an isolated island community developed practical maritime expertise.

Among the most significant exhibits are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Traditional Bequian whaleboats modelled on nineteenth-century New England designs.
* Boatbuilding tools showing how vessels were constructed using local craftsmanship.
* Harpoons, lances and associated hunting equipment illustrating the technology used before modern industrial whaling.
* Historic photographs documenting crews, shore stations and processing sites.
* Maritime models and navigation objects placing whaling within Bequia's broader seafaring culture.

</div>

One particularly striking aspect is that many of these objects are not relics from an abandoned profession. Similar styles of open wooden boats continue to be used in the legally permitted hunt, reinforcing the continuity between museum display and living heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iwc.int/management-and-conservation/whaling/aboriginal/bequia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iwc.int">[International Whaling Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iwc.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span></span></span>

For readers interested in unusual collections, this makes the museum significant not because it contains rare artefacts alone, but because it preserves practical knowledge that remains embedded within a small community rather than existing only in archives.

## How the Traditional Hunt Developed

Bequia's whaling tradition is often misunderstood as an ancient Indigenous practice. The historical record is more complex.

Modern whaling on Bequia developed during the late nineteenth century after William Thomas Wallace Jr., a Scottish sailor with experience aboard New England whaling ships, introduced Yankee-style hunting methods. Following the decline of plantation agriculture, whaling became an important seasonal source of income, oil and protein for island residents. By the end of the nineteenth century the industry had expanded substantially, exporting whale oil overseas while supporting local shipbuilders and mariners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bequia.travel/history-culture.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bequia.travel">[bequia.travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bequia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History &amp; Culture · Whalers, Shipbuilders &amp; the Sea &#124; Bequia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bequia History &amp; Culture · Whalers, Shipbuilders &amp; the Sea &#124; Bequia.travel...</span></span></span>

Today only a tiny remnant survives. The export trade has disappeared, and the hunt operates under an International Whaling Commission (IWC) quota allowing up to four humpback whales annually under the category of Aboriginal Subsistence Whaling. In practice, weather, the reliance on traditional sailing boats and the difficulty of locating whales mean the full quota is seldom reached.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iwc.int/management-and-conservation/whaling/aboriginal/bequia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iwc.int">[International Whaling Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iwc.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span></span></span>

This historical evolution explains why Bequia generates debate even within whaling policy circles. Unlike Arctic Indigenous hunts with much older documented origins, Bequia's tradition dates primarily from the nineteenth century, leading some critics to question whether the IWC's Aboriginal Subsistence Whaling category is the most appropriate framework. Supporters argue that more than a century of continuous cultural practice has created a distinct local tradition deserving recognition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bequia.travel/history-culture.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bequia.travel">[bequia.travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bequia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History &amp; Culture · Whalers, Shipbuilders &amp; the Sea &#124; Bequia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bequia History &amp; Culture · Whalers, Shipbuilders &amp; the Sea &#124; Bequia.travel...</span></span></span>

## Culture, Conservation and Ethical Dispute

The ethical argument surrounding Bequia rarely centres on legality alone. Instead, it reflects competing values that cannot easily be reconciled.

Supporters emphasise several points:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The hunt is internationally regulated rather than commercially open.
* Traditional sailing boats and hand-thrown harpoons greatly limit the scale of catches.
* Whale meat is distributed locally rather than exported internationally.
* Boatbuilding, navigation and crew traditions form part of Bequia's cultural identity.
* The hunt contributes to local food supplies and ceremonial community life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iwc.int/management-and-conservation/whaling/aboriginal/bequia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iwc.int">[International Whaling Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iwc.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Whaling Commission Bequia</span></span></span>

</div>

Critics focus on different concerns:

* Humpback whales are highly intelligent marine mammals whose hunting raises animal welfare questions regardless of population status.
* Whale watching has become an important economic alternative across the Caribbean, creating incentives for living whales rather than harvested ones.
* Some conservation organisations question whether Bequia's nineteenth-century origins fit the intent of Aboriginal Subsistence Whaling rules.
* Modern ethical attitudes towards whaling have shifted dramatically since the industry first developed, changing how many visitors interpret the practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jrca/16/0/16_59/_article/-char/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstage.jst.go.jp">[J-STAGE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstage.jst.go.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">J-STAGEWhale Watching: Trouble on the Small Whaling Island of Bequia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">J-STAGEWhale Watching: Trouble on the Small Whaling Island of Bequia</span></span></span>

Neither side generally disputes that the hunt is extremely small compared with historical commercial whaling. Instead, disagreement centres on whether cultural continuity justifies maintaining even a limited whale hunt in the twenty-first century.

## What the International Whaling Commission Actually Regulates

The IWC distinguishes Aboriginal Subsistence Whaling from commercial whaling.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://awionline.org/content/subsistence-whaling" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: awionline.org">[awionline.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">awionline.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

According to the Commission, Aboriginal Subsistence Whaling exists to support the cultural and nutritional needs of recognised communities while maintaining whale populations at sustainable levels. Quotas are established in multi-year blocks using scientific assessments of whale populations, and governments must provide detailed information about the cultural importance and operation of each hunt. The quota applies to whales struck rather than successfully landed, reflecting a precautionary management approach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iwc.int/management-and-conservation/whaling/aboriginal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iwc.int">[International Whaling Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iwc.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Whaling Commission Aboriginal Subsistence Whaling in the Arctic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Whaling Commission Aboriginal Subsistence Whaling in the Arctic</span></span></span>

For Bequia, the hunt remains subject to reporting requirements and international review. This does not mean that all parties agree with the policy. ## Why the Museum Matters Beyond Whaling

The Bequia Heritage Museum ultimately functions as more than a museum about whales. It preserves evidence of a distinctive island society whose survival depended on extraordinary maritime skill.

Its boats demonstrate advanced woodworking traditions developed on a small Caribbean island. Its tools illustrate techniques handed down through generations of builders and sailors. Its photographs document communities whose livelihoods combined fishing, boatbuilding, navigation and seasonal whaling. Even visitors who strongly oppose whaling often acknowledge the importance of preserving these historical records.

That combination of admiration and discomfort is precisely what makes the museum unusual within Saint Vincent and the Grenadines' landscape of heritage attractions. Rather than presenting a settled historical narrative, it asks visitors to confront a tradition that remains culturally meaningful to some residents while ethically troubling to many outsiders. Few museums preserve objects that are simultaneously historical artefacts, symbols of community identity and reminders of a practice that continues to provoke international debate.
