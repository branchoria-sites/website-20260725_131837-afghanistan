## Introduction

The broken remains of the Hejaz Railway are among [Saudi Arabia]({{ 'where-saudi-arabia-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s most atmospheric open-air historical collections. Rather than existing inside a single museum, they are scattered across hundreds of kilometres of desert as abandoned stations, rusting locomives, collapsed water towers, stone workshops and stretches of narrow-gauge track. Together they preserve the story of an ambitious Ottoman railway built to carry pilgrims to Madinah while strengthening imperial control over western Arabia, only to be shattered by war and rapidly overtaken by modern transport. Today these fragments occupy a distinctive place within Saudi Arabia's curiosity landscape: they are part industrial archaeology, part pilgrimage history and part desert ruin, with several restored stations functioning as museums while other remains still stand where they were left more than a century ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saudipedia.com/en/the-railway-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saudipedia.com">[saudipedia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saudipedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Railway Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Railway Museum - SaudipediaFebruary 8, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2021</span></span></span>

## Pilgrimage, empire and the railway's original purpose

The Hejaz Railway was conceived in 1900 under Ottoman Sultan Abdul Hamid II. Officially, its primary purpose was to make the pilgrimage to Madinah safer, faster and more affordable for Muslims travelling from Damascus and other parts of the empire. Before the railway, the journey could take around forty days by camel caravan through difficult terrain exposed to weather, disease and raids. Once the line opened to Madinah in 1908, the same journey could be completed in roughly five days.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saudipedia.com/en/the-railway-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saudipedia.com">[Saudipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saudipedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Railway Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Railway Museum - SaudipediaFebruary 8, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2021</span></span></span>

The railway also had a clear political role. By linking distant provinces with a permanent transport corridor, the Ottoman government could move troops and supplies far more efficiently while reinforcing its authority across the Hejaz. Pilgrimage and imperial strategy therefore became inseparable. What appears today as a chain of isolated ruins once formed a carefully engineered system of stations, workshops, bridges, wells and defensive structures stretching across one of the world's harshest landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openarchive.icomos.org/412/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openarchive.icomos.org">[openarchive.icomos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openarchive.icomos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Conserving and interpreting the Hijaz Railway in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ICOMOS Open Archive: EPrints on Cultural Heritage...</span></span></span>

For visitors interested in unusual heritage, this dual purpose makes the railway especially compelling. Unlike many abandoned railways that served commerce alone, every surviving station also belonged to one of the Islamic world's most significant pilgrimage routes. The landscape preserves evidence of both religious travel and geopolitical ambition in the same surviving buildings.

## Stations, locomotives and surviving desert remains

The fascination of the Saudi section lies in its fragmentation. Rather than encountering one complete preserved railway, visitors find scattered relics that collectively function as a vast outdoor archive.

Among the most significant surviving sites are:

* **Madinah Station**, now restored as the Hejaz Railway Museum, where original workshops, station buildings, locomotives and rolling stock have been conserved alongside archaeological exhibitions explaining the wider history of the city.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saudipedia.com/en/the-railway-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saudipedia.com">[Saudipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saudipedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Railway Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Railway Museum - SaudipediaFebruary 8, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2021</span></span></span>
* **Tabuk Station**, where the original Ottoman station complex survives with its stone buildings, water tower and restored railway vehicles. The station now operates as a heritage museum rather than a functioning railway.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.islamicarchitecturalheritage.com/listings/hijaz-railway-station-tabuk-station" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islamicarchitecturalheritage.com">[islamicarchitecturalheritage.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islamicarchitecturalheritage.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hijaz railway station tabuk station</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hijaz Railway Tabuk Station &#124; IRCICASeptember 29, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 29, 2020</span></span></span>
* **The AlUla and Hegra (Mada'in Salih) corridor**, where embankments, bridges, sidings and station remains still cut across the desert near one of Saudi Arabia's most important archaeological landscapes. These remains demonstrate how modern industrial infrastructure was threaded through an area already rich in ancient monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehejazrailway.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehejazrailway.com">[Hejaz Railway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehejazrailway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hejaz Railway HOME &#124; Hejaz Railway</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hejaz Railway HOME &#124; Hejaz Railway</span></span></span>

What makes these remains unusual is that many survive in their original setting rather than having been relocated into galleries. Steam locomotives, freight wagons and passenger coaches have often remained close to the places where they were abandoned after the railway ceased operating. The dry desert climate has slowed deterioration, allowing ironwork and masonry to survive remarkably well despite decades of exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehejazrailway.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehejazrailway.com">[Hejaz Railway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehejazrailway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hejaz Railway HOME &#124; Hejaz Railway</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hejaz Railway HOME &#124; Hejaz Railway</span></span></span>

Even apparently modest structures tell important stories. Water towers reveal the enormous logistical challenge of supplying steam engines in an arid environment. Repair workshops demonstrate that maintaining locomotives deep inside the desert required substantial engineering facilities. Stone station buildings provided accommodation for railway workers, military personnel and pilgrims, turning isolated outposts into small settlements.

Unlike carefully reconstructed heritage railways elsewhere, the Saudi remains often preserve an unfinished quality. Empty platforms, disconnected rails and silent workshops convey abandonment as much as technological achievement, giving the sites an atmosphere closer to industrial archaeology than conventional transport museums.

## Why the ruins became symbols of war

The railway's working life proved remarkably short.

During the First World War, the line became a strategic military target because it carried Ottoman soldiers, weapons and supplies. Arab Revolt forces, supported by British officers including T. E. Lawrence, repeatedly attacked bridges, tracks and trains in an effort to disrupt Ottoman communications. These operations entered popular culture through memoirs and later films, but the surviving ruins demonstrate that the conflict was fought against a very real industrial landscape rather than an empty desert.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehejazrailway.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehejazrailway.com">[Hejaz Railway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehejazrailway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hejaz Railway HOME &#124; Hejaz Railway</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hejaz Railway HOME &#124; Hejaz Railway</span></span></span>

Many dramatic photographs showing overturned locomotives or damaged rolling stock are associated with this wartime campaign. However, not every abandoned locomotive visible today can be confidently linked to a specific attack. Some equipment was simply left behind after operations ceased or during the railway's gradual abandonment. Distinguishing documented wartime wreckage from later abandonment is therefore important when interpreting individual sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehejazrailway.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehejazrailway.com">[Hejaz Railway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehejazrailway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hejaz Railway HOME &#124; Hejaz Railway</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hejaz Railway HOME &#124; Hejaz Railway</span></span></span>

After the war, changing political borders, the collapse of Ottoman rule, growing road transport and eventually commercial aviation made reconstruction increasingly impractical. Although proposals to revive the railway appeared over subsequent decades, the Saudi sections never returned to regular operation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openarchive.icomos.org/412/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openarchive.icomos.org">[openarchive.icomos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openarchive.icomos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Conserving and interpreting the Hijaz Railway in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ICOMOS Open Archive: EPrints on Cultural Heritage...</span></span></span>

## Why the railway still haunts the desert

The Hejaz Railway occupies an unusual position within Saudi Arabia's heritage. It is neither a fully preserved museum railway nor a completely forgotten ruin. Instead, different sections exist at different stages of preservation.

Some stations have been carefully restored and adapted for public interpretation, allowing visitors to walk through workshops, inspect locomotives and understand the railway's engineering. Other stretches remain almost untouched, where broken masonry, disappearing embankments and isolated rails gradually merge into the surrounding desert.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saudipedia.com/en/the-railway-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saudipedia.com">[Saudipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saudipedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Railway Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Railway Museum - SaudipediaFebruary 8, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2021</span></span></span>

Heritage specialists increasingly describe the railway as a cultural route rather than a collection of isolated monuments. The significance lies not only in individual stations but also in the continuous landscape connecting pilgrimage roads, Ottoman engineering, archaeological sites and modern heritage tourism. This broader interpretation recognises that the railway followed corridors used for centuries by traders and pilgrims before the first locomotive arrived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openarchive.icomos.org/412/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openarchive.icomos.org">[openarchive.icomos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openarchive.icomos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Conserving and interpreting the Hijaz Railway in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ICOMOS Open Archive: EPrints on Cultural Heritage...</span></span></span>

The railway therefore belongs naturally within Saudi Arabia's wider landscape of unusual heritage. Its abandoned engines and silent stations are not curiosities because they are mysterious or supernatural, but because they preserve a rare moment when steam technology, imperial politics and one of the world's greatest pilgrimage traditions briefly converged in the Arabian desert. Few historic railways elsewhere leave such an extensive chain of authentic industrial ruins scattered across an otherwise timeless landscape.
