## Introduction

Traditional silver jewellery in Oman was never simply decorative. For centuries, necklaces, pendants, amulet cases and figurative ornaments were believed to protect their wearers from envy, misfortune, harmful spirits and illness while also signalling family identity, status and regional tradition. Today these objects are among the most intriguing items displayed in Oman's ethnographic collections because they reveal how everyday life combined practical craftsmanship with deeply held folk beliefs. Rather than presenting them as evidence of magic in a sensational sense, museums interpret them as material expressions of social history, showing how protective ideas coexisted with Islamic practice, local customs and regional identities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nm.gov.om/en/collection/gift/crafts-industries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nm.gov.om">[National Museum Oman]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nm.gov.om</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum Omancollections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum Omancollections</span></span></span>

## What made Omani silver amulets protective?

Across Oman, silver itself was widely regarded as a beneficial material. Before gold became the preferred jewellery metal during the late twentieth century, finely worked silver ornaments formed an important part of a woman's personal wealth and were often passed between generations. Many pieces also carried an explicitly protective purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nmc.utoronto.ca/research-publications/faculty-publications/beauty-and-belief-endangered-tradition-omani-silver" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nmc.utoronto.ca">[Near &amp; Middle Eastern Civilizations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nmc.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Near &amp; Middle Eastern CivilizationsBeauty and Belief: The Endangered Tradition of Omani Silver Jewellery &#124; Department of Near &amp; Middle Ea...</span></span></span>

Protective jewellery addressed several common anxieties:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Defence against the evil eye, especially for children and pregnant women.
* Protection from harmful spirits or unseen dangers.
* Assistance with fertility, pregnancy and childbirth.
* General good fortune and family wellbeing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumnecklace; amulet &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumnecklace; amulet &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

The distinction between decoration and protection was often blurred. An elaborate necklace could simultaneously display wealth, identify the wearer's home region and serve as an amulet.

### The distinctive *taswira* amulet

Among the most recognisable forms in northern Oman is the *taswira*, an abstract human-shaped silver pendant found almost nowhere else in the Gulf. Museum collections describe these figures as headless human forms suspended from a ring, making them instantly recognisable among Omani ornaments. According to museum catalogues and specialist studies, these pendants were commonly worn by children to guard against evil influences and misfortune, while women also wore them as fertility symbols, particularly during pregnancy. Some traditions further associated them with helping young children learn to walk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nm.gov.om/en/collection/gift/crafts-industries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nm.gov.om">[National Museum Oman]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nm.gov.om</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum Omancollections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum Omancollections</span></span></span>

Their appearance often surprises modern visitors because they resemble stylised human figures rather than conventional jewellery. Museums therefore use them to demonstrate that the symbolic meaning of an object could outweigh its aesthetic design.

### Amulet cases containing sacred texts

Another important protective form was the silver amulet container, sometimes called a *hirz*. Rather than relying solely on the metal itself, these hollow pendants enclosed handwritten Qur'anic verses or other devotional texts prepared for a specific individual.

Museum research notes that the contents were personal and normally sealed permanently inside the silver case by the silversmith. The protective power therefore came from both the sacred text and the carefully crafted container.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumnecklace; amulet &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumnecklace; amulet &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## Amulets and the evil eye

Belief in the evil eye—the idea that envy or hostile attention could bring illness, bad luck or harm—has long been widespread across the Middle East and many other parts of the world. In Oman, silver jewellery formed one practical response to this concern.

Museum collections include particularly striking examples. One silver disc in the National Museum's collection is decorated on its reverse with a tiny bound female spirit. According to the museum's interpretation, the chained figure represents a captured spirit whose imprisonment warns other evil beings to stay away from the wearer. Rather than depicting an object of worship, the imagery symbolises protection through domination over harmful supernatural forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nm.gov.om/en/collection/gift/crafts-industries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nm.gov.om">[National Museum Oman]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nm.gov.om</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum Omancollections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum Omancollections</span></span></span>

These displays illustrate an important distinction. Museums generally present such objects as evidence of historical folk belief rather than endorsing the beliefs themselves. Visitors encounter them as windows into the ways communities understood danger, illness and misfortune before modern medicine and scientific explanations became widespread.

## Why regional styles mattered

Protective jewellery was not identical throughout Oman. Regional traditions shaped both appearance and the way an amulet was expected to work.

Specialist studies comparing surviving pieces note differences between northern Oman, central Oman and Dhofar. In northern Oman, protective necklaces were commonly worn openly where they also served as visible ornaments. In parts of central Oman and Dhofar, however, some traditions held that an amulet worked best when concealed beneath clothing rather than displayed publicly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumnecklace; amulet &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumnecklace; amulet &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

These differences remind visitors that there was never a single uniform system of folk belief across the country. Protective customs varied according to local communities, trade connections and inherited family traditions.

## How museums interpret these objects today

For anyone exploring Oman's curiosity culture, ethnographic museums provide the best place to understand protective silver jewellery in context.

The National Museum presents individual objects with detailed explanations of their protective functions, identifying children's amulets, spirit-protecting discs and symbolic figurative pendants as part of the country's craft heritage rather than isolated curiosities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nm.gov.om/en/collection/gift/crafts-industries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nm.gov.om">[National Museum Oman]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nm.gov.om</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum Omancollections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum Omancollections</span></span></span>

Private collections such as Bait Al Zubair similarly display jewellery alongside clothing, weapons and domestic artefacts, allowing visitors to see that ornaments belonged within a complete social world rather than existing as magical objects on their own. Together these displays explain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* how jewellery marked age, marriage and family status;
* how protective beliefs became embedded in everyday possessions;
* how skilled silversmiths combined artistic craftsmanship with symbolic meaning; and
* how local traditions differed across Oman's regions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nmc.utoronto.ca/research-publications/faculty-publications/beauty-and-belief-endangered-tradition-omani-silver" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nmc.utoronto.ca">[Near &amp; Middle Eastern Civilizations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nmc.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Near &amp; Middle Eastern CivilizationsBeauty and Belief: The Endangered Tradition of Omani Silver Jewellery &#124; Department of Near &amp; Middle Ea...</span></span></span>

</div>

This interpretive approach helps avoid a common misunderstanding. Modern museum labels generally frame these ornaments as cultural evidence rather than proof of supernatural claims, encouraging visitors to understand how belief systems shaped material culture.

## Why these silver amulets matter today

Traditional Omani silver jewellery has become considerably rarer in everyday life. During the late twentieth century, changing fashions and the growing popularity of gold jewellery contributed to the decline of many older silversmithing traditions. Researchers studying museum collections describe this body of craftsmanship as an endangered tradition whose techniques and symbolic meanings risk being forgotten if they are not documented and displayed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nmc.utoronto.ca/research-publications/faculty-publications/beauty-and-belief-endangered-tradition-omani-silver" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nmc.utoronto.ca">[Near &amp; Middle Eastern Civilizations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nmc.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Near &amp; Middle Eastern CivilizationsBeauty and Belief: The Endangered Tradition of Omani Silver Jewellery &#124; Department of Near &amp; Middle Ea...</span></span></span>

That makes protective jewellery especially significant within Oman's landscape of unusual collections. Unlike spectacular curiosities such as giant whale skeletons or archaeological treasures, these modest silver pendants reveal something more intimate: how ordinary people attempted to safeguard children, pregnancies, households and personal wellbeing through objects worn every day.

For visitors interested in folklore rather than sensational legends, Oman's silver amulets offer one of the country's richest examples of how craft, religion, local custom and popular belief became inseparable in the objects people carried closest to their bodies.
