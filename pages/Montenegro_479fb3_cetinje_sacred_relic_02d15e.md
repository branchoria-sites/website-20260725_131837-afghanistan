## Introduction

Cetinje's most famous sacred treasures occupy an unusual place in [Montenegro]({{ 'where-montenegro-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s cultural landscape because they are neither ordinary museum exhibits nor purely devotional objects. They are relics that continue to attract pilgrims while also raising difficult questions about ownership, provenance and national identity. Visitors encounter objects linked by tradition to Saint John the Baptist, the True Cross and the Byzantine icon known as Our Lady of Philermos, yet these treasures are divided between a working monastery and a state museum. Their stories stretch from Jerusalem and Constantinople to Rhodes, Malta, imperial Russia and the Balkans, making Cetinje one of Europe's most remarkable repositories of travelling Christian relics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cetinje Historic Core</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCetinje Historic Core - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2010</span></span></span>

For travellers interested in unusual collections rather than conventional museums, Cetinje offers something distinctive. The fascination lies not only in the relics themselves but also in the unresolved questions surrounding who should hold them, how they should be displayed and whether they belong primarily to religious communities, the Montenegrin state or institutions elsewhere that regard them as part of their own heritage.

## The Relics Kept at Cetinje Monastery

The Cetinje Monastery remains an active Orthodox monastery rather than a museum, and this shapes the visitor experience. Access to its treasury is governed first by religious practice and only second by tourism. The relics are treated as sacred objects for veneration, not simply as historical artefacts behind glass.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetinje_Monastery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cetinje Monastery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cetinje Monastery</span></span></span>

Among the monastery's best-known treasures are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The Right Hand of Saint John the Baptist, traditionally believed to be the hand used to baptise Jesus.
* A fragment traditionally identified as part of the True Cross.
* The incorrupt relics of Saint Peter of Cetinje, Montenegro's most important Orthodox saint and ruler.
* Historic ecclesiastical objects associated with the Petrović-Njegoš dynasty and the monastery's centuries-long role as the country's spiritual centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetinje_Monastery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cetinje Monastery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cetinje Monastery</span></span></span>

</div>

From the perspective of curiosity culture, these relics are unusual because they remain embedded in living religious practice. Visitors do not encounter explanatory labels presenting detached historical interpretation in the style of a secular museum. Instead, they enter a place where faith and history continue to coexist, making it difficult to separate devotional meaning from cultural heritage.

The provenance of the relics is itself extraordinary. The Right Hand of Saint John the Baptist is said to have travelled through Jerusalem, Constantinople, the Knights Hospitaller, imperial Russia and the Yugoslav royal family before arriving in Montenegro during the upheavals of the twentieth century. While the broad outline of this journey is well documented, historians note that some earlier stages rely heavily on religious tradition rather than continuous documentary evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orderofmalta.int/about-the-order-of-malta/the-icon-of-our-lady-of-philermos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orderofmalta.int">[Sovereign Military Order of Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orderofmalta.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sovereign Military Order of Malta The icon of Our Lady of Philermos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sovereign Military Order of MaltaThe icon of Our Lady of Philermos - Sovereign Military Order of Malta...</span></span></span>

## The Journey of Our Lady of Philermos

The most internationally famous object connected with Cetinje is the icon of Our Lady of Philermos. Unlike the monastery relics, it is preserved in the National Museum of Montenegro rather than inside the monastery treasury. This physical separation reflects modern decisions about conservation and public custody as much as religious practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cetinje Historic Core</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCetinje Historic Core - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2010</span></span></span>

The icon's journey is almost without parallel among surviving Christian devotional images.

According to historical tradition, it originated in the Byzantine world and became associated with the monastery on Mount Phileremos on Rhodes. When the Knights Hospitaller captured Rhodes in the early fourteenth century, the icon became their most revered Marian image. After the Ottoman conquest of Rhodes in 1522, the Knights carried it into exile before eventually establishing it in Malta, where it remained central to the Order's identity for centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orderofmalta.int/about-the-order-of-malta/the-icon-of-our-lady-of-philermos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orderofmalta.int">[orderofmalta.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orderofmalta.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sovereign Military Order of Malta The icon of Our Lady of Philermos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sovereign Military Order of MaltaThe icon of Our Lady of Philermos - Sovereign Military Order of Malta...</span></span></span>

Following Napoleon's occupation of Malta, the relics associated with the Knights—including the Philermos icon, the Right Hand of Saint John the Baptist and a fragment of the True Cross—entered the protection of Tsar Paul I of Russia. Revolutionary upheaval in the twentieth century scattered these treasures once again. During the Second World War they eventually reached Montenegro, where they remained largely hidden from international attention for decades before their confirmed public rediscovery in the late twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orderofmalta.int/about-the-order-of-malta/the-icon-of-our-lady-of-philermos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orderofmalta.int">[Sovereign Military Order of Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orderofmalta.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sovereign Military Order of Malta The icon of Our Lady of Philermos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sovereign Military Order of MaltaThe icon of Our Lady of Philermos - Sovereign Military Order of Malta...</span></span></span>

Modern scholarship is careful to distinguish between tradition and evidence. Claims that the icon was painted by Saint Luke belong to longstanding Christian tradition rather than verifiable historical fact. Art historians generally date the surviving panel to roughly the eleventh or twelfth century while recognising its exceptional religious importance regardless of its precise origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/handle/123456789/132658" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[L-Università ta&#x27; Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.mt.</span></span></span>

## Faith, Provenance and Competing Claims

The custody of Cetinje's sacred treasures illustrates how a single collection can simultaneously represent religious devotion, national heritage and international historical memory.

For the Serbian Orthodox Church, the monastery relics remain active sacred objects serving liturgical and devotional purposes. For the Montenegrin state, they are also part of the country's cultural patrimony and an important component of Cetinje's historic identity. UNESCO's documentation of Cetinje similarly identifies these relics as defining elements of the city's outstanding cultural significance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cetinje Historic Core</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCetinje Historic Core - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2010</span></span></span>

The icon of Our Lady of Philermos adds another layer of complexity because the Sovereign Military Order of Malta continues to regard it as the Order's most sacred historical icon. While the Order acknowledges that the original remains in Montenegro, it also preserves copies, organises pilgrimages connected with its history and maintains a strong spiritual association with the image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orderofmalta.int/about-the-order-of-malta/the-icon-of-our-lady-of-philermos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orderofmalta.int">[Sovereign Military Order of Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orderofmalta.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sovereign Military Order of Malta The icon of Our Lady of Philermos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sovereign Military Order of MaltaThe icon of Our Lady of Philermos - Sovereign Military Order of Malta...</span></span></span>

This has produced an unusual form of shared heritage rather than an active international ownership dispute in the legal sense. ## Why These Treasures Matter Beyond Religion

For readers exploring Montenegro's unusual collections, Cetinje demonstrates that the country's strangest heritage is not necessarily bizarre in appearance. Its uniqueness lies in the biographies of the objects.

Few places contain relics whose documented journeys connect Jerusalem, Byzantium, Rhodes, Malta, St Petersburg, Yugoslavia and modern Montenegro. Even fewer divide those treasures between a functioning monastery and a national museum while allowing them to remain simultaneously objects of scholarship, pilgrimage and state heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cetinje Historic Core</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCetinje Historic Core - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2010</span></span></span>

Visitors should therefore approach Cetinje with different expectations from those appropriate to an eccentric curiosity museum. Photography may be restricted, religious services take priority over sightseeing, and some of the most important stories concern provenance rather than spectacle. The enduring fascination comes from the way these relics blur the boundaries between museum collection, sacred treasury, diplomatic history and contested cultural memory, making them one of Montenegro's most distinctive and thought-provoking heritage experiences.
