## Introduction

The Ishango Bone is one of the Democratic Republic of the Congo's most remarkable archaeological discoveries and one of the world's most debated prehistoric objects. Found near Lake Edward at the archaeological site of Ishango, the small carved bone has become famous because its carefully grouped notches may represent one of humanity's earliest attempts to record numbers or recognise patterns. Yet despite decades of study, no single explanation has won universal acceptance. Rather than proving that prehistoric people invented advanced mathematics, the bone demonstrates something equally fascinating: that people living around 20,000 years ago deliberately engraved information in ways that still challenge modern interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>

For visitors interested in unusual archaeological treasures, the Ishango Bone occupies a unique place in the [DR Congo]({{ 'what-makes-the-dr-congos-collections-so/' | relative_url }})'s heritage. It is simultaneously a scientific artefact, a cultural symbol and an enduring mystery whose meaning remains unresolved.

## What the notches and quartz point look like

The artefact itself is surprisingly modest. It is about 10 centimetres long and consists of a dark fossilised bone with a small piece of quartz fixed into one end, giving it the appearance of a handle fitted with a sharp point. Archaeologists believe the quartz could have been used for engraving, scraping or other practical work rather than existing purely as decoration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>

Its real intrigue lies in the incised marks running down the shaft. These are arranged in three separate columns, with the cuts grouped into distinct clusters rather than carved randomly. Some groups appear to double earlier groups—for example three beside six, four beside eight and five beside ten—while other sequences have inspired mathematical interpretations involving addition or number patterns. Because the marks are organised rather than evenly spaced, most researchers agree they were intentional, even if their precise purpose remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>

The Ishango excavation produced many other archaeological finds, including harpoon points, quartz tools and additional bones bearing notches. This wider context suggests that making marked bones was not unique to a single object, although none has attracted the same attention as the famous specimen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ishango.naturalsciences.be/en/en-ishango-13.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ishango.naturalsciences.be">[ishango.naturalsciences.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ishango.naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalsciences.be.</span></span></span>

## The main mathematical and lunar interpretations

Since its discovery by Belgian geologist Jean de Heinzelin during excavations in the 1950s, several competing explanations have been proposed. None has been conclusively demonstrated, and each explains some features better than others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ishango.naturalsciences.be/en/en-ishango-09.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ishango.naturalsciences.be">[ishango.naturalsciences.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ishango.naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalsciences.be.</span></span></span>

The main interpretations include:

* **A tally stick.** Many archaeologists regard this as the safest explanation. The notches may simply record quantities—perhaps animals, fish, days or traded goods. Similar tally marks appear on prehistoric objects from many parts of the world, making this the least speculative interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ishango.naturalsciences.be/en/en-ishango-19.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ishango.naturalsciences.be">[ishango.naturalsciences.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ishango.naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalsciences.be.</span></span></span>
* **A mathematical tool.** De Heinzelin himself suggested that some notch groupings hinted at arithmetic, including doubling and numerical relationships. Later researchers expanded this idea, proposing that the bone may demonstrate early addition, subtraction or structured counting rather than simple record keeping.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>
* **Prime numbers or advanced arithmetic.** One influential interpretation argued that one column represents prime numbers between 10 and 20, leading to popular descriptions of the bone as the world's oldest mathematical instrument. While this idea captured public imagination, many specialists consider it difficult to prove because alternative explanations fit the same markings and because it assumes mathematical concepts that leave no independent archaeological evidence from the same period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/Ancient-Africa/ishango.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: math.buffalo.edu">[math.buffalo.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">math.buffalo.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an old Mathematical Object</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">an old Mathematical Object</span></span></span>
* **A base-12 calculating aid.** More recent mathematical studies have suggested the grouped notches could reflect counting based on twelve, using three and four as subsidiary units. Advocates compare the object to a primitive calculating device rather than a modern calculator. This remains an interesting but debated hypothesis rather than an accepted conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.1019" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does the Ishango Bone Indicate Knowledge of the Base 12? An Interpretation of a Prehistoric Discovery, the First Mathematical Tool o...</span></span></span>
* **A lunar calendar.** Archaeologist Alexander Marshack proposed that microscopic examination showed the marks recorded phases of the Moon over several months. This interpretation has also been widely discussed, especially in popular books, but many archaeologists argue that the evidence does not uniquely support a lunar record over other forms of notation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishango_bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ishango bone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ishango bone</span></span></span>

## Why no single explanation has prevailed

The continuing debate exists because the Ishango Bone lacks the context that would make its purpose obvious. There is no accompanying inscription, no comparable object with an agreed meaning and no written tradition from which to interpret the markings.

Several practical problems limit certainty:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The marks are open to more than one numerical grouping.
* Different researchers count and interpret some notches differently.
* Similar prehistoric notched bones elsewhere served a variety of purposes.
* The object's practical quartz point suggests it was also a working tool, making it possible that the markings were only one aspect of its use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ishango.naturalsciences.be/en/en-ishango-18.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ishango.naturalsciences.be">[ishango.naturalsciences.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ishango.naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalsciences.be.</span></span></span>

</div>

As a result, most archaeologists distinguish between what is well supported and what remains speculative. It is widely accepted that the notches were deliberately organised. It is not accepted that they definitively prove knowledge of prime numbers, a calendar, or sophisticated arithmetic.

## Why the original remains in Belgium

The original Ishango Bone is housed at the Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences in Brussels. It was taken there after its discovery by Jean de Heinzelin, who worked for the institute during the Belgian colonial period, and it has remained in its collections ever since.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>

Its location also reflects a broader issue affecting Congolese cultural heritage. Many important archaeological and ethnographic objects collected during the colonial era are preserved in European museums rather than in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The Ishango Bone has therefore become part of wider discussions about colonial collecting, museum stewardship and the future return of African cultural heritage.

For visitors to Brussels, the institute has exhibited the bone and developed educational material explaining both its archaeological discovery and the competing theories surrounding its meaning. The wider Ishango archaeological collection, including thousands of excavated artefacts, is likewise preserved there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>

## Why the Ishango Bone still matters

The enduring importance of the Ishango Bone is not that it conclusively rewrites the history of mathematics, but that it demonstrates the intellectual sophistication of prehistoric communities living in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Whether the notches recorded counts, lunar observations or numerical relationships, they reveal deliberate symbolic behaviour long before writing emerged.

Within the DR Congo's landscape of unusual heritage, the Ishango Bone stands apart because its mystery has survived every attempt at explanation. Unlike many archaeological puzzles that eventually receive a broadly accepted interpretation, this small carved bone continues to invite fresh analysis while reminding researchers that prehistoric evidence often preserves questions better than answers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[naturalsciences.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>
