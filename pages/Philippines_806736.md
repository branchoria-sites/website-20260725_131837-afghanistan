## Introduction

The strongest examples include the Kabayan burial caves and site museum in Benguet, where Indigenous mummified remains are treated as ancestors rather than mere archaeological specimens; the University of Santo Tomas Museum, whose natural-history collections retain the atmosphere of an old teaching cabinet; the Marikina Shoe Museum, where local craft history competes with the notoriety of Imelda Marcos’s footwear; and the family-run Cata-al war museum in Negros Oriental. Together, they show that strange collections in the Philippines are usually rooted in living community memory, colonial history, religion or private collecting rather than in staged paranormal entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-museums/regional-area-and-site-museums/kabayan-burial-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalmuseum.gov.ph">[nationalmuseum.gov.ph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalmuseum.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Also on exhibit are ethnographic objects, mostly of the Ibaloy ethnolinguistics group and some...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Where the Philippines’ strangest collections are found

The country’s unusual heritage is geographically dispersed. Metro Manila contains old institutional collections and specialist museums, while the Cordillera highlands preserve mortuary traditions that cannot be separated from Indigenous authority and sacred landscapes. Provincial museums frequently occupy unexpected buildings, including prisons, water tanks and private homes. This makes the Philippine scene less like a neat circuit of “weird museums” and more like a network of objects whose meanings change according to who owns, displays or venerates them.

## The Kabayan mummies: ancestors, not horror exhibits

The Kabayan Burial Caves in Benguet are the country’s most important uncanny heritage complex. Mummified human remains survive in caves and rock shelters associated with the Ibaloi people, while the National Museum’s Kabayan site museum displays one mummy alongside ethnographic material from Ibaloi and Kalanguya communities. The burial caves were declared National Cultural Treasures in 1973 and are managed as both archaeological heritage and a continuing Indigenous cultural landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-museums/regional-area-and-site-museums/kabayan-burial-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalmuseum.gov.ph">[nationalmuseum.gov.ph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalmuseum.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Also on exhibit are ethnographic objects, mostly of the Ibaloy ethnolinguistics group and some...Read more...</span></span></span>

Their popular nickname, “fire mummies”, refers to preservation methods involving prolonged drying and exposure to smoke. Oral accounts and scientific interpretations have not always agreed on every stage of the process, including the precise preservatives used or the dates when the custom flourished. Researchers have therefore treated sweeping claims about the mummies’ great age with caution. The safest interpretation is that deliberate mummification formed part of a socially important mortuary tradition in the Cordillera, rather than a mysterious lost technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/361561/origins-of-benguet-mummification" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newsinfo.inquirer.net">[Inquirer.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newsinfo.inquirer.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">origins of benguet mummification</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">of Benguet mummification - News - Inquirer.netFebruary 19, 2013 — 19 Feb 2013 — Scientists from the National Museum sustain oral accounts...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 19, 2013</span></span></span>

For visitors, the important point is that these are not horror props. Recent conservation work describes the remains as enduring ancestors who retain spiritual and ritual importance for Ibaloi communities. Conservation involves environmental monitoring, physical protection and consultation with elders, not simply maintaining a visually dramatic exhibit. Some burial locations are restricted or kept discreet because of deterioration, theft and earlier looting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVIII-M-9-2025/1281/2025/isprs-archives-XLVIII-M-9-2025-1281-2025.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isprs-archives.copernicus.org">[copernicus.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isprs-archives.copernicus.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isprs archives XLVIII M</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISPRS ArchivesPreventive Conservation of the Kabayan Mummiesby FDP Reyes · 2025 — The Kabayan mummies, or meking, represent the ancestral...</span></span></span>

The history of Apo Anno, a tattooed mummified ancestor, shows why display and ownership are sensitive. After being removed from his burial place, the body passed through exhibition and private hands before eventually being returned for reburial under protective arrangements. The case transforms the visitor’s question from “How was this body preserved?” to “Who has the right to display an ancestor?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wmf.org/projects/kabayan-mummy-caves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wmf.org">[World Monuments Fund]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wmf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wmf.org.</span></span></span>

A separate Ibaloi mummy is displayed at the Baguio Museum among wider Cordillera archaeological and ethnographic collections. Photography of the remains is prohibited, reinforcing the distinction between respectful viewing and souvenir hunting. The museum is visitor-facing, but the mummy should be approached as a culturally sensitive human presence rather than the equivalent of a fairground oddity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/ibaloi-mummy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Ibaloi Fire Mummy in Baguio</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Ibaloi Fire Mummy in Baguio</span></span></span>

## An old-fashioned cabinet inside a university

The University of Santo Tomas Museum in Manila offers a different form of strangeness: the survival of the encyclopaedic university cabinet. Formally established in 1869, it grew from the requirement that major colleges maintain natural-history teaching collections. Its holdings combine zoological specimens, ethnographic objects, coins, medals, religious images, Asian decorative arts and paintings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ustmuseum.ust.edu.ph/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ustmuseum.ust.edu.ph">[UST Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ustmuseum.ust.edu.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UST MuseumUST Museum - University of Santo TomasMost of the collection dates back to 1869. The University of Santo Tomas Museum, consider...</span></span></span>

This mixture gives the museum the texture of an older world in which art, anatomy, mineralogy, ethnography and sacred objects were not always separated into specialised institutions. The curiosities are academically grounded rather than paranormal, but the experience can resemble a historic cabinet of wonders: preserved animals and scientific specimens share an institutional story with devotional sculpture, university regalia and papal memorabilia.

Its credibility is comparatively strong because the collection belongs to a long-established university and developed partly for teaching. That does not make every old label or classification timeless. Like other colonial-era natural-history and ethnographic collections, its objects emerged from systems that categorised nature and cultures through European academic frameworks. What makes it valuable today is precisely this double role: it preserves specimens and artworks while also revealing how knowledge was assembled in the Spanish colonial Philippines.

## The Marikina Shoe Museum and a disputed political symbol

The Marikina Shoe Museum is often described simply as the home of Imelda Marcos’s shoes. That is accurate but incomplete. The institution was created to document Marikina’s footwear industry, local craftsmanship and the city’s identity as a centre of shoemaking. Its displays also include footwear associated with politicians, public figures and foreign cultures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pia.gov.ph/news/ph-first-ladies-grace-renowned-marikina-shoe-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pia.gov.ph">[Philippine Information Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pia.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ph first ladies grace renowned marikina shoe museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Philippine Information AgencyPH First Ladies grace renowned Marikina Shoe Museum10 Jul 2024 — Founded in 2001, the Marikina Shoe Museum i...</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, the Marcos shoes dominate public interest. More than 700 pairs from the former first lady’s collection were placed on display when the museum opened in 2001, turning an everyday object into evidence of political excess, celebrity image-making and national memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wspartners.bbc.com/episode/w3ct5yk0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wspartners.bbc.com">[BBC Partners]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wspartners.bbc.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Partners Imelda Marcos&#x27;s famous shoe collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Partners Imelda Marcos&#x27;s famous shoe collection</span></span></span>

This creates an interpretive problem. Displayed without enough historical explanation, the shoes can become glamorous curiosities detached from the dictatorship, corruption allegations and economic inequality with which they are associated. Framed critically, however, they can show how material objects acquire political power. A shoe is simultaneously a product of Marikina craftsmanship, a personal fashion item, a confiscated or transferred state-held object and an international shorthand for conspicuous consumption.

Visitors should therefore expect a specialist local-history museum with one overwhelmingly famous subsection, not a comprehensive museum of the Marcos period. Its strangeness lies less in the number of shoes than in the unstable meaning of the collection: industrial heritage and political spectacle occupy the same rooms.

## Private obsessions made public

### Cata-al’s battlefield collection

The Cata-al World War II Museum in Valencia, Negros Oriental, is one of the clearest Philippine examples of a private collection becoming a public attraction. Built by a father-and-son collecting effort, it contains weapons, helmets, shells, bombs, equipment and other wartime material reportedly recovered from areas connected with fighting on Negros. It is family-run rather than a large state institution, and visits have traditionally depended on donations or modest local arrangements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dumaguete.com/valencia-negros-oriental/cata-al-memorial-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dumaguete.com">[dumaguete.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dumaguete.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cata-al Memorial Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cata-al Memorial Museum</span></span></span>

Its appeal comes from density and proximity. Objects that would be carefully isolated in display cases at a national military museum can appear as an accumulated landscape of rusted machinery and battlefield debris. Personal narration adds immediacy, particularly when the collectors connect an object to a local discovery site or remembered wartime story.

That intimacy also affects how the collection should be read. A privately assembled trove may preserve objects that would otherwise have been scrapped, but provenance, identification and conservation can vary. Visitors should distinguish between an object’s physical authenticity and the certainty of the story attached to it. A genuine Japanese helmet, for example, does not automatically prove every claimed detail about where it was found or who wore it. The museum is best understood as local memory collected from the ground up, not as the final scholarly authority on the Pacific War.

### Christmas every day at Casa Santa

Casa Santa Museum in Antipolo turns a private festive collection into an immersive commercial attraction. The collection began in 1994, opened to visitors in 2004 and has expanded to more than 3,700 Santa Claus and Christmas-related objects from different countries. The operator openly describes the process by which a personal hobby developed into a business.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://casasantamuseum.com/about-us/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: casasantamuseum.com">[Casa Santa Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">casasantamuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Casa Santa Museum About Us &#124; Tours &#124; Casa Santa Museum Antipolo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Casa Santa Museum About Us &#124; Tours &#124; Casa Santa Museum Antipolo</span></span></span>

Unlike the mummies or war relics, Casa Santa does not trade on death, trauma or contested archaeology. Its oddity comes from scale and repetition: one seasonal figure multiplied across rooms, styles and national traditions. Additional staged environments, including a toy-factory section, make it closer to an attraction built around a collection than a conventional research museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://casasantamuseum.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: casasantamuseum.com">[Casa Santa Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">casasantamuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on casasantamuseum.com.</span></span></span>

Its curation is primarily family-oriented and experiential. Visitors should expect decorative abundance, themed photography and festive storytelling rather than a critical history of Saint Nicholas, consumer culture or the global Christmas industry. Even so, it is a useful example of how collecting becomes tourism: an ordinary household enthusiasm crosses a threshold where quantity, display design and ticketed access turn it into a public curiosity.

## Prisons and unlikely museum buildings

Museo Sugbo in Cebu City occupies the former provincial prison known historically as Cárcel de Cebú. Constructed during the Spanish colonial period and used for various forms of incarceration into the late twentieth century, the complex became a provincial museum after prisoners were transferred elsewhere. It reopened to the public in August 2025 following a period of closure and redevelopment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_Sugbo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museo Sugbo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo Sugbo</span></span></span>

The site is unusual because the building itself is the strongest exhibit. Coral-stone walls, cells and enclosed courtyards shape how visitors encounter galleries on Cebu’s archaeology, politics, colonial experience and wartime history. Unlike a purpose-built museum, the former gaol carries traces of confinement that cannot be neutralised by display panels.

Its transformation also raises a common question about prison museums: does the interpretation give sufficient attention to prisoners, punishment and state violence, or does the dramatic architecture merely provide atmosphere? Museo Sugbo’s wide historical remit means that it is not solely a museum of imprisonment. Visitors seeking a detailed social history of incarceration may therefore find the building more evocative than the collection. Even so, its reuse places colonial government, revolution, Japanese occupation and post-war provincial power inside a setting where authority was physically enforced.

Ang Panublion Museum in Roxas City offers a gentler but equally striking example of adaptive reuse. It occupies a circular structure built as a municipal water tank in 1910. The museum now preserves local historical and cultural material, converting a piece of civic infrastructure into what its name describes as a guardian of precious things. ## Folklore, monsters and the museum that is missing

Philippine folklore is rich in shape-shifters, witches, ghosts, giants and other supernatural beings, yet the country has relatively few permanent, professionally curated museums devoted specifically to them. This absence is notable because places such as Capiz and Siquijor are already marketed through associations with monsters, sorcery, folk healing and spiritual practice.

Capiz has long been stereotyped as the home of a feared shape-shifting creature in Philippine folklore. Modern tourism repeatedly uses, disputes or jokes about that reputation, but Roxas City’s main heritage museum is a local-history institution rather than a dedicated monster museum. The continued controversy over monster imagery in public celebrations shows that folklore branding is not culturally neutral: some residents see commercial potential, while others see an insulting provincial stereotype.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aswangproject.com/aswang-in-capiz/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aswangproject.com">[aswangproject.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aswangproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">THE ASWANG PROJECTThe truth about the ASWANG in Capiz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">THE ASWANG PROJECTThe truth about the ASWANG in Capiz</span></span></span>

Siquijor presents a similar tension. The island is associated in popular travel culture with healers, herbal practice and magic, but these traditions generally appear through festivals, guided experiences, private consultations and tourism narratives rather than through a major collection-based museum. Treating every healer as an occult performer would flatten distinct practices into a visitor-friendly fantasy.

The lack of a formal folklore museum also affects credibility. Stories are often transmitted through tourism articles, television features, social media and oral accounts, where legend and reported fact easily merge. A well-curated institution could document regional variations, historic sources, colonial demonisation, healing traditions and modern popular culture without declaring supernatural claims true. Until such a museum exists, visitors should treat monster and witchcraft tourism as a living field of storytelling and identity politics, not as a stable collection of verified paranormal evidence.

## How to judge an unusual Philippine collection

The most memorable Philippine curiosity sites require different standards of interpretation. A practical assessment begins with four questions.

**Who controls the objects?** National and university museums generally have formal conservation systems, while private collections may depend heavily on one family’s knowledge and resources. Indigenous ancestral remains require an additional question: whether descendant communities participate in decisions about access, display and care.

**What kind of claim is being made?** “This object was recovered on Negros” is a provenance claim. “This object belonged to a named soldier” is a more specific and harder claim. “This province is inhabited by supernatural creatures” belongs to folklore or tourism unless supported as a study of belief rather than literal evidence.

**Is the setting educational, devotional or commercial?** Casa Santa is openly entertainment-led, while Kabayan is a protected ancestral landscape. The Marikina Shoe Museum combines civic heritage with political memory. Confusing these categories can make a commercial attraction appear scholarly or make a sacred site seem like themed entertainment.

**What is absent from the label?** Political objects need political context; battlefield relics need provenance; human remains need ethical interpretation; colonial collections need explanation of how objects were acquired and classified. The most responsible visit is not the one that rejects wonder, but the one that asks what kind of wonder is being produced.

## Why these collections matter

The Philippines’ strangest collections are compelling because they resist a single definition of “weird”. The [Kabayan mummies]({{ 'kabayan-mummies/' | relative_url }}) are extraordinary, but their deepest meaning lies in ancestry, conservation and cultural authority. The University of Santo Tomas preserves the encyclopaedic habits of an old teaching museum. Marikina’s shoes turn fashion into an argument about craft, power and memory. Cata-al’s weapons show how a private search for wartime remnants can become a community archive, while Casa Santa demonstrates how collecting enthusiasm can be commercialised without pretending to be academic.

The country’s curiosity culture is therefore strongest where objects sit between categories: sacred remains that are also protected heritage, prison architecture used for civic education, household collections transformed into attractions, and folklore that generates tourism without settling into a formal museum. These places matter not because they prove supernatural mysteries, but because they reveal what Filipinos have chosen to preserve, what outsiders have labelled strange, and how display can turn an ancestor, shoe, shell casing or festive ornament into a contested piece of public history.
