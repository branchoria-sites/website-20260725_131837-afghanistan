## Introduction

The Museum of Old and New Art (MONA), near Hobart in Tasmania, is not a cabinet of curiosities in the historical sense, yet it deliberately recreates many of the feelings that made Renaissance and Enlightenment curiosity cabinets so compelling. Instead of arranging objects by period, civilisation or artistic style, MONA places Egyptian antiquities beside provocative contemporary installations, scientific-looking machines beside ritual objects, and works about death, sex, belief and bodily functions in the same underground spaces. The result is a museum that encourages visitors to make their own connections rather than follow an official narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mona.net.au/?cmp=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mona.net.au">[mona.net.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mona.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mona Mona – Museum of Old and New Art &#124; Hobart, Tasmania</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mona Mona – Museum of Old and New Art &#124; Hobart, Tasmania</span></span></span>

Within [Australia]({{ 'where-australia-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s landscape of unusual collections, MONA occupies a distinctive position. It is neither a medical museum nor a folklore archive, but a deliberately unsettling environment where collecting itself becomes part of the exhibition. Its approach echoes the old *Wunderkammer*, or cabinet of curiosities, while questioning how museums create authority, classify knowledge and decide what deserves cultural value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://recollections.nma.gov.au/issues/volume_9_number_2/papers/run_david_run" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: recollections.nma.gov.au">[recollections.nma.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">recollections.nma.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">re Collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Run David run...</span></span></span>

## Why MONA feels like a cabinet of curiosities

Early cabinets of curiosities gathered rare natural specimens, antiquities, scientific instruments and exotic objects into rooms designed to provoke wonder rather than deliver systematic education. Visitors were expected to compare, speculate and argue about relationships between objects.

MONA adopts this same intellectual strategy using contemporary museum design. Founder David Walsh assembled a collection that mixes ancient artefacts with modern artworks without presenting a conventional chronological journey. Visitors descend into dark subterranean galleries where artworks emerge unexpectedly from tunnels and chambers, with interpretation delivered primarily through the museum's digital guide rather than wall labels. The absence of traditional explanatory panels means visitors must actively construct meaning instead of simply accepting institutional authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Old_and_New_Art" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum of Old and New Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum of Old and New Art</span></span></span>

Rather than celebrating encyclopaedic knowledge, MONA embraces uncertainty. Themes of mortality, desire, religion, gambling, violence and bodily existence recur across thousands of years, making ancient funerary objects appear surprisingly contemporary while modern conceptual art can resemble archaeological relics removed from their original context. This deliberate collapse of historical categories gives the museum much of its cabinet-of-curiosities atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://recollections.nma.gov.au/issues/volume_9_number_2/papers/run_david_run" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: recollections.nma.gov.au">[recollections.nma.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">recollections.nma.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">re Collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Run David run...</span></span></span>

## How MONA mixes old and new objects

The museum's title—Museum of Old and New Art—describes its organising principle. Ancient Egyptian antiquities, classical coins and archaeological objects share space with ambitious contemporary installations from internationally recognised artists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Old_and_New_Art" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum of Old and New Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum of Old and New Art</span></span></span>

The effect is less about historical comparison than about recurring human obsessions. Across different galleries, visitors encounter:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Ancient funerary objects alongside artworks exploring death and memory.
* Religious imagery beside works questioning belief, ritual and morality.
* Scientific-looking installations beside deliberately irrational or emotional works.
* Objects associated with civilisation displayed alongside works centred on bodily waste, violence or decay.

</div>

Because the objects are not arranged as evidence of historical progress, no single interpretation dominates. Ancient artefacts become contemporary again, while contemporary art acquires the aura of mysterious relics. The collection therefore resembles a modern curiosity cabinet more than a conventional art museum, despite being carefully curated rather than randomly accumulated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Old_and_New_Art" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum of Old and New Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum of Old and New Art</span></span></span>

## The *Cloaca* machine and bodily spectacle

Perhaps no work captures MONA's philosophy better than Belgian artist Wim Delvoye's *Cloaca Professional*, commissioned specifically for the museum.

Suspended within its own purpose-built gallery, the installation functions as an artificial digestive system. Food is placed into the machine, moved through a sequence of chemically controlled chambers that imitate human digestion, and eventually emerges as material indistinguishable in appearance and smell from human faeces. Feeding sessions themselves become scheduled public events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wimdelvoye.be/work/cloaca/cloaca-professional/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wimdelvoye.be">[wimdelvoye.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wimdelvoye.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wim Delvoye Cloaca Professional • wimdelvoye.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wim Delvoye Cloaca Professional • wimdelvoye.be</span></span></span>

The machine deliberately confuses familiar categories.

From one perspective it resembles laboratory equipment, with pumps, tubes, vessels and controlled chemical reactions suggesting scientific precision. From another, it behaves like performance art. It also recalls the nineteenth-century fascination with scientific demonstration, where machinery itself became public entertainment.

The installation raises uncomfortable questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* If an object performs genuine biological chemistry, is it scientific equipment or sculpture?
* Does faithfully reproducing digestion produce knowledge, humour or simply spectacle?
* Why are museum visitors comfortable admiring ancient human remains yet often shocked by the biological processes shared by every living person?

</div>

Rather than answering these questions, *Cloaca* exposes how museums influence emotional responses through context. Identical equipment in a laboratory would be viewed differently from the same equipment presented inside an art museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unsw.edu.au/newsroom/news/2016/02/blending-science--art-and-other-excrement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unsw.edu.au">[edu.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unsw.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UNSW Sites Blending science, art and other excrement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNSW Sites Blending science, art and other excrement</span></span></span>

## Artistic provocation versus scientific authority

MONA repeatedly borrows the visual language of science without claiming scientific authority.

Several installations resemble laboratories, archives or research collections, encouraging visitors to assume they possess objective meaning. Yet the museum frequently undermines that expectation by pairing factual objects with symbolic, emotional or provocative works. This strategy makes visitors aware that museums do not simply display reality—they construct narratives through selection, arrangement and interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://recollections.nma.gov.au/issues/volume_9_number_2/papers/run_david_run" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: recollections.nma.gov.au">[nma.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">recollections.nma.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">re Collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Run David run...</span></span></span>

This distinguishes MONA from Australia's university pathology museums or natural-history collections. Institutions such as medical museums present specimens primarily to educate through documented scientific evidence. MONA instead uses similar visual cues to explore philosophical questions about knowledge, value and belief. The goal is reflection rather than instruction.

Even the museum's interpretive approach reinforces this distinction. By replacing conventional wall labels with digital interpretation that visitors choose to access—or ignore—MONA reduces the authoritative voice traditionally associated with museums. Visitors encounter uncertainty as part of the exhibition itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Old_and_New_Art" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum of Old and New Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum of Old and New Art</span></span></span>

## Why MONA matters within Australia's curiosity culture

Australia has relatively few institutions devoted entirely to occult collections, cryptids or cabinets of curiosities. MONA fills part of that cultural space without pretending to be a museum of mysteries or paranormal artefacts.

Instead, it demonstrates how the curiosity cabinet can be reinvented for the twenty-first century. The museum preserves genuine antiquities and internationally significant contemporary artworks, but their significance comes less from rarity than from the surprising conversations created between them. Ancient religion meets biotechnology; archaeology meets conceptual art; scientific imagery meets theatrical provocation.

For visitors interested in Australia's stranger cultural landscape, MONA is significant because it transforms the act of collecting into its central subject. Rather than asking only what individual objects mean, it asks why humans collect extraordinary things at all, why certain objects become museum pieces while others remain ordinary, and how institutions shape what societies consider worthy of wonder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://recollections.nma.gov.au/issues/volume_9_number_2/papers/run_david_run" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: recollections.nma.gov.au">[nma.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">recollections.nma.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">re Collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Run David run...</span></span></span>

      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
