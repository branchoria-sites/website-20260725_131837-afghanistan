## Introduction

The Kabayan mummies of Benguet are unusual not simply because they are naturally preserved human remains, but because they force a difficult question: should they be treated as museum exhibits, archaeological specimens or living ancestors within an Indigenous cultural tradition? The answer increasingly favoured by the Ibaloi community, the National Museum of the [Philippines]({{ 'where-the-philippines-keeps-its/' | relative_url }}) and many conservation specialists is that they are first and foremost ancestors. That principle shapes how the mummies are conserved, photographed, interpreted and, in many cases, deliberately kept away from mass tourism. Rather than presenting them as a macabre attraction, current practice aims to balance public education with respect for Indigenous authority, cultural continuity and the physical survival of the remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-museums/regional-area-and-site-museums/kabayan-burial-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalmuseum.gov.ph">[National Museum of the Philippines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalmuseum.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves – National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves – National Museum</span></span></span>

## Are the Kabayan Mummies Exhibits or Ancestors?

The Kabayan mummies occupy an unusual place within Philippine heritage. Although visitors can see a carefully interpreted example at the National Museum's Kabayan Site Museum, most of the mummified ancestors remain in burial caves and rock shelters spread across a sacred landscape rather than inside conventional museum galleries. This reflects an important distinction: the burial caves are not simply archaeological sites but part of an ancestral landscape that retains cultural meaning for local Ibaloi communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-museums/regional-area-and-site-museums/kabayan-burial-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalmuseum.gov.ph">[National Museum of the Philippines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalmuseum.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves – National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves – National Museum</span></span></span>

This approach differs from older museum traditions that often removed human remains from their cultural settings for scientific study or public display. In Kabayan, decades of looting and illegal removal demonstrated how easily ancestral remains could become collectible objects. The establishment of the site museum in the early 1980s followed community efforts to protect the caves after widespread theft, with local government and the National Museum collaborating to recover vulnerable mummies and provide secure storage where necessary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-museums/regional-area-and-site-museums/kabayan-burial-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalmuseum.gov.ph">[National Museum of the Philippines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalmuseum.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves – National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves – National Museum</span></span></span>

As a result, modern interpretation increasingly frames the mummies as members of an ancestral community rather than anonymous specimens. Visitors are encouraged to understand the people, beliefs and burial customs behind the preservation process instead of treating the remains as curiosities.

## How the Mummies Were Preserved

The preservation method itself contributes to the ethical debate because it demonstrates deliberate cultural practice rather than accidental preservation.

Historical accounts, archaeological research and Ibaloi oral traditions describe a lengthy preparation process in which selected individuals underwent careful drying before burial. Researchers generally agree that prolonged dehydration and exposure to smoke played major roles, although the exact sequence of techniques and substances used probably varied over time and remains the subject of scholarly discussion. Simplified tourist descriptions that present a single secret formula or "lost technology" are therefore misleading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVIII-M-9-2025/1281/2025/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isprs-archives.copernicus.org">[ISPRS Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isprs-archives.copernicus.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ISPRS Archives ISPRS-Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISPRS ArchivesISPRS-Archives - Preventive Conservation of the Kabayan Mummies: Surveying and Environmental Monitoring to Save the Mummies...</span></span></span>

Understanding the preservation process also changes how the bodies are viewed. They are not simply preserved corpses but the result of intentional mortuary rituals expressing status, memory and continuing relationships between the living and the dead. That cultural meaning explains why many descendants resist treating them as spectacle.

## Why Access and Photography Are Restricted

Many visitors expect archaeological sites to encourage photography, but Kabayan operates under a different ethical logic.

Restrictions on access and photography exist for several overlapping reasons:

* **Respect for ancestors.** For many local people, photographing human remains for entertainment or social media is incompatible with their status as revered ancestors rather than museum objects.
* **Physical conservation.** Increased visitor traffic affects fragile cave environments through changes in humidity, temperature, dust and accidental contact.
* **Security.** The burial caves have suffered repeated looting, making controlled access an important protection against theft and vandalism.
* **Community authority.** Decisions about access increasingly recognise the views of Indigenous custodians alongside those of archaeologists and government agencies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-museums/regional-area-and-site-museums/kabayan-burial-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalmuseum.gov.ph">[nationalmuseum.gov.ph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalmuseum.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves – National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of the Philippines Kabayan Burial Caves – National Museum</span></span></span>

These restrictions can surprise tourists accustomed to unrestricted photography at heritage sites. However, they reflect a broader international movement towards treating Indigenous ancestral remains with dignity rather than as unrestricted visual attractions.

The distinction is particularly important in the age of social media. Images shared without context can easily transform culturally significant ancestors into "creepy" internet content, reinforcing stereotypes that local communities and museum professionals have worked to replace with more respectful interpretation.

## Repatriation, Conservation and Indigenous Authority

The most important debate today is no longer whether the mummies are scientifically valuable—they clearly are—but who should make decisions about their care.

Recent conservation work has deliberately moved away from purely technical preservation towards collaborative management. Environmental monitoring projects have been carried out in partnership with Ibaloi elders, the National Museum of the Philippines and conservation specialists. Researchers have emphasised that building trust with the community is as important as measuring humidity or temperature because conservation decisions affect living cultural traditions, not simply archaeological material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVIII-M-9-2025/1281/2025/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isprs-archives.copernicus.org">[ISPRS Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isprs-archives.copernicus.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ISPRS Archives ISPRS-Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISPRS ArchivesISPRS-Archives - Preventive Conservation of the Kabayan Mummies: Surveying and Environmental Monitoring to Save the Mummies...</span></span></span>

Scientific monitoring has also shown why intervention must be carefully planned. The cave environments experience persistently high and fluctuating humidity, conditions that contribute to the deterioration of the mummies. Conservation therefore involves balancing natural cave conditions, visitor access and minimal intervention while respecting cultural protocols.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVIII-M-9-2025/1281/2025/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isprs-archives.copernicus.org">[ISPRS Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isprs-archives.copernicus.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ISPRS Archives ISPRS-Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISPRS ArchivesISPRS-Archives - Preventive Conservation of the Kabayan Mummies: Surveying and Environmental Monitoring to Save the Mummies...</span></span></span>

Questions of repatriation are more nuanced than they are for many overseas museum collections because most Kabayan mummies remain within their ancestral region. The ethical issue is less about returning remains from foreign institutions than about ensuring that Indigenous communities retain meaningful authority over how ancestors are stored, interpreted, conserved and displayed. This mirrors wider international museum practice, where increasing emphasis is placed on consultation with descendant communities before displaying or researching human remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nma.gov.au/about/publications/repatriation-handbook/chapter-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nma.gov.au">[National Museum of Australia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nma.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nma.gov.au.</span></span></span>

## What the Kabayan Mummies Teach About Heritage


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The Kabayan mummies illustrate a broader shift in how unusual collections are understood in the Philippines. Instead of asking whether they make an interesting attraction, heritage managers increasingly ask whether tourism serves the people whose ancestors are involved.

</div>

That perspective affects practical decisions about visitor numbers, interpretation, conservation priorities and photography policies. It also explains why the Kabayan Burial Caves feel fundamentally different from commercial mummy exhibitions elsewhere. Their purpose is not to maximise spectacle but to preserve both fragile human remains and an Indigenous cultural landscape whose significance continues into the present.

Within the Philippines' landscape of unusual heritage, the Kabayan mummies therefore stand apart. Their greatest importance lies not in their rarity as preserved bodies, but in the continuing insistence that they should be understood as ancestors whose dignity shapes every decision about whether, how and by whom they are seen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ejournals.ph/article.php?id=11189" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejournals.ph">[ejournals.ph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejournals.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ejournals.ph.</span></span></span>
