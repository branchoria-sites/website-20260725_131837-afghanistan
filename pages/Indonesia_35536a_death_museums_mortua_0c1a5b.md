## Introduction

[Indonesia]({{ 'where-indonesia-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s most distinctive death-related collections are not confined to conventional museums. They exist across three overlapping worlds: university research collections that study mortality, ancestral landscapes where the dead remain active members of family life, and public heritage sites that visitors can enter but which still function as places of remembrance. That combination makes Indonesia unusual. Rather than separating museums from ritual, many death-related collections sit at the boundary between scholarship, tourism, religion and continuing family obligation.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Indonesia_35536a_death_museums_mortua_0c1a5b-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Death illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Indonesia_35536a_death_museums_mortua_0c1a5b-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Indonesia_35536a_death_museums_mortua_0c1a5b-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For visitors interested in unusual museums, the country offers both a purpose-built museum dedicated to death and living mortuary traditions in which graves, effigies and even the bodies of ancestors remain part of everyday cultural practice. Understanding the difference between an educational display and an active ritual landscape is essential, because the ethical questions surrounding human remains, sacred objects and photography are often as important as the collections themselves.

## When death becomes a subject of study

### Inside Surabaya's university museum of death

Indonesia's closest equivalent to a dedicated death museum is the Ethnography Museum and Centre for Death Studies at Airlangga University in Surabaya. Established originally as an ethnographic museum and relaunched in 2016 with death as its central exhibition theme, it is widely described as Indonesia's first university museum devoted specifically to mortality and funerary culture. Its purpose is educational rather than sensational, bringing together anthropology, archaeology and physical anthropology to examine how different Indonesian communities understand death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.indonesia.travel/th/en/destination/java/east-java/museum-dan-pusat-kajian-etnografi-universitas-airlangga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indonesia.travel">[indonesia.travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indonesia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Indonesia Unair Museum &amp; Center for Ethnographic Studies Surabaya</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Indonesia Unair Museum &amp; Center for Ethnographic Studies Surabaya</span></span></span>

Instead of presenting death as horror, the museum explains how burial customs express ideas about family, religion, social status and the relationship between the living and the deceased. Exhibitions have included funerary equipment, grave models, skeletal material used for teaching, comparative displays of regional mortuary traditions and interpretations of how different ethnic groups mark death. The museum also functions as a research centre, supporting academic work in anthropology and forensic studies rather than operating solely as a public attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unair.ac.id/department-of-anthropology-pioneer-of-indonesian-physical-anthropology-studies/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unair.ac.id">[Universitas Airlangga Official Website]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unair.ac.id</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitas Airlangga Official WebsiteDepartment of Anthropology: Pioneer of Indonesian Physical Anthropology Studies - Universitas Airla...</span></span></span>

Its continuing academic role is significant. Staff regularly organise educational activities such as bone-identification workshops and public outreach, illustrating that the collection is intended to develop understanding of human biology, archaeology and cultural diversity rather than simply display unusual objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museoetno.fisip.unair.ac.id/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museoetno.fisip.unair.ac.id">[Museum Kematian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museoetno.fisip.unair.ac.id</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Kematian- Museum Kematian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum Kematian- Museum Kematian</span></span></span>

### More than a curiosity

The museum occupies an unusual position within Indonesia's museum landscape. It is simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* a university teaching collection;
* a research centre for anthropology and death studies;
* a public museum with visitor access; and
* an example of how museums can interpret mortality without relying on shock value.

</div>

Its exhibitions deliberately frame death as a universal human experience expressed through different cultural traditions rather than as something supernatural or macabre. That educational framing distinguishes it from commercial "museum of death" attractions found elsewhere in the world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.indonesia.travel/th/en/destination/java/east-java/museum-dan-pusat-kajian-etnografi-universitas-airlangga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indonesia.travel">[indonesia.travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indonesia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Indonesia Unair Museum &amp; Center for Ethnographic Studies Surabaya</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Indonesia Unair Museum &amp; Center for Ethnographic Studies Surabaya</span></span></span>

## Toraja graves, effigies and preserved ancestors

The most internationally recognised death-related heritage in Indonesia lies not inside a museum building but across the ritual landscape of Tana Toraja in South Sulawesi. Here, cliff burials, rock-cut tombs, family mausoleums and carved ancestral effigies attract visitors from around the world while remaining active places of family remembrance.

The carved wooden figures, often called ancestral effigies in English, are not decorative sculptures created for tourists. They represent particular deceased individuals and traditionally overlook burial sites from niches carved into limestone cliffs. Their purpose is commemorative and symbolic, maintaining a continuing relationship between ancestors and descendants rather than functioning as museum exhibits. Anthropologists generally interpret them as expressions of lineage, social identity and continuing kinship obligations rather than literal portraits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.indonesia.travel/th/en/destination/java/east-java/museum-dan-pusat-kajian-etnografi-universitas-airlangga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indonesia.travel">[Visit Indonesia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indonesia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Indonesia Unair Museum &amp; Center for Ethnographic Studies Surabaya</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Indonesia Unair Museum &amp; Center for Ethnographic Studies Surabaya</span></span></span>

Equally distinctive are the region's elaborate funerals. In many Toraja communities, death is understood as a gradual social transition rather than an immediate separation. Families may postpone final ceremonies while preparing the substantial resources required for ritual obligations. During this period the deceased may continue to be treated with remarkable familiarity within the household before the full funeral is held. These practices are frequently simplified in travel writing, but their primary meaning remains religious and familial rather than theatrical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://umac.icom.museum/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/UMACj-14-2-Prague-2022-v2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: umac.icom.museum">[UMAC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">umac.icom.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International committee for university muse...</span></span></span>

One of the best-known traditions is the periodic care of ancestors after burial. During certain ceremonies, relatives may clean graves, repair coffins and respectfully care for the remains of family members as part of continuing remembrance. Although widely photographed, these ceremonies are not performances staged for visitors. Their timing, participation and meaning are determined by families and local custom.

## Why these places are not simply open-air museums

Visitors sometimes describe Toraja as an "open-air museum of death", but that comparison is only partly accurate.

Unlike museum collections:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the graves remain family property;
* the effigies continue to have ritual significance;
* ceremonies are organised by descendants rather than heritage managers;
* access varies according to local custom and family permission.

</div>

Tourism has undoubtedly increased international visibility, yet the underlying social system has not disappeared. Heritage protection, local government, religious practice and tourism coexist, sometimes comfortably and sometimes with tension over commercialisation, photography and visitor behaviour.

For this reason, many scholars prefer to describe the region as a living cultural landscape rather than a museum. The distinction matters because visitors encounter places that remain spiritually meaningful to local communities rather than historical displays removed from everyday life.

## Ethics, authority and the display of human remains

Indonesia illustrates several different approaches to displaying the dead.

Within a university museum, skeletal remains and mortuary artefacts are interpreted through research, teaching and public education. Institutional oversight, academic ethics and documentation provide a framework for explaining why particular objects are displayed and how they contribute to knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholar.unair.ac.id/en/organisations/ethnography-museum-and-center-of-death-study/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholar.unair.ac.id">[Universitas Airlangga]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholar.unair.ac.id</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Universitas Airlangga Ethnography Museum and Center of Death Study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitas AirlanggaEthnography Museum and Center of Death Study - Universitas Airlangga...</span></span></span>

Within living ritual landscapes such as Toraja, however, authority usually rests with families, customary leaders and local communities rather than museum curators. Human remains are encountered because they remain embedded within continuing cultural practice.

These contrasting settings create different ethical expectations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Consent and community authority.** Museums increasingly recognise the importance of working with source communities when displaying human remains or culturally sensitive funerary material.
* **Photography.** Images that may seem appropriate in a museum can be inappropriate during active ceremonies or within sacred burial spaces.
* **Interpretation.** Tourist narratives sometimes exaggerate unusual customs while overlooking their religious and social meaning.
* **Respectful behaviour.** Visitors are entering places of remembrance, not theatrical attractions, even when access is permitted.

</div>

These issues mirror international debates about human remains in museums while taking on additional complexity because many Indonesian mortuary traditions continue to evolve rather than belonging solely to the past.

## What makes Indonesia distinctive

Many countries possess museums displaying mummies, skeletons or archaeological burials. Indonesia is distinctive because its most memorable death-related collections span both institutional and living contexts.

A visitor can move from an academic museum explaining funerary anthropology in Surabaya to ancestral landscapes where the dead remain woven into contemporary family life. Rather than presenting death as either scientific specimen or historical curiosity, Indonesia demonstrates how remembrance, research, tourism and ritual can exist simultaneously.

For anyone exploring the country's stranger museums and unusual heritage, these places reveal something deeper than macabre curiosity. They show how collections associated with death can become vehicles for education, identity and continuing relationships between the living and their ancestors, making them among the most culturally significant and ethically complex attractions in Indonesia.

    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
