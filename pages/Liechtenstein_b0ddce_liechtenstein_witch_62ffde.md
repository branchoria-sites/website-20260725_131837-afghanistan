## Introduction

[Liechtenstein]({{ 'where-liechtenstein-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }}) has no dedicated witch museum, despite one of the darkest histories of witch persecution in the German-speaking world. Between the mid-seventeenth century and 1682, the territories of Vaduz and Schellenberg—then separate lordships within the Holy Roman Empire rather than the modern Principality of Liechtenstein—experienced waves of prosecutions that led to around one hundred executions and devastated entire communities. The trials eventually became so notorious that intervention from the Holy Roman Emperor halted them after complaints about torture, unlawful procedures and abuse of power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_witch_trials" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liechtenstein witch trials</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein witch trials</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Liechtenstein_b0ddce_liechtenstein_witch_62ffde-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Witch Trials illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Liechtenstein_b0ddce_liechtenstein_witch_62ffde-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Liechtenstein_b0ddce_liechtenstein_witch_62ffde-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For visitors interested in unusual history, this absence is striking. Unlike parts of Germany, Switzerland or Scotland, where museums and memorials examine witch persecutions directly, Liechtenstein presents this chapter more quietly through broader historical interpretation rather than a specialist attraction. The result is an unusual kind of heritage: one of Europe's most severe regional witch hunts is remembered, but not through a dedicated museum.

## Why Does Liechtenstein Have No Witch Museum?

The lack of a witch museum does not reflect an absence of history. Instead, it reflects the country's scale and the way its museums are organised.

Liechtenstein has fewer than 40,000 inhabitants and a compact museum network centred on the Liechtenstein National Museum in Vaduz. Rather than maintaining separate institutions for every historical theme, the country generally integrates difficult subjects into broader exhibitions on political, social and cultural history. The National Museum's permanent displays focus on the long development of the principality from prehistoric settlement through modern statehood, leaving relatively little space for specialised museums devoted to individual historical episodes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/our-museums/nationalmuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liechtenstein National Museum &#124; Liechtenstein National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein National Museum &#124; Liechtenstein National Museum</span></span></span>

There is also a chronological complication. The worst persecutions occurred decades before the Principality of Liechtenstein was created in 1719. At the time, Vaduz and Schellenberg were independent territories ruled by the Counts of Hohenems. Modern Liechtenstein therefore commemorates atrocities that happened in the same geographical area but under a different political system, making the story part of regional rather than strictly national history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.nationalmuseum.ch/en/2024/08/how-the-principality-of-liechtenstein-came-into-being/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.nationalmuseum.ch">[Swiss History Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.nationalmuseum.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Swiss History BlogHow the Principality of Liechtenstein came into being – Swiss National Museum - Swiss history blogAugust 20, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 20, 2024</span></span></span>

Rather than creating a tourism attraction around the witch hunts, cultural institutions have generally treated them as one element within a wider story about governance, justice and the formation of the state.

## The Vaduz and Schellenberg persecutions

The territory experienced several outbreaks of witch prosecutions, but the campaign between 1679 and 1682 became by far the most destructive.

The trials spread through the County of Vaduz and the Lordship of Schellenberg, fuelled by denunciations extracted under torture. As in many major European witch hunts, accused people were pressured into naming supposed accomplices, creating expanding chains of accusation that engulfed families and neighbours. Both women and men were prosecuted, distinguishing the region from places where accusations fell overwhelmingly upon women. Around one hundred executions are generally accepted by historians, an extraordinarily high figure for such a small population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_witch_trials" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liechtenstein witch trials</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein witch trials</span></span></span>

Economic conditions worsened the crisis. The ruling Counts of Hohenems were heavily indebted, and confiscated property from convicted people could improve their finances. Modern historians caution against reducing the trials to simple financial motives, but the relationship between political instability, debt and confiscation remains an important part of explaining why prosecutions escalated so dramatically.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_witch_trials" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liechtenstein witch trials</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein witch trials</span></span></span>

Because the population was so small, the social damage was immense. Executions removed substantial numbers of adults from villages that depended on close family networks, while suspicion and forced testimony undermined trust throughout the communities.

## Torture, legal abuse and imperial intervention

What makes the Liechtenstein trials especially significant is not only their scale but also the way they ended.

Evidence was frequently obtained through torture, with confessions leading investigators towards ever larger lists of alleged accomplices. Such methods reflected broader European witch-hunting practices, where torture often produced expanding accusations rather than reliable evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/early-modern-witch-trials/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[The National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The National Archives Early Modern witch trials</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesEarly Modern witch trials - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

The turning point came when several people escaped the territory and appealed beyond the local authorities. Complaints reached Emperor Leopold I through intermediaries including clergy who challenged the legality of the proceedings. An imperial commission investigated the prosecutions and concluded that the trials had been conducted unlawfully. In 1682 the Emperor ordered the prosecutions to cease, making the episode an important example of imperial authority intervening to restrain abusive local justice within the Holy Roman Empire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_witch_trials" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liechtenstein witch trials</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein witch trials</span></span></span>

For legal historians, this intervention is as important as the executions themselves. It illustrates that even during Europe's witch-hunting era, some higher authorities regarded local procedures as illegitimate and were prepared to overturn them.

## How museums and folklore sites interpret the legacy

Visitors expecting reconstructed dungeons, theatrical witch exhibits or collections of supposed magical objects will not find them in Liechtenstein.

Instead, interpretation is restrained. The Liechtenstein National Museum presents the country's development through archaeology, political history and everyday life rather than through immersive displays about witchcraft. The persecutions appear as part of the wider historical narrative of the seventeenth century rather than as an independent attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/our-museums/nationalmuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liechtenstein National Museum &#124; Liechtenstein National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein National Museum &#124; Liechtenstein National Museum</span></span></span>

Elsewhere in the country, folklore survives in legends, Alpine traditions and local storytelling, particularly around mountain landscapes and supernatural beliefs. These traditions preserve aspects of popular imagination without attempting to recreate the judicial terror of the witch trials. This creates a clear distinction between folklore and historical persecution: one belongs to living cultural heritage, while the other is treated as a documented injustice.

Compared with neighbouring countries, the silence is noticeable. Germany, Switzerland and several Central European regions have developed memorials, museums or interpretation centres dedicated specifically to witch persecutions. Liechtenstein instead relies on historical scholarship and its general museums to preserve public memory, producing a quieter but arguably more integrated approach to this difficult past.

## Why the absence itself is historically interesting

For a project about strange museums and unusual heritage, Liechtenstein's most intriguing feature is arguably what is missing.

The country experienced one of the final large witch hunts in the Holy Roman Empire, yet it has chosen not to transform that history into a standalone visitor attraction. Instead, the emphasis falls on historical context, legal failure and state formation rather than spectacle.

That decision shapes how the witch trials are remembered. Visitors encounter them not as entertainment or dark tourism but as part of a broader story about how abuse of power, torture and weak legal safeguards affected a tiny Alpine society before the creation of the modern principality. The result is a form of museum silence that is itself revealing: the absence of a witch museum reflects contemporary priorities about remembrance, historical responsibility and the presentation of national identity.
