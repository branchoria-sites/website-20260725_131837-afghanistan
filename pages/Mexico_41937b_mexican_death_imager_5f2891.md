## Introduction

[Mexico]({{ 'where-mexico-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s best-known images of death are often misunderstood outside the country as simply spooky or Halloween-like. The National Museum of Death in Aguascalientes offers a very different interpretation. Rather than presenting skulls and skeletons as objects of fear, it explains how they have expressed memory, faith, humour, political criticism and artistic identity across more than five centuries of Mexican history. Housed and managed by the Autonomous University of Aguascalientes, the museum is one of the country's most distinctive cultural institutions because it treats death imagery as a changing visual language rather than a collection of macabre curiosities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx">[Museo Nacional de la Muerte]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uaa.mx.</span></span></span>

For visitors exploring Mexico's unusual museums, this approach is important. The galleries show that the same skull motif can mean remembrance in one context, satire in another, religious devotion elsewhere, and contemporary artistic experimentation in yet another. Understanding those shifts in meaning is the museum's central achievement.

## Why Mexican Death Imagery Means More Than Horror

Many museums displaying skeletons or funerary objects focus on mortality itself. The National Museum of Death instead asks why societies repeatedly reinvent the image of death.

Its permanent displays trace changing interpretations from pre-Hispanic cultures through the colonial era, the nineteenth century and contemporary Mexico. Instead of suggesting one continuous tradition, the museum shows how Indigenous beliefs, Catholic symbolism, popular printmaking, folk craft and modern visual culture have all contributed different ideas about death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx">[Museo Nacional de la Muerte]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uaa.mx.</span></span></span>

Visitors therefore encounter objects that may appear visually similar but serve very different purposes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* ceremonial and funerary artefacts connected with pre-Hispanic beliefs;
* colonial religious art emphasising mortality and salvation;
* satirical prints mocking social inequality;
* sugar skulls, decorative paper art and festival objects linked with remembrance;
* modern paintings, prints and installations exploring personal or political themes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitmexico.com/en/destino/18411/ennational-museum-of-deathesmuseo-nacional-de-la-muerte" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmexico.com">[Visit Mexico]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmexico.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mexico National Museum of Death</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mexico National Museum of Death</span></span></span>

</div>

The museum's message is that skull imagery is not automatically cheerful, tragic or frightening. Its meaning depends on who created it, when it was made and the community that uses it.

### From Private Collection to University Museum

The museum opened in 2007 after engraver Octavio Bajonero Gil donated his personal collection to the Autonomous University of Aguascalientes. Rather than creating a cabinet of curiosities, the university developed an educational museum dedicated to the history of death imagery in Mexico. In 2014 the exhibition narrative was significantly expanded through additional collections and a redesigned interpretation covering the evolution of funerary art across Mexican history. A major refurbishment completed in 2023 introduced more interactive displays while retaining the historical focus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx">[uaa.mx]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uaa.mx.</span></span></span>

The museum occupies several historic university buildings in Aguascalientes' city centre, itself a reminder that this is an academic institution rather than a commercial horror attraction. Temporary exhibitions continue to explore how contemporary artists reinterpret mortality alongside the permanent historical displays.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ags.gob.mx/turismo/conocemas/museodelamuerte/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ags.gob.mx">[Aguascalientes City Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ags.gob.mx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.mx.</span></span></span>

## Skulls and Skeletons Across Mexican History

One of the museum's strengths is showing that the familiar skeleton figure has repeatedly acquired new meanings.

Pre-Hispanic societies incorporated death into broader ideas about cosmic cycles, ancestors and renewal. Colonial religious art then introduced European Christian imagery concerned with mortality, judgement and salvation. Rather than replacing earlier traditions completely, these visual languages gradually overlapped, producing distinctive forms of Mexican funerary art.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitmexico.com/en/destino/18411/ennational-museum-of-deathesmuseo-nacional-de-la-muerte" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmexico.com">[Visit Mexico]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmexico.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mexico National Museum of Death</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mexico National Museum of Death</span></span></span>

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, skeletal figures had also become powerful tools of satire. The engravings of José Guadalupe Posada, born in Aguascalientes, transformed skeletons into recognisably human characters who mocked politicians, fashionable elites and social pretensions. His famous elegant female skeleton, later known as *La Catrina*, originally criticised people who attempted to imitate European high society rather than celebrating death itself. Over time, artists including Diego Rivera helped turn the figure into one of the most recognisable symbols associated with Mexican visual culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/resource/ppmsc.03450/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress Calaveras del montón, número 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressCalaveras del montón, número 1 - digital file from original &#124; Library of CongressJanuary 1, 1910...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1910</span></span></span>

Seen in the museum, these prints are presented less as isolated masterpieces than as evidence that death imagery could become journalism, political commentary and popular entertainment simultaneously.

## How Context Changes the Meaning of Death

Perhaps the museum's most valuable lesson is that identical symbols can communicate entirely different ideas.

A sugar skull displayed during family remembrance is not equivalent to a satirical newspaper print. A carved skeleton in folk art serves a different purpose from a colonial religious painting of death, even though both employ skeletal imagery. Likewise, contemporary artists often use skulls to explore violence, identity, migration or environmental concerns rather than traditional ritual.

By arranging collections chronologically and thematically, the museum encourages visitors to ask questions before assuming a meaning:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was the object devotional or satirical?
* Was it intended for ritual, public education or artistic expression?
* Does it commemorate specific people, criticise society or reflect religious belief?
* How would its original audience have understood it?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx">[Museo Nacional de la Muerte]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museonacionaldelamuerte.uaa.mx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uaa.mx.</span></span></span>

</div>

This approach distinguishes the museum from attractions that present skulls simply as exotic decoration or frightening spectacle.

## Why the Museum Matters Within Mexico's Curiosity Culture

Within Mexico's wider landscape of unusual museums, the National Museum of Death occupies a distinctive position. Unlike collections built around preserved human remains or sensational stories, its subject is representation rather than physical death. The exhibits explore how artists, craftspeople, printers and communities have repeatedly transformed mortality into images that express humour, remembrance, protest, spirituality and national identity.

That makes it an important companion to other unusual Mexican collections. Where museums such as those displaying naturally preserved bodies raise ethical questions about exhibiting the dead, the Museum of Death asks a different question: why people continue to create images of death at all.

Its answer is that Mexican death imagery has never had a single meaning. Across changing historical periods, skulls and skeletons have become visual shorthand for equality before death, criticism of power, remembrance of loved ones, artistic invention and cultural continuity. Understanding that complexity reveals why these familiar images belong as much to art history and social history as they do to the country's reputation for the macabre.
