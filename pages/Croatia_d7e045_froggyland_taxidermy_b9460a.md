## Introduction

Froggyland in Split is one of [Croatia]({{ 'where-croatia-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s most distinctive curiosity attractions: a collection of 507 taxidermied frogs arranged in 21 miniature scenes that mimic human life. Visitors encounter frogs attending school, playing cards, performing in a circus, working as blacksmiths or musicians, and taking part in everyday activities from the early twentieth century. At first glance the displays appear humorous and surreal, but they also raise modern questions about the ethics of displaying animals for entertainment. The museum therefore occupies an unusual place in Croatia's landscape of strange collections. It is both a surviving example of a largely forgotten style of European taxidermy and a reminder that attitudes towards animals have changed considerably over the past century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Froggyland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## How the frog scenes were made

The collection is attributed to Hungarian taxidermist Ferenc Mere (1878–1947), who is believed to have created the displays between about 1910 and 1920. According to the museum's account, Mere originally produced more than 1,000 posed frogs, although only 507 survive today. After passing through several owners and being rediscovered decades later in what is now Serbia, the surviving dioramas eventually found a permanent home in Split.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Froggyland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

What makes the collection remarkable is not simply the number of specimens but the craftsmanship involved. Frogs are far more difficult to preserve than many mammals or birds because of their delicate skin. Accounts of Mere's technique describe the animals being emptied through the mouth rather than cut open across the body, allowing the skin to remain outwardly intact before being carefully filled and posed. Tiny handmade props complete each scene, creating convincing miniature theatres rather than simple rows of preserved specimens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/froggyland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Froggyland in Split</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas ObscuraFroggyland in SplitOctober 8, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 8, 2015</span></span></span>

The scenes depict ordinary social life rather than fantasy. Classrooms, weddings, sporting events, workshops, orchestras and cafés all appear, often with humorous details such as misbehaving pupils or card players cheating one another. Although the frogs are the obvious attraction, the displays also preserve a miniature portrait of Central European everyday life as imagined in the early twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://splitcurated.com/listings/froggyland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: splitcurated.com">[Split Curated]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">splitcurated.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Split Curated Froggyland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Split CuratedFroggyland - Split Curated...</span></span></span>

## Anthropomorphic taxidermy as popular spectacle

Today Froggyland feels unique, but it belongs to a broader historical tradition. During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, anthropomorphic taxidermy—posing preserved animals as though they were people—was a fashionable form of entertainment across parts of Europe and North America. These displays combined technical skill, humour and theatrical storytelling at a time when taxidermy itself was widely admired as both science and craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/jhc/article-abstract/35/2/333/6605186" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicCarl Akeley’s ‘lost’ decorative taxidermy and anthropomorphic groups &#124; Journal of the History of Collections &#124; Oxford Academi...</span></span></span>

The best-known British practitioner was Walter Potter, whose rabbits, kittens and frogs were similarly arranged in elaborate human scenes. Froggyland differs because it concentrates almost entirely on frogs and survives as one of the largest intact collections devoted to a single species. Rather than presenting animals in natural habitats, it transforms them into actors playing recognisably human roles, blurring the boundary between natural history, folk humour and popular art.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/a-ribbiting-display" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust for Scotland A ribbiting display</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for Scotland A ribbiting display</span></span></span>

This historical context helps explain why the collection can seem simultaneously amusing and unsettling. Modern museum visitors often expect taxidermy to illustrate wildlife or scientific knowledge. Froggyland instead preserves an older tradition in which preserved animals served as characters in comic narratives intended to entertain audiences as much as educate them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/the-hungarian-taxidermist-who-saw-frogs-as-people" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura The Hungarian Taxidermist Who Saw Frogs As People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura The Hungarian Taxidermist Who Saw Frogs As People</span></span></span>

## Animal ethics and modern visitor reactions

The ethical debate surrounding Froggyland centres less on what visitors see today than on how the collection was created more than a century ago. The frogs were intentionally caught, killed and preserved to become works of art and entertainment. While such practices attracted little public criticism when the collection was made, many modern visitors view them through contemporary standards of animal welfare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Froggyland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Online reviews and media coverage reveal sharply divided reactions. Many visitors admire the extraordinary craftsmanship and regard the museum as a fascinating historical curiosity. Others feel uncomfortable that hundreds of animals were deliberately killed for decorative purposes and argue that the displays normalise unnecessary animal use. The museum's owner has responded by emphasising that the specimens are historical objects created in a different era rather than products of modern taxidermy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northcountrypublicradio.org/news/npr/1000842832/plus.npr.org/throughline" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northcountrypublicradio.org">[NCPR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northcountrypublicradio.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northcountrypublicradio.org.</span></span></span>

Unlike controversies involving contemporary wildlife collections, Froggyland does not encourage the production of new specimens. Instead, it presents an inherited collection whose historical significance is increasingly intertwined with ethical reflection. Visitors are therefore asked, implicitly rather than explicitly, to consider whether preserving such objects helps document changing cultural values or merely prolongs practices that many people now reject.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.tufts.edu/museumstudents/2021/06/28/froggyland-the-museum-of-frog-people/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.tufts.edu">[Tufts Self-Serve Blogs and Websites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.tufts.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tufts Self-Serve Blogs and WebsitesFroggyland: The museum of frog people – Museum Studies Blog at Tufts University...</span></span></span>

## Why Froggyland matters in Croatia's curiosity culture


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Within Croatia's wider landscape of unusual museums, Froggyland stands apart because it is neither a scientific institution nor a purely fictional attraction. It is an authentic surviving collection from an earlier period of European popular culture, preserved largely intact and displayed as both historical artefact and tourist curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Froggyland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

Its importance lies not only in its eccentric appearance but also in the questions it encourages. The museum demonstrates how tastes in collecting, ideas about animals and expectations of museums have evolved. What once functioned as humorous entertainment is now equally valuable as evidence of changing ethics, museum practice and public attitudes towards the relationship between humans and animals.
