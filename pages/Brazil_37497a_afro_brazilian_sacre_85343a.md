## Introduction

The collection now known as **Nosso Sagrado** (“Our Sacred”) is one of [Brazil]({{ 'what-makes-brazils-strangest-collections/' | relative_url }})’s most unusual and historically significant heritage collections because its objects did not enter a museum through archaeology, donation or collecting. Instead, many were seized during police raids on Afro-Brazilian religious communities and catalogued as evidence of supposed criminal activity. Today the same ritual objects are being reinterpreted as sacred cultural heritage and as evidence of state repression rather than of wrongdoing. This transformation has made the collection an important example of how museums can confront difficult histories instead of preserving the assumptions that created them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://periodicos.unb.br/index.php/museologia/article/view/45280" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: periodicos.unb.br">[UnB Periodicals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">periodicos.unb.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UnB PeriodicalsA chegada e chegadas do nosso sagrado à república &#124; Museologia &amp; InterdisciplinaridadeNovember 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2022</span></span></span>

Unlike many museum stories, this is not simply about conserving unusual artefacts. It is about changing the meaning attached to those artefacts. The movement of the collection from police custody to the Museu da República, together with its renaming, reflects a wider process of historical reparation for Afro-Brazilian religious communities whose sacred objects were criminalised for decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ibirapitanga.org.br/en/historias/ancestral-words-a-story-about-the-our-sacred-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibirapitanga.org.br">[Instituto Ibirapitanga]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibirapitanga.org.br</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Instituto Ibirapitanga Ancestral words: a story about the Our Sacred Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instituto IbirapitangaAncestral words: a story about the Our Sacred Collection - Instituto IbirapitangaMay 20, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2025</span></span></span>

## How Did Sacred Objects Become Police Evidence?

During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Afro-Brazilian religions were subjected to sustained surveillance by police authorities. Although freedom of religion formally existed, police frequently relied on criminal law provisions relating to fraud, public order, healing practices or supposed superstition to raid places of worship. Ceremonial objects—including ritual tools, statues, vessels, musical instruments and offerings—were confiscated and treated as physical evidence in criminal investigations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://periodicos.unb.br/index.php/museologia/article/view/45280" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: periodicos.unb.br">[UnB Periodicals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">periodicos.unb.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UnB PeriodicalsA chegada e chegadas do nosso sagrado à república &#124; Museologia &amp; InterdisciplinaridadeNovember 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2022</span></span></span>

These seizures were not isolated incidents but formed a pattern during the First Brazilian Republic. Rather than recognising the objects as belonging to living religious traditions, officials interpreted them through racist and colonial assumptions that portrayed African-derived religions as primitive or dangerous. The confiscated material accumulated within police institutions and eventually became known as the **Museum of Black Magic** collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://periodicos.unb.br/index.php/museologia/article/view/45280" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: periodicos.unb.br">[UnB Periodicals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">periodicos.unb.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UnB PeriodicalsA chegada e chegadas do nosso sagrado à república &#124; Museologia &amp; InterdisciplinaridadeNovember 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2022</span></span></span>

The collection therefore preserves two intertwined histories:

* the religious traditions represented by the objects themselves;
* the machinery of policing that removed them from their communities.

That dual history explains why the collection is so unusual. The objects cannot be understood simply as ethnographic artefacts because their route into museum care was itself part of a system of repression.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pantheon.ufrj.br/handle/11422/28898?locale=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pantheon.ufrj.br">[Pantheon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pantheon.ufrj.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufrj.br.</span></span></span>

## Why the Label "Black Magic" Distorted Their Meaning

For decades the official title **Museum of Black Magic** framed the collection according to the language used by police rather than by the communities from which the objects had been taken. The label implied that the collection documented occult crime or superstition instead of legitimate religious practice.

This naming mattered because museum catalogues shape how visitors understand collections. By presenting ritual implements as examples of "black magic", institutions repeated the prejudices that had justified their seizure. Sacred objects became curiosities illustrating alleged criminality rather than expressions of faith, ancestry and religious identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ibirapitanga.org.br/en/historias/ancestral-words-a-story-about-the-our-sacred-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibirapitanga.org.br">[Instituto Ibirapitanga]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibirapitanga.org.br</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Instituto Ibirapitanga Ancestral words: a story about the Our Sacred Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instituto IbirapitangaAncestral words: a story about the Our Sacred Collection - Instituto IbirapitangaMay 20, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2025</span></span></span>

A further irony is that, in 1938, part of the collection became Brazil's first protected ethnographic collection under the national heritage authority. The heritage listing helped preserve the objects physically, but it also preserved the discriminatory title and interpretive framework for many decades. The collection survived, yet its official description continued to reflect the perspective of those who had confiscated it rather than those who had created and used it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ibirapitanga.org.br/en/historias/ancestral-words-a-story-about-the-our-sacred-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibirapitanga.org.br">[Instituto Ibirapitanga]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibirapitanga.org.br</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Instituto Ibirapitanga Ancestral words: a story about the Our Sacred Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instituto IbirapitangaAncestral words: a story about the Our Sacred Collection - Instituto IbirapitangaMay 20, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2025</span></span></span>

## Renaming, Transfer and Historical Reparation

Pressure to reinterpret the collection grew through decades of advocacy by Afro-Brazilian religious leaders, scholars and museum professionals. A particularly important milestone came with the **Liberte Nosso Sagrado** ("Free Our Sacred") campaign, launched in 2017, which argued that the objects should leave police custody and be treated as religious heritage rather than criminal evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ibirapitanga.org.br/en/historias/ancestral-words-a-story-about-the-our-sacred-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibirapitanga.org.br">[Instituto Ibirapitanga]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibirapitanga.org.br</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Instituto Ibirapitanga Ancestral words: a story about the Our Sacred Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instituto IbirapitangaAncestral words: a story about the Our Sacred Collection - Instituto IbirapitangaMay 20, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2025</span></span></span>

In 2020 the collection was transferred from the Civil Police Museum to the Museu da República in Rio de Janeiro. The move represented far more than a change of storage location. It marked an institutional acknowledgement that the previous setting had reinforced the original injustice by continuing to present sacred objects through a policing framework.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pantheon.ufrj.br/handle/11422/28898?locale=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pantheon.ufrj.br">[Pantheon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pantheon.ufrj.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufrj.br.</span></span></span>

The collection was subsequently renamed **Nosso Sagrado** ("Our Sacred"), replacing the old title with language chosen to affirm the religious significance of the objects. Recent scholarship describes the renaming not as a symbolic gesture alone but as part of an ongoing reparative process involving museum staff, researchers and representatives of Afro-Brazilian religious communities working together on interpretation, documentation and stewardship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pantheon.ufrj.br/handle/11422/28898?locale=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pantheon.ufrj.br">[Pantheon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pantheon.ufrj.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufrj.br.</span></span></span>

## Why This Collection Is Different from Most Museum Repatriation Cases

Many museum restitution debates concern artefacts removed during colonial expansion and now held overseas. Nosso Sagrado presents a different kind of historical injustice.

Here, the contested journey occurred largely within Brazil itself. The state seized objects from its own citizens, classified them as criminal evidence, then preserved them under that interpretation for generations. As a result, the collection raises questions not only about ownership but also about institutional language, museum authority and the continuing effects of religious discrimination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pantheon.ufrj.br/handle/11422/28898?locale=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pantheon.ufrj.br">[Pantheon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pantheon.ufrj.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufrj.br.</span></span></span>

The case also illustrates that historical reparation can involve several linked processes rather than a single act of return:

* acknowledging how collections were originally formed;
* replacing discriminatory cataloguing and terminology;
* involving descendant communities in interpretation and governance;
* recognising the spiritual status of the objects rather than treating them as ethnographic curiosities alone.

</div>

Researchers increasingly describe these changes as an ongoing process rather than a completed settlement. The collection remains in public care, but its interpretation now seeks to expose the history of repression instead of reproducing it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pantheon.ufrj.br/handle/11422/28898?locale=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pantheon.ufrj.br">[Pantheon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pantheon.ufrj.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufrj.br.</span></span></span>

## What Visitors Should Expect Today

Visitors encountering the collection should not expect a display devoted to mystery, occultism or sensational stories. Instead, the significance lies in understanding why ordinary-looking ritual objects once became police exhibits and how museums can change the narratives attached to them.

The collection includes ceremonial objects used in Afro-Brazilian religious practice, but its greatest importance is historical rather than aesthetic. Every object embodies two overlapping stories: its original sacred purpose and the period when the Brazilian state interpreted that same object as evidence of criminal behaviour. Understanding both histories is essential to appreciating why Nosso Sagrado has become one of Brazil's most distinctive heritage collections and a landmark in debates over museum ethics, religious freedom and historical justice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://periodicos.unb.br/index.php/museologia/article/view/45280" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: periodicos.unb.br">[unb.br]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">periodicos.unb.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UnB PeriodicalsA chegada e chegadas do nosso sagrado à república &#124; Museologia &amp; InterdisciplinaridadeNovember 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2022</span></span></span>
