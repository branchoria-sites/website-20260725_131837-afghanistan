## Introduction

The Ishango Bone is one of the most famous archaeological objects ever discovered in the Democratic Republic of the [Congo]({{ 'where-congos-strangest-collections-keep/' | relative_url }}), yet it is displayed in Belgium rather than in the country where it was found. This small, notched bone has become internationally known because some researchers believe it may represent one of humanity's earliest mathematical or counting devices. At the same time, it has become a symbol of a much broader question: who should own, interpret and display Congo's most important cultural heritage?



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Congo_77f256_ishango_and_restitut_9da3cb-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Lost Heritage illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Congo_77f256_ishango_and_restitut_9da3cb-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Congo_77f256_ishango_and_restitut_9da3cb-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For visitors interested in Congo's unusual history, the Ishango Bone is more than an archaeological curiosity. It illustrates how remarkable objects excavated during the colonial era often left the country, becoming celebrated in European museums while remaining largely inaccessible to the communities whose landscapes produced them. The debate surrounding the bone therefore combines archaeology, museum ethics, colonial history and modern restitution campaigns.

## Who Owns Congo's Most Mysterious Objects?

The Ishango Bone was excavated during Belgian archaeological expeditions at Ishango, near Lake Edward in what is now Virunga National Park, between 1950 and 1959. The principal excavations were led by Belgian geologist Jean de Heinzelin after earlier discoveries had identified the site as an important prehistoric settlement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ishango.naturalsciences.be/en/en-ishango-09.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ishango.naturalsciences.be">[ishango.naturalsciences.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ishango.naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalsciences.be.</span></span></span>

Today the artefact is housed in the Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences in Brussels, together with thousands of other archaeological remains recovered from the same excavations. The museum presents the bone as one of the highlights of its prehistoric collections and provides interactive displays inviting visitors to consider possible interpretations of its markings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>

Its location abroad reflects a wider pattern. During the Belgian colonial period, enormous numbers of Congolese archaeological, ethnographic and scientific objects were exported to European museums and research institutions. Some were collected during official expeditions, others through missionary activity, colonial administration or private collecting, leaving many of Congo's most internationally recognised artefacts outside the country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://restitutionbelgium.be/en/report" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: restitutionbelgium.be">[Restitution Belgium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">restitutionbelgium.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Restitution BelgiumEthical Principles for the Management and Restitution of Colonial Collections in Belgium (June 2021) &#124; Restitution Bel...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2021</span></span></span>

## The Ishango Bone Mystery

The bone itself measures roughly 10 centimetres and is believed to be around 20,000 years old, although some dating estimates are slightly older depending on the archaeological layer being discussed. Embedded at one end is a small piece of quartz, perhaps used for engraving or cutting. What makes the object extraordinary are three columns of carefully grouped notches carved into its surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>

No one knows with certainty what those marks meant.

Several major interpretations have been proposed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A tally stick**, used to record quantities or events.
* **A mathematical aid**, with numerical relationships suggesting addition, doubling or other calculations.
* **A lunar calendar**, possibly tracking phases of the Moon.
* **A symbolic object**, whose markings carried meanings now impossible to reconstruct.

</div>

Some researchers have suggested the grouped notches hint at surprisingly sophisticated numerical thinking, while others argue that claims about advanced mathematics go well beyond what the evidence can support. Even recent scholarly work continues to offer new hypotheses rather than definitive answers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.1019" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does the Ishango Bone Indicate Knowledge of the Base 12? An Interpretation of a Prehistoric Discovery, the First Mathematical Tool o...</span></span></span>

This uncertainty is precisely why the Ishango Bone remains fascinating. It is not simply "the world's oldest calculator", as popular media sometimes describe it. Instead, it is an exceptionally rare prehistoric object whose purpose remains unresolved.

## How Congolese Objects Reached Europe

The Ishango Bone is unusual because it was recovered through scientific excavation rather than military seizure or commercial art dealing. Nevertheless, its removal occurred within the structures of Belgian colonial rule.

Belgian scientific institutions organised extensive archaeological and natural-history expeditions across the colony. Material judged scientifically significant was commonly transported to Belgium for study, conservation and exhibition. The Ishango collections followed this established practice and remain together in Brussels today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ishango.naturalsciences.be/en/en-ishango-09.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ishango.naturalsciences.be">[ishango.naturalsciences.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ishango.naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalsciences.be.</span></span></span>

The wider movement of Congolese heritage was far more varied. Objects entered European museums through:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* colonial government expeditions;
* missionary collecting;
* ethnographic research;
* military campaigns;
* commercial dealers;
* private collectors.

</div>

Modern provenance research increasingly distinguishes between these different pathways instead of treating all collections as though they shared identical histories. Some acquisitions were documented archaeological recoveries, while others occurred through coercion, unequal colonial power or outright violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://restitutionbelgium.be/en/report" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: restitutionbelgium.be">[Restitution Belgium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">restitutionbelgium.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Restitution BelgiumEthical Principles for the Management and Restitution of Colonial Collections in Belgium (June 2021) &#124; Restitution Bel...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2021</span></span></span>

That distinction has become central to present-day restitution discussions.

## Provenance and Restitution Debates

The Ishango Bone has become part of wider conversations about returning African cultural heritage to its countries of origin.

In recent years Congolese officials have publicly expressed interest in seeking the artefact's return. Discussions intensified alongside broader debates over Belgian colonial collections and high-profile restitution initiatives involving cultural objects from across Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebulletin.be/node/95750" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebulletin.be">[The Bulletin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebulletin.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bulletin Congo to request return of famous Ishango Bone &#124; The Bulletin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The BulletinCongo to request return of famous Ishango Bone &#124; The BulletinNovember 23, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 23, 2021</span></span></span>

Unlike objects clearly identified as war booty or looted ceremonial treasures, however, the Ishango Bone occupies a more complex legal and ethical position. Questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Should scientifically excavated archaeological finds removed during colonial administration automatically be returned?
* Does the colonial political context itself make continued overseas ownership problematic?
* Should internationally significant discoveries remain in long-established research collections?
* Could long-term loans, shared curation or rotating exhibitions provide alternatives to permanent transfer?

</div>

Belgium has responded by expanding provenance research into colonial-era collections and by encouraging closer cooperation with source countries. Expert reports have argued that decisions should rest on detailed investigation of how individual objects left their communities or excavation sites rather than applying a single rule to every collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://restitutionbelgium.be/en/report" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: restitutionbelgium.be">[Restitution Belgium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">restitutionbelgium.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Restitution BelgiumEthical Principles for the Management and Restitution of Colonial Collections in Belgium (June 2021) &#124; Restitution Bel...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2021</span></span></span>

## Why the Debate Matters Beyond One Bone

The Ishango Bone has acquired symbolic importance that extends well beyond archaeology.

For many Congolese observers, the issue reflects a larger imbalance in global heritage. Some of the country's most celebrated objects are better known to visitors in Brussels than to people living near the places where they were discovered. Their stories are often interpreted through European museums, labels and research traditions rather than through Congolese institutions and scholarship.

At the same time, Belgian researchers emphasise that the bone has benefited from decades of conservation and scientific study and remains available to international researchers. These are not necessarily opposing goals: many museum professionals now advocate partnerships that combine conservation expertise with greater Congolese authority over interpretation, research priorities and public access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/science/collections-data/anthropology-and-prehistory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalsciences.be.</span></span></span>

The debate therefore concerns not only physical ownership but also who tells the story of one of Africa's most intriguing prehistoric discoveries.

## Visiting the Heritage Today

Travellers hoping to see the original Ishango Bone currently need to visit Brussels, where it is displayed by the Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences. Interactive exhibits encourage visitors to examine the famous notches and consider competing explanations for their meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/250-years-of-natural-sciences/the-ishango-bone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of Natural Sciences The Ishango Bone &#124; Institute of Natural Sciences</span></span></span>

Within the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the original excavation site lies in the Virunga region. Although archaeologically significant, it is not a conventional visitor attraction, and access is affected by conservation priorities and longstanding security challenges within and around Virunga National Park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.astronomicalheritage.net/index.php/show-entity?identity=85&amp;idsubentity=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.astronomicalheritage.net">[web.astronomicalheritage.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.astronomicalheritage.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UNESC O Portal to the Heritage of Astronomy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO Portal to the Heritage of Astronomy - Show entity...</span></span></span>

For visitors exploring Congo's unusual heritage, the Ishango Bone therefore represents a distinctive kind of curiosity: an object that is inseparable from the country, yet physically absent from it. Its mystery lies not only in the prehistoric marks carved into its surface, but also in the continuing debate over where such extraordinary pieces of Congolese heritage belong and who should have the primary right to interpret them.

    var localeMarkets = localeMatches();
    if (localeMarkets.exact && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.exact) >= 0) return localeMarkets.exact;
    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;

    return available[0] || '';
  }
