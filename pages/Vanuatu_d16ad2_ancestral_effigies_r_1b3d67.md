## Introduction

[Vanuatu]({{ 'where-vanuatu-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s ancestral effigies are among the most visually striking cultural objects in the Pacific, but they are not curiosities in the ordinary museum sense. Many were created as mortuary figures that incorporated the skulls of named ancestors, making them both works of ritual craftsmanship and human remains. Their appearance has long attracted collectors and museums, yet their meaning has always rested with the communities that created them. The recent return of Malakula effigies from the United States has therefore become far more than a restitution story. It has highlighted who has the right to decide whether these objects are displayed, stored, studied or removed from public view, and why customary authority remains central to those decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. ## How the Malakula mortuary figures were made

The best-known ancestral effigies from Vanuatu are the large mortuary figures associated with parts of Malakula, often referred to by the name *rambaramp*. They were not generic sculptures but carefully constructed memorials to particular deceased men. After complex funerary rites, a skull could be incorporated into a life-sized or larger figure modelled with clay, fibres, plant materials and pigments. The finished effigy represented the individual at a recognised stage of life rather than an anonymous ancestor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. The relics, including two skulls and three large effigies (rambaramp), were recognized by Vanuat...</span></span></span>

These figures originally stood within sacred men's houses rather than public spaces. Their purpose was tied to remembrance, lineage and relationships between the living and the dead. They were not created for trade or exhibition, and they were understood within local systems of customary authority that governed who could approach them, handle them or even speak about them.

Although the traditions surrounding these figures largely ceased in many communities after widespread conversion to Christianity during the twentieth century, this did not strip the surviving effigies of their cultural status. Communities continued to recognise them as ancestors rather than historical decorations. That distinction remains fundamental to current debates over collecting and display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. ## From sacred ancestors to trafficked art

During the colonial era and throughout much of the twentieth century, Pacific ceremonial objects and human remains entered international collecting networks. In Vanuatu, ancestral skulls, mortuary figures and other sacred objects were removed from villages, sometimes through purchase, sometimes through theft, and often with little regard for customary law or community consent. Anthropologists have documented a broader market in Pacific funerary material that treated sacred objects as ethnographic specimens or art commodities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. The relics, including two skulls and three large effigies (rambaramp), were recognized by Vanuat...</span></span></span>

The 2024 repatriation of five crates from New York illustrates how these collecting histories continue to shape museum ethics today. Following an eight-year FBI investigation into the estate of a deceased collector, authorities returned two modelled skulls and three large Malakula effigies to Vanuatu. The largest measured approximately 3.5 metres in length and weighed around 318 kilograms, making its transport unusually difficult because of its fragility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. Equally significant was how the objects were identified. Curator Kaitip Kami of the Vanuatu Cultural Centre recognised the figures immediately as belonging to communities on his home island of Malakula. Their value lay not simply in artistic style but in the fact that they represented identifiable ancestors whose origins remained culturally legible despite decades abroad.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. The relics, including two skulls and three large effigies (rambaramp), were recognized by Vanuat...</span></span></span>

The case also exposed an uncomfortable reality behind the international market for Indigenous material. Objects incorporating human remains could circulate for decades as collectible art while remaining, from the perspective of descendant communities, members of an ancestral landscape rather than museum artefacts.

## Who has authority over display and return?

The most important question is not whether ancestral effigies are historically valuable—they unquestionably are—but who has the authority to decide their future.

For Vanuatu, authority is rooted first in customary ownership. The Vanuatu Cultural Centre's research policy explicitly states that cultural knowledge, practices and associated heritage belong to individuals, families, lineages and communities. It also recognises that some knowledge and objects are *tabu*, meaning access is restricted, and that traditional custodians retain rights over how cultural material is used and interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vanuatuculturalcentre.gov.vu/research/research-permits/research-policy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vanuatuculturalcentre.gov.vu">[Vanuatu Cultural Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vanuatuculturalcentre.gov.vu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Vanuatu Cultural Centre VANUATU CULTURAL RESEARCH POLICY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Vanuatu Cultural Centre VANUATU CULTURAL RESEARCH POLICY</span></span></span>

This framework differs from conventional museum assumptions that acquisition automatically grants unrestricted display rights. Instead, museums increasingly act as custodians working alongside communities whose authority predates any institutional collection.

The country's long-established network of community cultural fieldworkers reinforces this model. Rather than treating heritage as something removed from villages and interpreted solely by specialists, the network documents traditions through local researchers who remain accountable to their own communities. That approach has become influential internationally because it places cultural governance alongside documentation rather than separating the two.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vanuatuculturalcentre.gov.vu/index.php/woman-culture-program" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vanuatuculturalcentre.gov.vu">[Vanuatu Cultural Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vanuatuculturalcentre.gov.vu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Vanuatu Cultural CentreWoman Cultural Program. The Vanuatu Cultural Centre &amp; Side by Side Film PresentSeptember 25, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 25, 2018</span></span></span>

The implication for ancestral effigies is clear. Decisions about exhibition, conservation, photography or restricted access are not purely technical museum questions. They are matters of cultural governance.

## Why repatriation does not automatically mean public display

Returning an object to its country of origin is only the first stage of repatriation.

Once ancestral remains arrive in Vanuatu, further decisions must be made about whether they should remain in secure museum care, return to their original communities, be stored with restricted access or, in some cases, no longer be publicly exhibited at all. Those decisions are guided by consultation rather than a single universal rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. The relics, including two skulls and three large effigies (rambaramp), were recognized by Vanuat...</span></span></span>

Curator Kaitip Kami expressed this principle directly during the 2024 return, describing the effigies as "part of a human being" and arguing that they should not be displayed outside Vanuatu. His remarks underline that these are not simply historical objects but ancestors whose treatment carries continuing ethical and spiritual responsibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. The relics, including two skulls and three large effigies (rambaramp), were recognized by Vanuat...</span></span></span>

This perspective explains why repatriation debates increasingly distinguish between ordinary ethnographic collections and collections containing human remains. The latter involve questions of dignity, descendant rights and customary law that cannot be resolved solely through museum acquisition records.

## Why this matters for Vanuatu's unusual collections


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

For visitors interested in unusual museums and extraordinary artefacts, Malakula's ancestral effigies are undeniably remarkable. Yet their importance lies precisely in resisting the label of curiosity.

</div>

They demonstrate how an object can appear extraordinary to outsiders while remaining, to its own community, part of an ongoing relationship with named ancestors. That difference changes how museums interpret them, how governments negotiate their return and how tourism must approach them.

Within Vanuatu's broader landscape of unusual heritage, ancestral effigies therefore represent an important shift in museum practice. They remind visitors that the most compelling objects are sometimes those whose significance cannot be separated from the people who continue to hold authority over them. The story is ultimately not about exotic sculptures returning to a national collection, but about communities reclaiming the right to determine how their ancestors should be remembered.
