## Introduction

Monsopiad's House of Skulls is one of [Malaysia]({{ 'where-malaysia-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s most debated heritage displays because it combines an arresting collection of human remains with an Indigenous community's continuing authority over its own history. Located within Monsopiad Heritage Village near Kota Kinabalu, the building preserves 42 human skulls traditionally identified as the trophies of the Kadazan warrior Monsopiad, whose exploits are placed around three centuries ago in local oral tradition. Unlike many museum displays of human remains, these skulls remain within a living cultural landscape managed by Monsopiad's descendants rather than being transferred into a state museum or university collection. That makes the site important not only for understanding Sabah's headhunting past, but also for exploring who has the right to interpret, care for and present culturally sensitive objects to the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sabahtourism.com/destination/monsopiad-heritage-village/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sabahtourism.com">[Sabah Tourism Board]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sabahtourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sabah Tourism Board Monsopiad Heritage Village &#124; Sabah Tourism Board</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sabah Tourism BoardMonsopiad Heritage Village &#124; Sabah Tourism BoardNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

## The Monsopiad story and headhunting tradition

The House of Skulls cannot be understood simply as a collection of macabre trophies. According to Kadazan oral tradition, Monsopiad became a feared warrior during a period when inter-community conflict and headhunting formed part of warfare and social prestige in parts of Borneo. The 42 skulls hanging from the rafters are presented as the heads of enemies defeated during his lifetime, with the number itself becoming central to his legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sabahtourism.com/destination/monsopiad-heritage-village/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sabahtourism.com">[Sabah Tourism Board]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sabahtourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sabah Tourism Board Monsopiad Heritage Village &#124; Sabah Tourism Board</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sabah Tourism BoardMonsopiad Heritage Village &#124; Sabah Tourism BoardNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

Modern scholarship treats these stories carefully. Historians distinguish between the historical reality that headhunting existed among several Indigenous societies in Borneo and the legendary details attached to individual heroes. The exact identity of every skull cannot be independently verified through documentary evidence, but the collection's significance lies less in proving every episode literally than in preserving a community's historical memory and ritual tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Visitors therefore encounter two overlapping narratives:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the oral history of Monsopiad as an ancestral warrior;
* evidence that headhunting formed part of historical social and military practices in parts of Borneo;
* the continuing importance of the skulls within Kadazan cultural identity rather than as anonymous archaeological specimens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why the House of Skulls remains culturally active

The ethical character of the site changes because it is not simply an old building filled with historic artefacts. Researchers describe the House of Skulls as an Indigenous ritual house whose collection continues to exist within local systems of belief, authority and customary practice. Rather than treating the skulls as detached museum objects, their custodians maintain that they remain connected to ancestors, ritual obligations and community identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. In many conventional museums, curators determine how human remains are stored, labelled and exhibited according to professional conservation standards developed largely within European museum traditions. At Monsopiad Heritage Village, decisions instead rest primarily with the descendants of Monsopiad and the community that inherited responsibility for the site. Academic research has highlighted this as an example of Indigenous museology, where local knowledge systems shape collection management instead of simply adapting to outside museum practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The village itself reinforces this wider setting. The skull house sits alongside traditional buildings, ceremonial spaces, family heirlooms and guided interpretation delivered by descendants, encouraging visitors to see the skulls as one element of a broader cultural landscape rather than the sole attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sabahtourism.com/destination/monsopiad-heritage-village/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sabahtourism.com">[Sabah Tourism Board]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sabahtourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sabah Tourism Board Monsopiad Heritage Village &#124; Sabah Tourism Board</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sabah Tourism BoardMonsopiad Heritage Village &#124; Sabah Tourism BoardNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

## Tourism, consent and authority over human remains

The House of Skulls raises questions that museums around the world increasingly face: who has authority over ancestral human remains, and how should they be displayed?

In many countries, museums have removed or repatriated Indigenous remains after recognising that collecting practices during colonial periods often ignored consent. Monsopiad presents a different situation. The skulls were not removed into an outside institution but remain under the stewardship of the community that claims cultural responsibility for them. Consequently, debates focus less on repatriation than on whether community custodianship itself provides sufficient ethical authority for continued public display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Supporters of the current approach argue that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* the descendants retain cultural authority over the site;
* removing the skulls to a conventional museum could separate them from their ritual and historical context;
* guided interpretation reduces the risk of treating them merely as gruesome curiosities;
* tourism income contributes to maintaining Kadazan cultural heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

Critics and museum ethicists nevertheless identify continuing tensions. Human remains always raise questions about dignity, informed consent and whether individuals who died centuries ago would have wished to become permanent public exhibits. Tourism marketing can also emphasise the dramatic appeal of "42 skulls", encouraging visitors to approach the site primarily as a spectacle rather than as a place carrying spiritual and historical meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

These competing perspectives illustrate that ethical display depends not only on where remains are kept but also on who interprets them, how visitors are prepared to encounter them and whether descendant communities genuinely control the narrative.

## Why this case matters beyond Sabah

The House of Skulls has become an important case study in debates about decolonising museums because it challenges assumptions that Western museum practice automatically represents the most ethical model for caring for sensitive collections. Instead, it demonstrates that Indigenous communities may possess their own established systems governing respect, ritual care and interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The case also illustrates a broader shift within heritage practice. Rather than asking only whether human remains should be displayed, many scholars now ask who decides, whose values shape interpretation and whether local communities exercise meaningful authority over collections connected to their own history. Monsopiad Heritage Village provides an unusually clear example because ownership, interpretation and cultural continuity remain closely linked through one extended family and community rather than passing entirely into state institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382448016_Decolonising_Collection_Management_in_an_Indigenous_Ritual_House_in_Malaysia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Within Malaysia's landscape of unusual heritage sites, this makes the House of Skulls distinctive. Its significance lies not simply in preserving 42 human skulls, but in demonstrating how an object that appears shocking to outsiders can simultaneously function as ancestral heritage, community memory and a continuing expression of Indigenous self-representation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sabahtourism.com/destination/monsopiad-heritage-village/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sabahtourism.com">[Sabah Tourism Board]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sabahtourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sabah Tourism Board Monsopiad Heritage Village &#124; Sabah Tourism Board</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sabah Tourism BoardMonsopiad Heritage Village &#124; Sabah Tourism BoardNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
