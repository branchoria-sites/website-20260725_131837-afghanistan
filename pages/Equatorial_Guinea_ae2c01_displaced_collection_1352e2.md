## Introduction

One of the most unusual aspects of [Equatorial Guinea]({{ 'where-did-equatorial-guineas-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s museum story is not what visitors can see inside the country, but what they cannot. During Spanish colonial rule, thousands of ethnographic objects and large numbers of animal, botanical and geological specimens were removed to museums and scientific institutions in Spain, particularly Barcelona. Today these collections are far larger, better documented and more accessible than many comparable collections remaining in Equatorial Guinea itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es/index.php/cultureandhistory/article/view/263" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es">[Culture and History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Culture and HistoryAnthropology and image in colonial contexts: the scientific expedition to Spanish territories in the Gulf of Guinea (1...</span></span></span>

For anyone interested in the country's unusual heritage, this absence has become part of the story. Ritual carvings, ceremonial objects, hunting equipment, masks, household artefacts and scientific specimens collected from forests, villages and colonial expeditions now sit in European museums and research collections. They are valuable historical resources, but they also raise difficult questions about colonial collecting, ownership, representation and whether more of Equatorial Guinea's heritage should eventually return.

## Why so much Equatoguinean heritage is abroad

Unlike many African territories administered by Britain or France, Equatorial Guinea was governed by Spain until 1968. During the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, colonial officials, missionaries, military officers, naturalists and museum expeditions actively collected objects for Spanish institutions.

### Ethnographic collections

The city's ethnographic museums preserve substantial collections originating from Equatorial Guinea, including material from Fang, Bubi and other communities. These include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* carved wooden figures;
* ritual and ceremonial objects;
* weapons and hunting equipment;
* musical instruments;
* jewellery and personal ornaments;
* baskets, pottery and domestic tools;
* textiles and everyday household items.

</div>

Many entered museum collections through colonial officials, missionaries or organised field expeditions rather than through local museums or community institutions.

The importance of these collections is historical as well as artistic. They document material culture that has sometimes disappeared or changed significantly over the past century, but they also reflect the selective interests of colonial collectors, who often preferred ritual, ceremonial or "exotic" objects over ordinary aspects of daily life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es/index.php/cultureandhistory/article/view/263" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es">[Culture and History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Culture and HistoryAnthropology and image in colonial contexts: the scientific expedition to Spanish territories in the Gulf of Guinea (1...</span></span></span>

### Natural-history collections

Barcelona also houses one of Europe's largest scientific collections containing material from Equatorial Guinea.

The Natural Sciences Museum of Barcelona holds more than four million specimens overall, with significant holdings originating from Equatorial Guinea across several zoological collections. Museum documentation specifically identifies important collections of mammals, birds, reptiles, molluscs and other specimens gathered during colonial expeditions. ## How colonial officials, missionaries and dealers collected

The movement of Equatoguinean objects into European museums occurred through several different routes rather than a single organised programme.

Missionaries frequently assembled collections while establishing churches and schools. Religious objects, ancestral carvings and items connected with local spiritual practices were often removed as examples of customs that missionaries believed were disappearing or should be replaced by Christianity.

Colonial administrators also collected objects during government service. Some entered museums directly, while others passed through private hands before eventually being donated or sold.

Scientific expeditions formed another major source. Researchers gathered zoological specimens, plants, insects and ethnographic material simultaneously, treating both local cultures and local wildlife as subjects for systematic study. The 1948 scientific expedition to Spanish Guinea sponsored by Spanish institutions became especially influential in expanding Barcelona's ethnographic collections and setting a pattern for further collecting during the 1950s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es/index.php/cultureandhistory/article/view/263" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es">[Culture and History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultureandhistory.revistas.csic.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Culture and HistoryAnthropology and image in colonial contexts: the scientific expedition to Spanish territories in the Gulf of Guinea (1...</span></span></span>

Commercial dealers played a smaller but still important role, purchasing artefacts from collectors or colonial residents before placing them in European museums or private collections.

## Why these collections matter today

The displaced collections present an unusual paradox.

On one hand, European museums have often preserved objects that might otherwise have been damaged, dispersed or lost during periods of political and economic change. Many collections have been professionally catalogued, photographed and increasingly digitised, making them available to researchers worldwide. The Natural Sciences Museum of Barcelona, for example, continues to expand online access to parts of its collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bioexplora.cat/en/open-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bioexplora.cat">[Bioexplora]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bioexplora.cat</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Explora la natura i també el museu &#124; Museu de Ciències Naturals de Barcelona</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bioexplora &#124; Explora la natura i també el museu &#124; Museu de Ciències Naturals de Barcelona...</span></span></span>

On the other hand, preservation does not erase the circumstances under which many objects left Equatorial Guinea. Museum records frequently identify the collector but provide relatively little information about the communities from which objects originated or whether their removal involved meaningful local consent.

For Equatoguineans interested in their own cultural history, this can mean that important artefacts are easier to study in Barcelona than in Malabo or Bata.

## Provenance and restitution questions

Unlike debates surrounding internationally famous collections such as the Benin Bronzes, Equatoguinean collections have received relatively limited public attention. There have been no equally prominent restitution campaigns, and relatively few objects have become symbols of international controversy.

Nevertheless, the same broader questions increasingly apply:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* How fully should museums investigate colonial-era acquisition histories?
* Which objects were legitimately purchased, and which may have been removed under unequal colonial conditions?
* Should digital access, long-term loans or shared exhibitions become more common?
* Are some culturally significant objects appropriate candidates for return?

</div>

Many museums have shifted towards more transparent provenance research rather than assuming older collecting practices were unproblematic. Spanish museums have also increasingly explored the colonial history behind their Equatoguinean collections through exhibitions examining how and why these objects entered European institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cultura.gob.es/mnantropologia/colecciones/fondos-museogr-ficos-y-documentales/colecciones-viajeras/ikunde.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultura.gob.es">[Cultura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultura.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cultura Ikunde</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ikunde - MUSEO NACIONAL DE ANTROPOLOGÍA &#124; Ministerio de Cultura...</span></span></span>

## The absence has become part of the museum story

For visitors exploring Equatorial Guinea's unusual heritage, the country's museum landscape cannot be understood simply by looking inside its borders.

The absence of thousands of ethnographic artefacts and scientific specimens has itself become a defining feature of the country's cultural history. Rather than one spectacular curiosity museum, Equatorial Guinea's most remarkable "collection" is dispersed across European institutions, particularly in Barcelona, where ritual carvings, colonial photographs, preserved wildlife and everyday objects continue to shape how the former colony is studied and interpreted.

That makes these displaced collections more than archives of the past. They remain living records of colonial science, missionary activity, museum practice and changing attitudes towards cultural ownership. As museums place greater emphasis on provenance research and collaborative interpretation, the story increasingly shifts from simply displaying Equatoguinean objects to examining how they arrived in Europe, whose voices have been represented, and how those histories should be told in the future.
