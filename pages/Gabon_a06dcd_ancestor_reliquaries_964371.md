## Introduction

Many of [Gabon]({{ 'where-gabons-strangest-collections-keep/' | relative_url }})'s most celebrated ancestor reliquary sculptures entered museums without the very elements that gave them meaning. The famous Fang wooden guardian figures and Kota metal-covered guardians were not originally independent works of art. They formed only one part of a sacred ensemble that also included baskets or bark containers holding carefully preserved ancestral bones, ritual substances and family relics. Once these assemblages were dismantled for collectors, museums typically acquired the sculpted guardians while the human remains were retained, reburied, destroyed, hidden or simply disappeared from the historical record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/310870" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Okak-Fang artist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOkak-Fang artist - Eyema byeri (reliquary guardian figure) - Okak-Fang peoples - The Metropolitan Museum of...</span></span></span>

Understanding what was removed is essential to understanding the objects themselves. A reliquary guardian displayed alone in a gallery represents only the visible fragment of a much larger religious system centred on lineage, memory and continuing relationships between the living and the dead.

## How Fang and Kota reliquaries worked

Among several communities in what is now Gabon, ancestor reliquaries were family treasures rather than public monuments. Their purpose was not to commemorate the dead in an abstract sense but to maintain an active connection between generations.

For the Fang, a carved wooden figure or head sat atop a cylindrical bark container known as a reliquary. Inside were selected skulls and other important bones of respected ancestors together with valued objects and powerful ritual materials. The complete ensemble was known as a byeri. Family elders consulted these ancestral relics before major decisions including marriages, journeys, political negotiations and hunting expeditions. The guardian figure announced the sacred nature of the contents while symbolically protecting them from both physical theft and improper viewing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/310870" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Okak-Fang artist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOkak-Fang artist - Eyema byeri (reliquary guardian figure) - Okak-Fang peoples - The Metropolitan Museum of...</span></span></span>

Kota communities used a different visual language but a similar principle. Their distinctive guardians, covered with copper and brass sheets, were attached to or placed above baskets containing ancestral bones. Because many Kota groups moved settlements periodically as agricultural land changed, portable reliquaries allowed ancestors to travel with the living community rather than remaining in fixed burial places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://smarthistory.org/kota-mfa/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smarthistory.org">[Smarthistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smarthistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">– Kota reliquary (mbulu ngulu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smarthistory – Kota reliquary (mbulu ngulu)...</span></span></span>

Seen in isolation, these sculptures can appear highly abstract or decorative. In their original setting, however, they functioned as visible markers directing attention towards the hidden ancestral remains beneath them.

## Why guardians were separated from ancestral remains

The separation happened for several different reasons, not all of them identical.

Some Fang communities regarded the relics themselves as the truly irreplaceable sacred element. Early European traders and collectors often found that carved guardians could be exchanged more readily than the ancestral bones they protected. In other cases, missionaries and colonial administrations actively discouraged or prohibited ancestor cults during the early twentieth century, leading to the abandonment, destruction or concealment of complete reliquaries. Sculptures survived because they appealed to collectors, while baskets and human remains often did not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalprojects.carlos.emory.edu/items/show/9009" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalprojects.carlos.emory.edu">[digitalprojects.carlos.emory.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalprojects.carlos.emory.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Byeri Guardian Figure · Michael C. Carlos Museum Collections Online</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Byeri Guardian Figure · Michael C. Carlos Museum Collections Online</span></span></span>

Museum collections therefore frequently preserve:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the carved guardian;
* little or none of the original bark container or basket;
* no ancestral bones;
* minimal information about the family's ritual practices.

</div>

This dramatically altered how the objects were understood. A religious assemblage became an individual sculpture.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art explicitly notes that Fang guardian figures were "invariably separated" from their reliquary containers before leaving Africa. That observation reflects a wider collecting pattern seen across European and North American museum collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/art/collection/search/310870" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Okak-Fang artist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOkak-Fang artist - Eyema byeri (reliquary guardian figure) - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

## What happened to the missing human remains?

There is no single answer because individual histories differ.

Some ancestral bones remained with descendant families even after guardians entered the art market. Others appear to have been reburied when communities abandoned earlier religious practices or converted to Christianity. In many documented cases, the archaeological trail simply ends because collectors recorded only the sculpture and ignored the rest of the assemblage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalprojects.carlos.emory.edu/items/show/9009" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalprojects.carlos.emory.edu">[digitalprojects.carlos.emory.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalprojects.carlos.emory.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Byeri Guardian Figure · Michael C. Carlos Museum Collections Online</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Byeri Guardian Figure · Michael C. Carlos Museum Collections Online</span></span></span>

This creates an important interpretive problem. Visitors naturally ask where the bones went, but museums often cannot answer with certainty because acquisition records from the colonial period rarely documented the fate of the associated human remains.

The absence therefore reflects both historical collecting practices and incomplete documentation rather than a single, universal sequence of events.

## Provenance, collecting and the modern art market

The removal of guardians transformed them into highly desirable works of modern art.

From the early twentieth century onwards, Fang and Kota sculptures attracted artists, dealers and collectors who admired their striking forms. Their influence on European modernism is well documented, yet the qualities celebrated by collectors—bold abstraction, simplified anatomy and expressive geometry—were originally inseparable from their ritual role as guardians of ancestral relics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Institution Reliquary guardian figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Institution Reliquary guardian figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span></span></span>

This shift also changed the questions museums ask. Instead of asking:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which ancestors did this reliquary protect?
* Which family commissioned it?
* What ceremonies surrounded it?

</div>

catalogues often focused on:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* stylistic schools;
* carving techniques;
* attributed artists;
* auction history;
* aesthetic innovation.

</div>

As provenance research has become more sophisticated, museums increasingly acknowledge that these sculptures entered collections as fragments of larger sacred assemblages rather than complete artworks. Labels now more commonly explain that the guardian originally crowned a reliquary containing ancestral remains instead of presenting it as an isolated carving.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/310870" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Okak-Fang artist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOkak-Fang artist - Eyema byeri (reliquary guardian figure) - Okak-Fang peoples - The Metropolitan Museum of...</span></span></span>

## Why the missing remains matter today

The absence of the bones changes how these objects are experienced.

In a museum gallery, visitors encounter an elegant sculpture illuminated in a display case. Originally, initiated family members encountered a guardian whose importance depended entirely upon what lay beneath it. The power of the object resided not simply in the carving but in the continuing relationship between ancestors, descendants and sacred relics.

This distinction also shapes current debates about provenance and interpretation. Even when no human remains survive in museum collections, curators increasingly recognise that displaying only the guardian risks presenting a devotional object as a purely aesthetic masterpiece. Explaining the missing basket, the absent bones and the disrupted ritual context helps restore part of the meaning that was lost when the reliquary was dismantled.

Within Gabon's landscape of unusual collections, these incomplete reliquary guardians are therefore unusual not because they are mysterious objects, but because what is missing is as historically significant as what remains on display.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
