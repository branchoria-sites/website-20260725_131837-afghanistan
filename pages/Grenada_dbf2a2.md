## Introduction

## The museum beneath the sea

Molinere Underwater Sculpture Park is Grenada’s clearest claim to a place on the world map of strange museums. Created by sculptor Jason deCaires Taylor off the west coast near Molinere Bay, it opened in 2006 as the first public underwater sculpture park of its kind. Its works lie among sand patches, gullies and natural rock formations, meaning that visitors encounter them by snorkelling, diving or viewing them from a glass-bottomed boat rather than walking through galleries. The artist’s project site currently describes roughly 75 works spread across about 800 square metres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://underwatersculpture.com/projects/molinere-underwater-sculpture-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: underwatersculpture.com">[Underwater Sculpture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">underwatersculpture.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">molinere underwater sculpture park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Underwater SculptureMolinere Underwater Sculpture ParkIn 2024, Carriacou opened its first underwater sculpture park, A World Adrift, afte...</span></span></span>

The park is unusual not simply because statues have been placed underwater, but because submersion is part of the artwork. The surfaces are intended to change as algae, sponges, corals and other organisms colonise them. Human faces blur, limbs acquire marine growth and scenes that initially resemble eerie archaeological ruins gradually become living reef structures. Taylor used marine-suitable materials and designed the installations to support habitat formation while drawing some visitor pressure away from natural reefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://underwatersculpture.com/coral-carnival/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: underwatersculpture.com">[Underwater Sculpture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">underwatersculpture.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">coral carnival</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Underwater SculptureThe Coral CarnivalIn 2024, Carriacou opened its first underwater sculpture park, A World Adrift, after destruction of...</span></span></span>

### Coral Carnival

The Molinere collection was expanded with *Coral Carnival*, a group of works drawing on Grenada’s carnival traditions. The artist’s project information identifies the installation as commissioned and funded by Grenada’s Ministry of Tourism and the World Bank, using materials including pH-neutral geopolymer cement, marine-grade stainless steel, calcium carbonate and natural pigments. The development makes the park more locally specific: the figures are not merely anonymous bodies but participants in a recognisable tradition of masquerade, costume and public performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://underwatersculpture.com/coral-carnival/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: underwatersculpture.com">[Underwater Sculpture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">underwatersculpture.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">coral carnival</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Underwater SculptureThe Coral CarnivalIn 2024, Carriacou opened its first underwater sculpture park, A World Adrift, after destruction of...</span></span></span>

This matters because underwater sculpture parks can easily become picturesque backdrops detached from the places hosting them. Incorporating carnival imagery gives Grenada’s collection a stronger relationship to living culture. Even so, it remains a tourist-facing artwork rather than an ethnographic museum of masquerade. Visitors interested in the meanings of particular characters will need interpretation beyond the visual encounter itself.

## A new fleet off Carriacou

Grenada’s underwater collection expanded beyond the main island in October 2024 with *A World Adrift*, installed off Jack-A-Dan Island near [Carriacou]({{ 'carriacou/' | relative_url }}). The work consists of 30 small boat sculptures, each navigated by the figure of a local schoolchild. The boats represent young people travelling through an uncertain future, with climate displacement, vulnerability and resilience built into the installation’s meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://underwatersculpture.com/projects/a-world-adrift/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: underwatersculpture.com">[Underwater Sculpture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">underwatersculpture.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Underwater Sculpture A World Adrift</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Underwater Sculpture A World Adrift</span></span></span>

The timing gave the project an unexpectedly direct force. Hurricane Beryl made landfall on Carriacou on 1 July 2024 as a Category 4 hurricane, causing severe destruction across the island and neighbouring Petite Martinique. The installation had been delayed by the disaster before eventually opening later that year. An artwork already concerned with threatened futures therefore entered the sea beside a community confronting the immediate consequences of an extreme storm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/carriacou-after-beryl-153039/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Carriacou After Beryl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Carriacou After Beryl</span></span></span>

*A World Adrift* is not a collection of wreckage or memorial objects recovered after Beryl. It is a planned contemporary installation whose interpretation was sharpened by events. That distinction is important. The work is credible as art and public environmental commentary, but its symbolic boats should not be confused with archaeological remains, actual migrant vessels or documentary evidence of the hurricane.

Taken together, Molinere and Carriacou have turned Grenada’s coastal waters into an evolving national collection. The sea is simultaneously gallery, conservation environment and source of physical change. Few countries have made underwater art so central to their offbeat visitor identity.

## Grenada National Museum: artefacts, revolution and an old prison

The Grenada National Museum in St George’s has traditionally provided the broadest physical collection of the country’s unusual historical material. Its displays have covered Indigenous settlement, European colonisation, African enslavement, plantation production, early transport, fishing, whaling, independence, the Grenada Revolution and the killing of Prime Minister Maurice Bishop. Items associated with sugar and rum production sit beside archaeological pottery, maritime equipment, photographs and political documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.puregrenada.com/grenada-national-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: puregrenada.com">[Grenada Tourism Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">puregrenada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada Tourism AuthorityGrenada National MuseumOn the inside the museum displays historical artefacts and collections focusing on the fi...</span></span></span>

The building itself contributes to the atmosphere. Parts of the complex date from the colonial period and have served at different times as military barracks, a hotel and a warehouse. Behind the museum are the remains of the old prison and Drill Yard. The site was used as a court and common gaol during French rule, continued in penal use after the British takeover, and was later associated with military drilling. It is therefore not a purpose-built neutral gallery but a layered place in which colonial government, imprisonment, hospitality and collecting have occupied adjoining spaces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.gd/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.gd">[Grenada National Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.gd.</span></span></span>

Among its more curious reported objects are equipment from Grenada’s former whaling industry, an old rum still and a marble bath traditionally associated with Joséphine de Beauharnais, later Empress of the French. The bath attribution is best treated cautiously. It is an example of the kind of object whose appeal depends upon a personal connection to a famous historical figure, yet “said to have been used by” is not the same as a fully demonstrated chain of ownership.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grenada_National_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada National Museum</span></span></span>

The whaling material has firmer importance within Grenada’s collection history. It records an industry that is now unfamiliar to many visitors and links the main island to the maritime traditions of Carriacou and the Grenadines. Implements that might look like sinister weapons when isolated in a case make more sense when interpreted through labour, subsistence, trade and changing attitudes towards marine animals.

Current access information requires caution. The museum’s own website was displaying “Closed Permanently” when checked, while its social-media material indicated a temporary closure from 24 December 2025 until 31 January 2026. Other recent visitor information has referred to renovation closures and reopening announcements. ## Carriacou Museum and the fragility of island collections

Carriacou Museum, operated by the Carriacou Historical Society in Hillsborough, has preserved the history and traditions of Carriacou and Petite Martinique. Its importance lies in collecting material that could easily disappear from a small island community: Indigenous artefacts, plantation history, African-derived traditions, domestic objects and evidence of maritime livelihoods. The museum building itself has been associated with the island’s cotton-processing past, adding industrial heritage to the collection’s archaeological and social themes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.carriacoumuseum.gd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: carriacoumuseum.gd">[Carriacou Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">carriacoumuseum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on carriacoumuseum.gd.</span></span></span>

For enthusiasts of strange collections, its most compelling feature is the meeting of several histories that are often separated in larger institutions. Pre-Columbian objects, colonial plantation material, seafaring traditions and local cultural practices belong to different periods, yet on Carriacou they all help explain how a small island maintained a distinct identity through migration, enslavement, trade and environmental risk.

Hurricane Beryl seriously damaged the museum in July 2024. The Carriacou Historical Society subsequently described a rebuilding effort intended not merely to repair the structure but to restore a cultural centre for the community. A July 2025 visitor account reported that it remained closed almost a year after the hurricane, and the museum’s own April 2025 update concentrated on reconstruction rather than announcing normal reopening. Its present visitor status should therefore be checked directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.carriacoumuseum.gd/post/rebuilding-history-carriacou-museum-rises-again" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: carriacoumuseum.gd">[Carriacou Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">carriacoumuseum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carriacou Museum Rebuilding History: Carriacou Museum Rises Again</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carriacou Museum Rebuilding History: Carriacou Museum Rises Again</span></span></span>

The closure highlights a central issue in Grenada’s curiosity culture: preservation is vulnerable to climate as well as finance. In a large national institution, displaced collections may be transferred to another controlled store. On a small island badly damaged by a hurricane, the museum, the homes of staff and the wider infrastructure can all be affected simultaneously. The survival of the collection becomes part of the community’s disaster recovery.

## Faces carved beside the river

The Mt Rich Carib Stone Interpretation Centre in St Patrick offers a different kind of collection. Rather than moving archaeological material into display cases, it interprets more than 60 carvings on boulders beside the St Patrick River. Visitors view the petroglyphs in their landscape and can learn about nearby archaeological remains, abandoned plantation estates, local plants and village history through the community organisation MYCEDO.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mycedo.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mycedo.org">[MyCedo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mycedo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MYCEDOThe Interpretation Center provides information and a scenic view of the Mt. The carved faces and geometric forms are among Grenada’s most genuinely mysterious surviving objects, but “mysterious” should not be allowed to become a licence for fantasy. The makers did not leave a written explanation, and the precise meanings of individual designs are unknown. Archaeologists place the carvings within the pre-Columbian Indigenous history of the Caribbean; suggested ritual, ancestral or spiritual functions remain interpretations rather than translations of a known symbolic code.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._Rich_Petroglyphs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mt. Rich Petroglyphs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mt. Rich Petroglyphs</span></span></span>

Their location near water is significant because Caribbean rock-art sites frequently occur beside rivers, pools or other water sources. Researchers have proposed connections with spiritually important places, ancestors and ceremonial activity. Claims that a particular face represents a named deity, extraterrestrial visitor or lost civilisation go beyond the available evidence unless supported by a clear archaeological argument.

The centre’s community-led approach also makes it different from a conventional archaeological museum. Its value comes from keeping the stones in place and connecting them to the surrounding village. The disadvantage is exposure: rock art in a river environment is vulnerable to erosion, flooding, movement, touching and accidental damage. Grenada’s heritage legislation protects designated Indigenous artwork and archaeological remains, but practical safeguarding depends upon local stewardship and consistent enforcement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/grn214478.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faolex.fao.org">[FAOLEX Database]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faolex.fao.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fao.org.</span></span></span>

## Chocolate, rum and commercial curiosity displays

Grenada’s unusual collections are not confined to public heritage institutions. Several agricultural attractions combine working production with small museums or interpretation displays. They are more commercial than the National Museum, but they preserve machinery, techniques and stories that explain why spices, cocoa, sugar and rum occupy such a large place in the country’s identity.

The House of Chocolate in St George’s openly describes itself as a handcrafted “mini-museum”, café and boutique. Its displays introduce the history of cocoa and chocolate, while the business sells locally made bars, drinks, cakes and other products. Visitors should expect a compact commercial interpretation space rather than a large scholarly collection. Its strength is accessibility: the relationship between exhibit, raw ingredient and finished product can be tasted immediately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.houseofchocolategnd.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: houseofchocolategnd.com">[houseofchocolategnd.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">houseofchocolategnd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">House of Chocolate</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">House of Chocolate</span></span></span>

Belmont Estate offers a larger agricultural heritage experience on a working estate dating to the seventeenth century. Tours connect cocoa growing and chocolate production with farm buildings, machinery, gardens and a heritage museum. The site presents itself as an agri-tourism business that has sought to transform a plantation property into a locally owned enterprise focused on employment, agriculture and environmental responsibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://belmontestategrenada.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belmontestategrenada.com">[belmontestategrenada.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belmontestategrenada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on belmontestategrenada.com.</span></span></span>

The difficult question at any plantation attraction is how clearly it explains the human system behind the picturesque buildings and antique equipment. Cocoa trays, sugar machinery and estate ledgers are not merely charming relics of old-fashioned production. They belong to histories of colonisation, land ownership, forced labour and unequal trade. A strong visit should distinguish the ingenuity of agricultural techniques from nostalgia for plantation society.

River Antoine Rum Distillery is not primarily a museum, yet its historic machinery gives it the character of a working industrial collection. The estate continues to use a large waterwheel to power cane crushing, while traditional fermentation and distillation equipment allow visitors to see processes that elsewhere survive only as static exhibits. The result is closer to living industrial heritage than to a polished visitor centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/best-things-to-do-in-grenada-0hthtf5nt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times20 of the best things to do in Grenada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Times20 of the best things to do in Grenada</span></span></span>

These commercial sites vary in curatorial depth. They can be excellent at demonstrating how objects work and how products smell or taste, but they may devote less space to contested history than a public museum would. Visitors interested in more than food tourism should look for tours that discuss labour, ownership, slavery, environmental change and the difference between historical plantation production and today’s locally controlled businesses.

## What Grenada does not have

There is little reliable evidence of a dedicated public museum of witchcraft, ghosts, UFOs, cryptids, medical specimens or criminal curiosities operating in Grenada. Folklore remains culturally important, but it is more often carried through oral tradition, carnival, music, storytelling and beliefs attached to particular landscapes than gathered into a permanent specialist museum.

Grand Etang Lake, for example, is associated in popular accounts with a dangerous mermaid-like being said to lure people into the water. Such stories form part of the island’s uncanny geography, but the lake is a natural and folkloric site, not a museum collection. Treating every legendary place as an “attraction” risks flattening living belief into tourist horror. The useful distinction is between a story told about a place and an institution that collects, verifies or interprets material connected with that story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/best-things-to-do-in-grenada-0hthtf5nt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times20 of the best things to do in Grenada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Times20 of the best things to do in Grenada</span></span></span>

Grenada’s most compelling strange heritage therefore lies elsewhere: in objects that cross boundaries. Underwater statues become reefs; carved stones remain both archaeological evidence and unresolved images; whaling tools sit within a national story of sea work; and old plantation machinery functions simultaneously as technology, commercial heritage and evidence of colonial power.

## How to judge the collections

The country’s unusual sites range from formally protected archaeology to contemporary art and retail-led interpretation. Visitors can understand them more clearly by asking what kind of authority each place possesses.

**Documented contemporary art:** Molinere and *A World Adrift* have named artists, commissioning histories and recorded installation dates. Their symbolism is open to interpretation, but their origin is not mysterious.

**Archaeological heritage:** The Mt Rich carvings are authentic pre-Columbian rock art, yet their exact meanings remain uncertain. Responsible interpretation separates archaeological context from speculation.

**Historical collections:** The national and Carriacou museums preserve objects whose value depends on cataloguing, provenance and careful explanation. Famous-person associations deserve particular scrutiny when documentation is weak.

**Working heritage:** Belmont Estate and River Antoine demonstrate historic processes using functioning sites and machinery. Their commercial nature does not make them inauthentic, but it shapes which stories receive the greatest emphasis.

**Retail interpretation:** House of Chocolate is best understood as a small visitor display attached to a café and shop. It offers a useful introduction, not an exhaustive or academically neutral history of cocoa.

This varied landscape is precisely what makes Grenada distinctive. Its curiosity culture is not dominated by cabinets of monsters or shelves of inexplicable artefacts. Instead, unusual objects are embedded in the sea, rivers, plantations and damaged historic buildings. The strongest sites make those surroundings part of the interpretation rather than treating heritage as something that exists only behind glass.
