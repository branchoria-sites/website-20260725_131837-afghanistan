## Introduction

[Jamaica]({{ 'where-jamaicas-legends-meet-lost/' | relative_url }})’s natural history collections preserve a record of animals and plants that can no longer be seen alive on the island. The most important collection for this story is the Natural History Museum of Jamaica (NHMJ) in Kingston, which holds thousands of biological specimens, including examples of extinct Jamaican species such as the Jamaican giant galliwasp, the Jamaican petrel and Sloane’s urania moth. These objects are not curiosities in the traditional “cabinet of wonders” sense; they are scientific evidence of lost ecosystems, changing landscapes and the consequences of human activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhmj-ioj.org.jm/our-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhmj-ioj.org.jm">[Natural History Museum of Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhmj-ioj.org.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum of Jamaica -Our Collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum of JamaicaApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>

## Extinct Jamaican species in collections

### The Jamaican giant galliwasp: a lost island reptile

One of Jamaica’s most remarkable extinct animals is the Jamaican giant galliwasp (*Celestus occiduus*), a large secretive lizard that once lived in Jamaican forests and wetlands. The species has not been confirmed alive since the 19th century and is generally considered extinct or possibly extinct. Its disappearance is linked to habitat loss and introduced predators, including mongooses that were brought to Jamaica during the colonial period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaican_giant_galliwasp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jamaican giant galliwasp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jamaican giant galliwasp</span></span></span>

The galliwasp is especially significant because surviving specimens became the only physical evidence of an animal that vanished from Jamaica’s landscapes. Natural history collections around the world hold examples, but recent efforts have focused on reconnecting these specimens with their country of origin. In 2024, a preserved galliwasp specimen held by the University of Glasgow’s Hunterian Museum was returned to Jamaica through collaboration involving the University of the West Indies and Jamaican institutions, where it was deposited with the Natural History Museum of Jamaica.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumsgalleriesscotland.org.uk/sector-story/return-of-the-galliwasp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumsgalleriesscotland.org.uk">[Museums Galleries Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumsgalleriesscotland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museums Galleries Scotland Return of the Galliwasp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museums Galleries ScotlandReturn of the Galliwasp - Museums Galleries Scotland...</span></span></span>

This return was not simply about moving an old specimen from one museum to another. It highlighted a wider question in natural history: who should hold important biological heritage, especially when specimens were collected during colonial periods and removed from their original regions? The galliwasp became both a scientific object and a symbol of Jamaica reclaiming part of its natural history record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/reparation-deal-sends-museums-extinct-lizard-home-to-jamaica-jr830r0n9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The reptile, which vanished due to habitat destruction and introduced predators, was collected in the mid-19th century and has been part...</span></span></span>

### Sloane’s urania: the vanished Jamaican moth

Another striking specimen in Jamaican collections is Sloane’s urania (*Urania sloanus*), a large day-flying moth once found only in Jamaica. Unlike many moths, which are associated with darkness and camouflage, this species was known for its bright colours and unusual appearance. It was recorded as disappearing in the late 19th century, with habitat destruction and the loss of its food plants considered major factors in its decline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.extinction.photo/species/sloanes-urania/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: extinction.photo">[Extinction]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">extinction.photo</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sloane&#x27;s Urania</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sloane&#x27;s Urania - Extinction...</span></span></span>

The NHMJ’s zoological collections include a specimen of this extinct moth, preserving a physical link to a species that existed for centuries but could not survive the transformation of parts of the Jamaican landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhmj-ioj.org.jm/zoology/peek-in-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhmj-ioj.org.jm">[Natural History Museum of Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhmj-ioj.org.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum of Jamaica -Peek In Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum of JamaicaApril 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2025</span></span></span> Its story demonstrates why museum specimens matter: even when an animal disappears, collections allow scientists to study its appearance, distribution and ecological relationships.

### The Jamaican petrel and the problem of disappearing island species

The Jamaican petrel (*Pterodroma caribbaea*) is another extinct species represented in discussions of Jamaica’s lost biodiversity. A seabird once associated with Jamaica’s mountainous regions, it became rare as habitats changed and threats increased. The species is now considered extinct, although historical records and specimens remain important for understanding its former range and biology. The NHMJ identifies the Jamaican petrel among extinct species represented within its zoological holdings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhmj-ioj.org.jm/our-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhmj-ioj.org.jm">[Natural History Museum of Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhmj-ioj.org.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum of Jamaica -Our Collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum of JamaicaApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>

Island species are particularly vulnerable because they often evolve in isolation. Jamaica’s wildlife includes many endemic species — animals found nowhere else — meaning that when one disappears, a unique part of global biodiversity is lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhmj-ioj.org.jm/do-you-know/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhmj-ioj.org.jm">[Natural History Museum of Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhmj-ioj.org.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum of Jamaica -Do You Know</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum of JamaicaApril 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2025</span></span></span>

## Scientific specimens and conservation stories

The Natural History Museum of Jamaica is not only a display space; it functions as a national scientific archive. Its collections include more than 110,000 animal specimens covering insects, spiders, molluscs, crustaceans, amphibians, fish, reptiles, birds and mammals, alongside a botanical collection of more than 130,000 plant specimens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhmj-ioj.org.jm/zoology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhmj-ioj.org.jm">[Natural History Museum of Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhmj-ioj.org.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum of Jamaica -Zoology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum of JamaicaAugust 12, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 12, 2024</span></span></span>

For extinction research, these collections work like historical records. A preserved specimen can show where a species lived, what it looked like, and sometimes how populations changed over time. Scientists can compare older museum material with living relatives to investigate evolution, disease, habitat change and conservation priorities.

The collections also help prevent future losses. Jamaica remains home to many rare species, including endemic birds, frogs and reptiles. By documenting existing wildlife, museums create a baseline against which future declines can be measured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhmj-ioj.org.jm/do-you-know/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhmj-ioj.org.jm">[Natural History Museum of Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhmj-ioj.org.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum of Jamaica -Do You Know</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum of JamaicaApril 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2025</span></span></span>

A particularly important lesson from Jamaica’s extinct species is that extinction is often gradual rather than dramatic. The galliwasp did not vanish in a single event; the urania moth declined as habitats changed; the Jamaican petrel disappeared after long-term pressures. Museum specimens preserve the final chapters of these stories, allowing researchers and the public to understand how environmental changes affect island ecosystems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.extinction.photo/species/sloanes-urania/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: extinction.photo">[Extinction]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">extinction.photo</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sloane&#x27;s Urania</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sloane&#x27;s Urania - Extinction...</span></span></span>

## Returning important specimens to Jamaica

### Why repatriation matters for natural history


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The return of the Jamaican giant galliwasp specimen showed that natural history collections are increasingly part of conversations about heritage and ownership. ### A changing role for Jamaican collections

The Natural History Museum of Jamaica represents a shift from the old idea of museums as places where rare objects are simply stored. Today, extinct species collections are tools for understanding environmental change. The preserved remains of lost animals are warnings as much as they are exhibits.

For visitors, these collections offer an unusual kind of encounter: a chance to stand before evidence of creatures that once shared Jamaica’s forests, wetlands and skies. The fascination comes not from the impossible, but from the reality that a small preserved specimen can hold the story of an entire vanished world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhmj-ioj.org.jm/our-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhmj-ioj.org.jm">[Natural History Museum of Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhmj-ioj.org.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum of Jamaica -Our Collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum of JamaicaApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>
