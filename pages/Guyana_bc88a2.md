## Introduction

Georgetown contains most of the established museums, while several of the more eccentric collections sit in private homes or at difficult-to-reach heritage sites. Documentation and visitor information can be uneven, so opening arrangements should be confirmed directly. What makes Guyana distinctive is precisely this mixture of formal state curation, community memory, inherited objects and collections assembled through individual obsession.

## The National Museum’s giant sloth and taxidermy

The Guyana National Museum is the clearest starting point for visitors seeking physical oddities. Its collections combine archaeology, minerals, historical material, Indigenous crafts and specimens of Guyanese flora and fauna. The institution grew from collections established during the colonial period, although its present building opened in the mid-twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntg.gov.gy/monument/guyana-national-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntg.gov.gy">[ntg.gov.gy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntg.gov.gy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Guyana National Museum – National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 5, 2022 — # Guyana National Museum... The model was created using fossil evidence and unveiled in 2010 as the “Mega-T” [giant sloth]({{ 'giant-sloth/' | relative_url }}). Giant ground sloths disappeared thousands of years ago, and the elephant-sized reconstruction gives an immediate sense of how unfamiliar Guyana’s prehistoric animal life would have appeared. ## Indigenous collections without the exotic fantasy

The Walter Roth Museum of Anthropology holds Guyana’s most important visitor-facing collection relating to Indigenous peoples. Established in the 1970s and opened to the public in 1982, it occupies a historic timber building on Main Street in Georgetown. Its archaeological holdings include material excavated across all ten administrative regions, while its ethnographic collections document tools, crafts, clothing, domestic practices and ceremonial life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntg.gov.gy/monument/walter-roth-museum-of-anthropology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntg.gov.gy">[ntg.gov.gy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntg.gov.gy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">walter roth museum of anthropology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walter roth museum of anthropology</span></span></span>

For readers interested in shamanism, transformation beliefs or forest spirits, this is the institution most likely to provide the necessary cultural grounding. Yet it should not be approached as an occult museum. Objects that outsiders might label magical, mysterious or “tribal” belong to living societies and systems of knowledge. Their significance may involve healing, hunting, ancestry, social authority or relationships with animals and landscapes, rather than a simple division between religion and superstition.

That distinction matters in Guyana, where Indigenous heritage is often filtered through the fantasies of explorers, missionaries and colonial collectors. A feathered object, carved figure or ritual implement can easily be turned into exotic scenery when removed from the community that made it. The museum’s value lies in reconnecting artefacts with named peoples, historical periods and forms of daily life, although visitors have sometimes found the displays text-heavy and the interpretation dependent on careful reading rather than theatrical presentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.com/Attraction_Review-g294078-d4008424-Reviews-Walter_Roth_Museum_of_Anthropology-Georgetown_Demerara_Mahaica.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.com">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Walter Roth Museum of Anthropology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walter Roth Museum of Anthropology</span></span></span>

The museum has also been the subject of debate over institutional control. A 2016 proposal to transfer its collection to the National Museum prompted objections and was paused pending consultation with museum staff and Indigenous leaders. The dispute revealed that the building and collection were not merely tourist assets: they were tied to questions of cultural authority, preservation and who should decide how Indigenous history is displayed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpi.gov.gy/walter-roth-museums-relocation-on-hold/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpi.gov.gy">[Department of Public Information, Guyana]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpi.gov.gy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Public Information, Guyana Walter Roth Museum&#x27;s relocation on hold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of Public Information, Guyana Walter Roth Museum&#x27;s relocation on hold</span></span></span>

## Ancestral objects, masquerade and folklore

The Museum of African Heritage in Georgetown offers another route into material that may appear mysterious to an unfamiliar visitor. Its purpose is to collect, preserve and interpret art, records and artefacts connected with Africa and the African experience in Guyana. Its development was linked to post-colonial cultural nationalism and to the recovery of histories that had long been marginalised in official institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325866820_Inherited_Ancestors%27_Collections_of_a_Devoted_Curator_The_Museum_of_African_Heritage_in_Georgetown_Guyana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate The Museum of African Heritage in Georgetown, Guyana</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate The Museum of African Heritage in Georgetown, Guyana</span></span></span>

The museum’s importance is not simply that it contains African-looking objects. It explores how objects, stories and identities travelled and changed across the Atlantic. Its collections have expanded to include African and Caribbean art, material relating to prominent Afro-Guyanese figures, and interpretation of events including the 1763 Berbice uprising. In this setting, masks, carvings and ceremonial forms are evidence of cultural memory rather than props for an undefined idea of “dark magic”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://guyanatimesgy.com/museums-foster-development/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guyanatimesgy.com">[Guyana Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guyanatimesgy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Guyana Times Museums foster development</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guyana Times Museums foster development</span></span></span>

Guyana’s masquerade tradition offers a particularly vivid connection between collection and performance. Masquerade bands, associated especially with the Christmas season, combine drums, costumes, dance, satire and characters inherited and reshaped through African-Caribbean history. Museum interpretation outside Guyana has documented Guyanese instruments and testimony describing spiritual meanings attached to drums, including ideas that a drum may embody the tree, the animal skin and the maker. Such beliefs are not proof of supernatural activity; they show how an instrument can be understood simultaneously as a crafted object, a bearer of ancestry and an active presence in performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.horniman.ac.uk/plan-your-visit/around-the-horniman/afrikan-caribbean-masquerade/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horniman.ac.uk">[Horniman Museum and Gardens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horniman.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Horniman Museum and Gardens Afrikan-Caribbean Masquerade</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horniman Museum and Gardens Afrikan-Caribbean Masquerade</span></span></span>

There is no prominent permanent “jumbie museum” devoted to Guyanese ghosts or supernatural beings. Folklore instead appears intermittently in exhibitions, oral tradition, performance and community storytelling. That fragmented survival is culturally revealing. Spirits and legendary figures have not been separated neatly from domestic life and placed behind glass; they continue through stories, warnings, humour and seasonal customs.

## Private collections and personal obsession

The Guyana Heritage Museum at Met-en-Meerzorg, west of Georgetown, is closer to a traditional cabinet of curiosities. It is a privately developed collection associated with collector Gary Serrao and housed in an expanded domestic building. Accounts describe an eclectic accumulation of maps, pamphlets, books, stamps, coins, bottles, trade objects and miscellaneous Guyanese memorabilia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://guyanatimesgy.com/museums-foster-development/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guyanatimesgy.com">[Guyana Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guyanatimesgy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Guyana Times Museums foster development</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guyana Times Museums foster development</span></span></span>

Among the frequently cited highlights are an early seventeenth-century map, old glass trade beads, a rare shaft-and-globe bottle and nineteenth-century plans of Georgetown streets. The appeal lies partly in the individual objects, but also in their density. Unlike a national museum arranged into broad departments, this is the visible result of one person repeatedly deciding that an overlooked scrap of Guyana’s past should not be discarded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stabroeknews.com/2013/03/24/features/man-on-a-mission/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stabroeknews.com">[Stabroek News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stabroeknews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stabroek News Man on a mission</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stabroek News Man on a mission</span></span></span>

Private museums of this type can be intimate and memorable, although their interpretation may be less standardised than that of a state institution. Provenance—the documented history of where an object came from—can vary from item to item, and display choices may reflect the collector’s personal interests. That does not make the collection untrustworthy, but visitors should distinguish between a compelling story attached to an object and a claim supported by archival records.

The Roy Geddes Steel Pan Museum presents a different version of the collector’s house. Created by celebrated steelpan musician and maker Roy Geddes, it preserves instruments, photographs, awards and memorabilia connected with his career and the development of pan music in Guyana. Reports describe the collection extending through the ground floor and stairway of the family home, with visits shaped by conversation with Geddes and his wife rather than by impersonal display labels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rhythmnroots.gy/legends/roy-geddes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rhythmnroots.gy">[Guyana Rhythm &amp; Roots]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rhythmnroots.gy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rhythmnroots.gy.</span></span></span>

It is unusual because it blurs museum, workshop, archive and living room. The central subject is not supernatural, but the collection demonstrates how an apparently ordinary object—a metal drum fashioned through specialist tuning—can carry experimentation, performance history and national pride. It is also unusually personal: the maker, performer and museum subject have, at least historically, been present within the same domestic space.

## Police objects and colonial remains

The John Campbell Police Museum, within the Guyana Police Force complex at Eve Leary in Georgetown, is the country’s closest equivalent to a crime museum. It preserves institutional records, equipment and objects connected with policing. An older local account described it as a neglected “repository of things police”, suggesting that it had considerable historical value but little visibility among the general public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://guyanapoliceforce.gy/?page_id=2062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guyanapoliceforce.gy">[Guyana Police Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guyanapoliceforce.gy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on guyanapoliceforce.gy.</span></span></span>

Police museums naturally attract curiosity because mundane objects can become unsettling when connected with arrest, punishment or violence. Their limitations are equally important. An institution maintained by a police force generally presents history from the organisation’s point of view. Visitors should therefore treat displays as evidence of policing practice and official memory, not necessarily as a complete account of crime, colonial authority or the experiences of people subjected to law enforcement.

Farther from Georgetown, the Dutch Heritage Museum on Fort Island occupies the former Court of Policy and church building near Fort Zeelandia. Opened in 2007, it interprets Dutch colonial government and the history of the Essequibo River settlement. The building and fort give the site an atmosphere that conventional display cases alone could not create: isolation, river travel, thick masonry and surviving colonial architecture make the landscape itself part of the collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntg.gov.gy/monument/dutch-heritage-museum-court-of-policy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntg.gov.gy">[ntg.gov.gy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntg.gov.gy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dutch Heritage Museum (Court of Policy) – National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dutch Heritage Museum (Court of Policy) – National Trust</span></span></span>

This is not a dungeon attraction constructed for thrills. Its strangeness comes from encountering administrative and military remnants in a remote setting where imperial ambitions once depended on forts, plantations, waterways and coerced labour. Artefacts and architecture should therefore be read critically. They preserve the material confidence of colonial rule while often leaving the lives of enslaved and Indigenous people less physically visible.

## Jonestown and the ethics of dark tourism

Jonestown is Guyana’s most internationally notorious strange-history site, but it is not a conventional museum. On 18 November 1978, more than 900 members of Peoples Temple died at the agricultural commune founded by Jim Jones in Guyana’s north-west. Many were murdered or coerced, including children; US Congressman Leo Ryan and four others were killed at the nearby Port Kaituma airstrip. The event is frequently reduced to the phrase “drank the Kool-Aid”, a cliché that obscures coercion, abuse and the fact that the drink used was another brand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/999815cf150f8a0b04f32b009c6f5641" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

For decades, the location remained difficult to reach and largely reclaimed by vegetation. Reports described only fragments of structures, machinery and a rusting tractor surviving at the former settlement. Until recently, visitors were mainly journalists, researchers and relatives of the dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/999815cf150f8a0b04f32b009c6f5641" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

That changed when a private operator, with support from Guyana’s tourism authorities, began marketing overnight “Jonestown Memorial Tours”. An inaugural organised visit was reported in January 2025, and tours continue to be advertised as reflective journeys including the settlement grounds and pavilion area. The development has effectively turned a ruin into a visitor-facing collection of landscape, fragments, testimony and reconstructed narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wanderlustgy.com/product/jonestown-memorial-tour/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wanderlustgy.com">[wanderlustgy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wanderlustgy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wanderlustgy.com.</span></span></span>

The controversy is not about whether Jonestown should be remembered, but about who controls the story and who benefits financially. Supporters argue that guided access can correct myths, teach the dangers of authoritarian leadership and acknowledge an event long avoided in Guyana. Critics, including some survivors and commentators, fear exploitation, sensationalism and the conversion of mass death into a tourism product. Survivors themselves have expressed differing views, ranging from guarded support for education and memorialisation to opposition to commercial use of the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/dec/09/guyana-jonestown-massacre-site-tour" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Concern as Guyana considers opening Jonestown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Concern as Guyana considers opening Jonestown</span></span></span>

Any credible Jonestown interpretation should centre victims rather than the charisma of Jim Jones. It should explain racial composition, coercion, child deaths, forced labour, failed escape attempts and the wider political circumstances, while resisting lurid staging or souvenir culture. Until a permanent, professionally curated memorial or museum exists at the site, the quality of the experience will depend heavily on the operator, guide and sources used.

## What visitors can realistically expect

Guyana rewards the curious traveller, but its unusual collections rarely offer polished spectacle. The strongest experiences are small, object-led and historically layered. The National Museum provides the most accessible physical oddities; Walter Roth supplies essential Indigenous context; the Museum of African Heritage connects material culture with ancestry and resistance; private museums reveal the force of individual collecting; and Jonestown raises difficult questions about memory becoming a product.

Several practical distinctions help when judging a site:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **State museums** generally provide the broadest historical coverage, although displays and visitor information may be dated.
* **Academic or anthropological collections** can offer stronger context but may require slow reading and sensitivity to contested colonial collecting practices.
* **Private museums** are often more eccentric and personal, but opening times and documentation may be inconsistent.
* **Heritage ruins** preserve atmosphere and physical evidence, yet interpretation may depend almost entirely on a guide.
* **Commercial dark-tourism experiences** should be assessed by how they treat victims, evidence and local communities, not by how dramatic their marketing appears.

</div>

Guyana’s curiosity culture is best understood not as a hidden network of monster museums, but as a scattered archive of encounters: extinct animals reconstructed from fossils, spirits preserved through performance rather than specimens, ancestral objects reclaimed from colonial categories, police and fort collections shaped by institutional power, and a twentieth-century catastrophe still struggling to find an ethical public form.
