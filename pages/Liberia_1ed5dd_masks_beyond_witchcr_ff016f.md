## Introduction

Liberian masks are often misunderstood outside West Africa. Travel writing, colonial-era accounts and even some museum labels have too easily grouped them under the vague heading of "witchcraft". In reality, many Liberian masks belong to structured systems of education, public authority, initiation and ceremonial performance. They are not simply magical objects, nor are they meaningful when separated from the dancers, costumes, music and communities that bring them to life. Scholarly research and modern Liberian heritage organisations increasingly emphasise that a mask is only one element within a much broader cultural performance, and that reducing it to "witchcraft" obscures its social, artistic and historical importance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lnta.gov.lr/16-tribal-masks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lnta.gov.lr">[lnta.gov.lr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lnta.gov.lr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liberia National Tourism Authority16 Tribal Masks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liberia National Tourism Authority...</span></span></span>

Understanding this distinction is especially important for anyone encountering Liberian masks in museums. A carved wooden face displayed in a glass case may once have been part of a complete masquerade involving elaborate fibre costumes, drumming, dance and recognised community authority. Without that context, visitors can easily mistake a living cultural tradition for an exotic curiosity.

## Sande and Poro masquerade traditions

Across parts of [Liberia]({{ 'where-did-liberias-strangest-collections/' | relative_url }}), especially among peoples of the Upper Guinea forest region, masks are associated with organised institutions rather than isolated acts of ritual. Two of the best-known are the women's Sande society and the men's Poro society. Although practices differ between ethnic communities and have changed over time, both have historically been connected with education, initiation, moral instruction and community governance rather than a single religious doctrine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sfomuseum.org/fil/exhibitions/liberian-helmet-masks-sande-and-poro-societies-collections-phoebe-hearst-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sfomuseum.org">[sfomuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sfomuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sfomuseum.org.</span></span></span>

Rather than appearing randomly, masked performers traditionally emerge during recognised public occasions. These may include initiation periods, celebrations, ceremonies marking social transitions or events where community values are publicly reinforced. The masked figure represents an authorised role within the performance rather than an individual displaying artistic skill.

Several important points are often overlooked:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* Masks function together with costume, music and dance, not as independent artworks.
* Performances communicate ideas about responsibility, discipline, maturity and social identity.
* Individual communities maintain their own styles, meanings and performance rules rather than sharing one universal Liberian masking tradition.
* Many masks continue to belong to living cultural traditions rather than surviving only as historical artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lnta.gov.lr/16-tribal-masks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lnta.gov.lr">[lnta.gov.lr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lnta.gov.lr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liberia National Tourism Authority16 Tribal Masks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liberia National Tourism Authority...</span></span></span>

</div>

This broader framework explains why many Liberians object when ceremonial masks are described simply as objects of magic or superstition.

## How performance changes a mask's meaning

A museum visitor naturally focuses on the carved wood. Within Liberian masquerade traditions, however, the carving is only one component of a much larger event.

The performer is normally concealed beneath extensive fibre or cloth costumes that hide the human body. Music, rhythm and movement are equally important, transforming the carved object into a recognised public presence. The audience is not simply watching someone wearing a mask in the modern theatrical sense; the performance creates a socially recognised identity with specific authority and responsibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sfomuseum.org/fil/exhibitions/liberian-helmet-masks-sande-and-poro-societies-collections-phoebe-hearst-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sfomuseum.org">[sfomuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sfomuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sfomuseum.org.</span></span></span>

This explains why detached museum displays can unintentionally flatten meaning. A helmet mask resting on a stand may appear to be a sculpture, yet historically it was designed to move through space, respond to music and interact with a community that understood its symbolism.

Scholars of Liberian masquerades have therefore argued that studying the carving alone gives only part of the story. The performance, costume, sound and social setting are inseparable from the object's significance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/siris_sil_663352" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

## Women, authority and a common misunderstanding

One widespread misconception is that African masking traditions were universally performed by men. Liberia and neighbouring Sierra Leone provide an important counter-example.

Within Sande traditions, women perform important masked roles. Researchers have long noted that this is one of the best-known female masking traditions in Africa. Helmet masks associated with Sande represent ideals of maturity, dignity and respected womanhood rather than sinister or frightening spirits. Their carefully carved hairstyles, polished surfaces and composed expressions communicate social values recognised within the community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sfomuseum.org/fil/exhibitions/liberian-helmet-masks-sande-and-poro-societies-collections-phoebe-hearst-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sfomuseum.org">[sfomuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sfomuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sfomuseum.org.</span></span></span>

This does not mean every Liberian mask is female or that all communities follow identical customs. Instead, it demonstrates how misleading it is to treat all West African masks as interchangeable "tribal" objects. Gender roles, ceremonial responsibilities and artistic conventions vary across communities and institutions.

## What foreign museum labels often get wrong

Many Liberian masks entered European and North American collections during the colonial period or through twentieth-century art markets. Earlier catalogues frequently described them using broad terms such as "fetish", "witch doctor", "secret society object" or "primitive ritual", language that reflected colonial assumptions more than local understandings.

Contemporary museums increasingly provide richer interpretation by explaining:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* which community or region a mask comes from;
* whether it belonged to Sande, Poro or another masquerade tradition;
* how costume and performance completed the object;
* that the carving represents social authority as much as spiritual belief;
* that many traditions remain culturally significant today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sfomuseum.org/fil/exhibitions/liberian-helmet-masks-sande-and-poro-societies-collections-phoebe-hearst-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sfomuseum.org">[sfomuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sfomuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sfomuseum.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

Even so, museum collections still face practical limitations. The fibre costumes, music, choreography and spoken traditions that originally accompanied many masks are often absent, making it difficult to recreate their full cultural meaning.

## Living heritage rather than museum curiosities

Liberia's tourism and heritage authorities increasingly present masks as part of the country's living cultural identity instead of treating them as mysterious relics. Public presentations of the country's "Sixteen Tribal Masks", for example, explain that modern displays may include symbolic or educational representations inspired by authentic traditions rather than literal ceremonial objects. They also stress that masking has long been connected with education, community cohesion and cultural continuity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lnta.gov.lr/16-tribal-masks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lnta.gov.lr">[Liberia National Tourism Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lnta.gov.lr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liberia National Tourism Authority16 Tribal Masks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liberia National Tourism Authority...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because ceremonial objects and performances are not automatically tourist attractions. Some traditions remain private or restricted, while others have adapted to festivals, cultural exhibitions and heritage programmes. Responsible interpretation therefore requires recognising that museum displays represent only one aspect of traditions that continue to evolve.

## Why this matters for understanding Liberia

Viewing Liberian masks solely through the lens of witchcraft misses what makes them culturally distinctive. They are artistic works, certainly, but they are also instruments of education, symbols of recognised authority and parts of carefully structured performances that help define community life.

For visitors exploring Liberia's unusual heritage, the real curiosity is not supernatural mystery but the way a carved object becomes meaningful only through performance, shared knowledge and social recognition. Understanding that relationship replaces stereotypes with a richer picture of one of Liberia's most important living cultural traditions.

      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
