## Where Georgia’s unusual collections are found

Georgia has no well-documented national museum devoted specifically to cryptids, UFOs, ceremonial magic or witchcraft. The strongest public collections instead fall into four overlapping groups:

* **Scientific curiosities**, particularly the early-human fossils from [Dmanisi]({{ 'dmanisi/' | relative_url }}) and the entomological and industrial holdings of the [State Silk Museum]({{ 'silk-museum/' | relative_url }}).

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Political relics**, centred on Stalin, Soviet occupation and revolutionary underground activity.
* **Dynastic and devotional objects**, including death masks, reliquaries, icons and objects connected to saints or biblical tradition.
* **Immersive folk environments**, where historic work, clothing and domestic life are reconstructed through buildings, mannequins, tools and hand-carved scenes.

## Dmanisi and the deep past

The most extraordinary scientifically credible material in Georgia comes from Dmanisi, south-west of Tbilisi. Beneath the ruins of a medieval town, archaeologists found exceptionally early remains of human ancestors, fossil animals and stone tools dating to roughly 1.8 million years ago. International geological heritage records describe Dmanisi as providing the earliest known evidence of early humans outside Africa, with a substantial collection of hominin remains, thousands of animal fossils and thousands of stone artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5225/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDmanisi Hominid Archaeological SiteSeveral hominid individuals (4 skulls, 3 of them with maxillas, 4 mandible...</span></span></span>

For an ordinary visitor, the uncanny quality comes from scale rather than spectacle. The skulls are recognisably human yet sufficiently different to raise difficult questions about what counts as a species and how early populations moved across continents. Five relatively complete skulls found at the site show considerable variation between individuals, contributing to continuing scientific discussion about early human classification. The finds are not archaeological “anomalies” in the Fortean sense: they are carefully excavated, dated and studied evidence. Their strangeness lies in how dramatically they expand Georgia’s place in the human story.

The Dmanisi Museum-Reserve combines several historical layers: a Palaeolithic site, later Bronze Age material and the remains of a medieval settlement. Some original Dmanisi fossils are preserved by the Georgian National Museum in Tbilisi, so travellers should not assume that every celebrated skull is displayed at the excavation site itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://georgiantravelguide.com/en/dmanisi-museum-reserve" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: georgiantravelguide.com">[Georgian Travel Guide]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">georgiantravelguide.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Georgian Travel GuideDmanisi Museum-ReserveThe museum-reserve includes: a medieval town, archaeological layers of the Late Bronze Age and...</span></span></span>

## The State Silk Museum’s cabinet of cocoons

Tbilisi’s State Silk Museum is one of Georgia’s clearest examples of a genuine scientific cabinet of curiosity. Created in connection with a nineteenth-century Caucasian sericulture station, it treats silk not simply as luxury fabric but as an entire biological and industrial system involving moths, larvae, mulberry trees, diseases, dyes, machinery and human labour.

Its most distinctive holding is a collection of up to 5,000 silkworm cocoons of different species and varieties, gathered from the Caucasus, Europe and Asia. The museum also preserves butterflies, containers for silkworm eggs, dye samples, textiles, lace, production tools and educational models. Rows of cocoons differing in shape, size and colour can resemble an archive of tiny natural sculptures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museu.ms/museum/details/16726" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museu.ms">[Museu.MS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museu.ms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">special containers for silkworms and eggs (approximately...</span></span></span>

The museum is unusual because its old-fashioned scientific atmosphere is authentic rather than artificially themed. Its building, specimens and laboratory heritage grew from a working research institution. This places it somewhere between a natural-history museum, an industrial museum and a surviving nineteenth-century research collection.

After four years of restoration, the museum officially reopened on 9 October 2024. Its own published visitor information lists regular opening days from Tuesday to Saturday, although hours should still be checked before travel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://aracneproject.eu/state-silk-museum-reopens-celebrating-135-years-of-georgian-sericulture-heritage/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aracneproject.eu">[aracneproject.eu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aracneproject.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">state silk museum reopens celebrating 135 years of georgian sericulture heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">state silk museum reopens celebrating 135 years of georgian sericulture heritage</span></span></span>

## Stalin’s objects and the battle over memory

### The museum in Gori

The Joseph Stalin Museum in Gori is probably Georgia’s best-known dark-history attraction. Its complex includes the small house associated with Stalin’s childhood, a monumental museum building, personal possessions and gifts, a death mask and an armoured railway carriage used during his leadership of the Soviet Union. The result has often been described less as a critical history museum than as a preserved Soviet-era shrine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Joseph Stalin Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Joseph Stalin Museum</span></span></span>

The strangest object may be the railway carriage: a heavily armoured mobile interior linked to journeys including the Tehran and Yalta conferences. Yet the museum’s deeper peculiarity is curatorial. Portraits, presents and heroic imagery give material form to the cult of personality, while the violence of forced collectivisation, mass repression, deportation and the labour-camp system has historically occupied far less space.

Recent reporting and museum commentary continue to criticise the imbalance. Accounts from 2024 described the main galleries as overwhelmingly celebratory, with information about the Great Terror confined to a comparatively small area. Earlier plans to transform or substantially reinterpret the institution were not fully realised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/stalin-museum-celebrates-the-local-boy-made-good-2ffgg0h3r" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times Stalin museum celebrates the local boy made good</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Times Stalin museum celebrates the local boy made good</span></span></span>

Visitors should therefore approach Gori on two levels. It is a collection of authentic and attributed objects connected with Stalin, but it is also an artefact of Soviet museum-making. What is omitted is as important as what appears in the cases. The museum is valuable precisely because it exposes unresolved arguments within Georgia about whether Stalin should be remembered principally as a powerful Georgian, a Soviet victor or the architect of mass state violence. That dispute remains part of the country’s contemporary political culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/article/2024/may/19/stalin-statues-georgia-kremlin-street-protests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

### The underground printing house

Tbilisi’s underground printing-house museum offers a smaller, rougher and more atmospheric encounter with revolutionary history. The original clandestine operation produced illegal anti-tsarist publications in the early twentieth century. Access to the concealed press involved hidden rooms, passages and a well used as part of the entry system, allowing material to be produced away from police scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/stalins-underground-printing-house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Stalin&#x27;s Underground Printing House Museum in Tbilisi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Stalin&#x27;s Underground Printing House Museum in Tbilisi</span></span></span>

The site is often associated prominently with the young Stalin, although the operation involved a wider network of Bolsheviks and other revolutionaries. Its preserved machinery, subterranean chamber and revolutionary memorabilia make it feel closer to a bunker or conspiratorial hideout than a conventional biographical museum.

Interpretation has sometimes depended heavily on individual custodians and inherited Soviet narratives. For that reason, it is better treated as a historic site with a politically layered presentation than as a neutral account of revolutionary activity. Visitor reports and opening arrangements have varied, so access should be confirmed locally rather than assumed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.co.uk/Attraction_Review-g294195-d4961521-Reviews-J_Stalin_s_Underground_Printing_House_Museum-Tbilisi.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.co.uk">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tripadvisor.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Soviet occupation from the victims’ side

The Soviet Occupation Exhibition Hall in Tbilisi provides the essential counterweight to Gori. Opened in 2006 as part of the Georgian National Museum, it covers the Bolshevik invasion of 1921, anti-Soviet resistance, political repression and the restoration of Georgian independence in 1991.

Its collection includes more than 3,000 objects and documents, film material, interrogation records, execution or exile orders and personal files connected with people targeted by the regime. One of its most disturbing exhibits is a railway wagon associated with the killing of participants in the 1924 uprising.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.ge/index.php?m=350&amp;museum=53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ge">[Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.ge.</span></span></span>

This is not an oddity museum in a playful sense. It belongs in Georgia’s strange-collections landscape because it preserves the physical machinery of fear: bureaucratic documents, prison material, surveillance records and transport infrastructure turned into evidence of state violence. Seen alongside the Stalin Museum, it also demonstrates how two institutions can use objects from the same broad period to tell radically different moral stories.

## Napoleon’s death mask in Zugdidi

The Dadiani Palaces Museum in Zugdidi combines aristocratic interiors, dynastic history, weapons, art and family possessions. Its most famous curiosity is a bronze death mask of Napoleon Bonaparte. The object reached Georgia through the Dadiani family’s connection with the Murat branch of Napoleon’s extended family after Salome Dadiani married Achille Murat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hikasustravel.com/en/georgia/samegrelo/dadiani-palace-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hikasustravel.com">[Hikasus Travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hikasustravel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hikasustravel.com.</span></span></span>

Death masks were made by taking an impression from a person’s face shortly after death and then producing casts from that mould. They occupied an ambiguous position between documentary portraiture, memorial object and relic. The Zugdidi example is therefore more than a celebrity souvenir: it represents a nineteenth-century culture in which physical likeness was believed to preserve something unusually direct about the deceased.

Claims about exactly which casts are “original” can become confused. Napoleon’s first facial mould was made after his death on Saint Helena in 1821, but multiple casts and later versions circulated. Visitors should understand the Zugdidi mask as a significant Napoleonic object with a strong family provenance, while treating promotional claims about rarity or precise edition numbers cautiously unless supported by specialist cataloguing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.liverpoolmuseums.org.uk/artifact/death-mask-of-napoleon-i-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: liverpoolmuseums.org.uk">[Liverpool Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">liverpoolmuseums.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on liverpoolmuseums.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The palace collection matters within Georgia because it reveals how far dynastic collecting networks extended. A seemingly improbable French imperial relic in western Georgia becomes understandable once marriage, exile, aristocratic mobility and inherited collections are taken into account.

## Folk life rebuilt by hand

Near Batumi, the private Borjgalo Ethnographic Museum presents Adjarian rural life through handmade figures, carved objects, architectural models, tools and recreated work scenes. Its founder, woodcarver Kemal Turmanidze, spent decades constructing an immersive environment populated by costumed figures engaged in weaving, farming, beekeeping, wine production and other traditional activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://georgia.to/en/places-to-go/adjara/batumi/borjgalo-ethnographical-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: georgia.to">[Georgia Travel &amp; Tours]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">georgia.to</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Travel &amp; Tours Borjgalo Ethnographical Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Travel &amp; Tours Borjgalo Ethnographical Museum</span></span></span>

The museum can feel uncanny because the human figures are frozen in action inside densely arranged domestic and workshop settings. Unlike a state ethnographic museum, it reflects the vision and craftsmanship of one collector-maker. This gives it warmth and personality, but also means the display is an artistic reconstruction rather than an untouched village preserved in place.

Its value lies in the meeting of folklore, memory and outsider-scale making. Hundreds of carvings and carefully staged scenes transform ordinary labour into a personal miniature world. It is tourist-facing and accessible, yet more idiosyncratic than a standard regional history museum.

Tbilisi’s much larger Open Air Museum of Ethnography offers a more institutional comparison. Founded in 1966 as the first open-air ethnographic museum in the Caucasus, it gathers historic buildings and material culture from different regions of Georgia. Borjgalo is the personal cabinet; the Tbilisi museum is the national landscape reconstructed at architectural scale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.ge/index.php?exhibtion_id=1148&amp;m=326" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ge">[Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.ge.</span></span></span>

## Sacred objects are not simply exhibits

Georgia’s medieval churches, treasuries and art collections contain reliquaries, icons, crosses and objects connected by tradition to saints or biblical events. These should not be approached as though they were paranormal props. In an active religious setting, their function is devotional: believers may regard them as channels of sacred presence, while historians study their materials, inscriptions, style and documented ownership.

The most prominent example is Svetitskhoveli Cathedral in Mtskheta, where Georgian Orthodox tradition holds that the robe of Christ is buried beneath the cathedral. The object is not presented as an ordinary museum exhibit that visitors can inspect in a glass case. The site is an active cathedral and pilgrimage destination, and the tradition is part of its sacred identity rather than an archaeologically verified display claim. Georgian tourism material describes the robe as the country’s most venerated Christian relic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.gnta.ge/storage/files/doc/mart-lmadidebeli-momloc-velebi-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.gnta.ge">[GNTA API]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.gnta.ge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GNTA APIorthodox pilgrimage to - georgiaMany sacred relics and works of. ## Archaeology that resembles myth

Georgia’s archaeological museums contain objects that can look magical even when their context is well understood. The Vani Archaeological Museum, for example, preserves material from an important ancient centre in western Georgia, including rich burials, jewellery, ritual objects and evidence of contact between local communities and the wider Greek and Persian worlds.

Such finds are frequently drawn into romantic accounts of ancient Colchis and the legend of the Golden Fleece. The connection is culturally powerful but should not be treated as proof that a particular excavated treasure belonged to a named mythological figure. Archaeology can demonstrate wealthy communities, metalworking skill, trade and ritual practice; it cannot verify the literal events of an epic tale.

This tension is characteristic of Georgia. Ancient gold, unusual burials and ritual imagery make myth feel tangible, while museum interpretation must separate literary tradition from excavated evidence. The Georgian National Museum’s archaeological holdings include major gold and silver objects from ancient Colchis as well as material from Dmanisi, Vani and other sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.ge/index.php?lng=eng&amp;m=305" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ge">[Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">COLLECTIONS საქართველოს ეროვნული მუზეუმი... ## What visitors should expect

Georgia’s unusual collections range from internationally significant state museums to small private displays. Presentation quality, English-language interpretation and opening reliability can therefore differ sharply.

**Most academically grounded:** Dmanisi and the archaeological, natural-history and religious-art collections of the Georgian National Museum. These are the strongest choices for documented material, although individual galleries may change during renovation or exhibition rotation.

**Most politically contested:** the Stalin Museum in Gori. Its physical collection is substantial, but its interpretation should be compared with the Soviet Occupation Exhibition Hall and independent historical research.

**Most like a classic curiosity cabinet:** the State Silk Museum, where biology, design, industrial history and rows of specimens meet in a purpose-built historic institution.

**Most personal and eccentric:** Borjgalo, whose handmade figures and reconstructed village scenes reflect one artist-collector’s vision.

**Most surprising single object:** Napoleon’s death mask at the Dadiani Palaces Museum, provided its precise casting history is not exaggerated.

**Most dependent on belief:** sacred traditions at Mtskheta and other active religious sites. These are public places of worship rather than secular museums, and respectful behaviour takes priority over curiosity tourism.

Current hours should always be checked with the institution itself. Georgia’s museum network has undergone closures, restorations and administrative changes, and smaller sites may operate with limited staff or require guided access.

## Why Georgia’s curiosity culture matters

Georgia’s unusual collections reveal a country positioned between empires, trade routes, religious traditions and competing historical narratives. The objects are strange not because they have been assembled merely to shock, but because their journeys are unexpected: a French emperor’s face preserved in a western Georgian palace, human ancestors beneath a medieval town, revolutionary pamphlets hidden below Tbilisi, cocoons catalogued like jewels and a dictator’s childhood home enclosed inside monumental architecture.

They also show that museums do not simply store facts. Gori demonstrates how display can preserve propaganda; the Soviet Occupation exhibition shows how archives can restore suppressed victims; Dmanisi shows science revising humanity’s chronology; and Mtskheta shows how an object or place can remain meaningful without becoming a conventional exhibit.

For travellers interested in eerie history, folklore and curiosities, Georgia rewards a slower form of looking. The country’s most compelling oddities sit precisely where evidence and legend, reverence and scepticism, private memory and public history begin to overlap.
