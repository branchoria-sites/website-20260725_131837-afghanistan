## Introduction

The most remarkable gallery in [Ethiopia]({{ 'what-makes-ethiopias-collections-so/' | relative_url }}) is not filled with mysterious relics or legendary monsters. Instead, it displays fragmentary fossil bones that have transformed how humanity understands its own origins. At the centre is Lucy, the 3.2-million-year-old partial skeleton of *Australopithecus afarensis*, discovered in Ethiopia's Afar Region in 1974. Although visitors to the National Museum in Addis Ababa usually see a meticulously made replica rather than the original fossil, the experience remains one of the world's most unusual museum encounters: a meeting with an ancestor whose remains helped redefine the story of human evolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/al" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Ethiopia_3d91f7_lucy_human_origins_ba1073-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Human Origins illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Ethiopia_3d91f7_lucy_human_origins_ba1073-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Ethiopia_3d91f7_lucy_human_origins_ba1073-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Within Ethiopia's landscape of unusual collections, Lucy occupies a distinctive place. She is neither an archaeological treasure displayed for artistic beauty nor a religious relic preserved for devotion. Instead, her fossil fragments are scientific evidence presented almost like a secular relic, inviting visitors to contemplate millions of years of human history through a surprisingly small collection of bones.

## What visitors actually see in the fossil galleries

The National Museum's human origins galleries focus on some of the most significant hominin discoveries ever made. The centrepiece is Lucy (catalogue number AL 288-1), but the display also introduces visitors to other Ethiopian fossil discoveries that together illustrate an exceptionally deep record of early human evolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/al" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span></span></span>

One detail often surprises first-time visitors: the skeleton on public display is normally a replica. The genuine fossil consists of hundreds of fragile pieces preserved under carefully controlled conditions and kept in secure storage to minimise the risks posed by light, vibration, humidity and accidental damage. The cast allows the museum to present Lucy's anatomy while protecting an irreplaceable scientific specimen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8ba1e84b3ff1cc774264fa441f3057ff" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News Ethiopian fossil Lucy leaves for her first exhibition in Europe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discovered in 1974 in Ethiopia, Lucy belongs to the species Australopithecus afarensis and is more than 3 million years old. Her skeleton...</span></span></span>

The gallery combines reconstructed skeletons, fossil casts, explanatory panels and geological context rather than presenting Lucy as an isolated curiosity. This approach helps visitors understand that no single fossil explains human evolution. Instead, Lucy forms part of a growing body of evidence recovered from Ethiopia's Rift Valley over decades of palaeoanthropological research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/australopithecus-afarensis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

Unlike many archaeological museums that celebrate kings, monuments or civilisations, these galleries present individuals who lived millions of years before recorded history. That perspective gives the museum an unusual atmosphere: visitors are not looking into the history of a nation but into the much older history of humanity itself.

## What Lucy revealed about early upright walking

Lucy became famous not because she was the oldest human ancestor ever discovered, but because her skeleton was unusually complete. Roughly 40 per cent of her skeleton survived, providing researchers with enough anatomical evidence to examine how an early hominin moved through its environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/al" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span></span></span>

Her bones revealed an intriguing combination of features.

* Her pelvis and angled thigh bones clearly supported efficient upright walking.
* Her feet were adapted to bearing body weight while walking on two legs.
* At the same time, her long arms, curved fingers and powerful upper body suggest she still climbed trees regularly.
* Rather than representing a modern human body plan, Lucy demonstrated an evolutionary transition that combined terrestrial walking with substantial climbing ability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/al" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span></span></span>

This combination challenged earlier assumptions that upright walking evolved only after humans had developed much larger brains. Lucy showed instead that bipedal locomotion emerged considerably earlier, fundamentally changing scientific thinking about the sequence of human evolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/al" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Origins AL 288-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span></span></span>

Even details that appear insignificant—such as the angle of a knee joint or the shape of the pelvis—became critical pieces of evidence. In the fossil gallery, these ordinary-looking fragments demonstrate how anatomical interpretation depends on careful comparison rather than dramatic discoveries.

## Beyond Lucy: Ethiopia's wider fossil record

Lucy's fame can overshadow the broader significance of Ethiopia's fossil record. The museum's displays increasingly place her alongside other discoveries that deepen and sometimes complicate the story she first told.

One of the most important is Selam (DIK-1-1), an exceptionally preserved child of the same species discovered in Dikika in 2000. Although popularly nicknamed "Lucy's baby", Selam was not Lucy's offspring and actually lived around 100,000 years earlier. The fossil preserves much of the skull, torso and limbs, making it one of the most complete juvenile early hominin skeletons ever found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/dik" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Origins DIK-1-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Origins DIK-1-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span></span></span>

Selam allowed researchers to investigate childhood growth in *Australopithecus afarensis*. Her teeth suggest a growth pattern resembling that of young chimpanzees, while aspects of her developing brain hint at evolutionary changes moving towards later human development. Her skeleton also reinforces the picture of a species capable of both habitual upright walking and effective tree climbing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/dik" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Origins DIK-1-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Origins DIK-1-1 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program</span></span></span>

Seen together, Lucy and Selam transform isolated fossil finds into a population rather than a single famous individual. Visitors begin to appreciate that Ethiopia preserves not merely one iconic skeleton but an extensive archive documenting several stages of early human evolution.

## Why originals, replicas and travelling fossils matter


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The distinction between an original fossil and a museum replica is especially important in palaeoanthropology because the evidence itself is irreplaceable. Every crack, mineral deposit and microscopic surface feature may contain information valuable for future research. Conservation therefore often takes priority over public display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8ba1e84b3ff1cc774264fa441f3057ff" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News Ethiopian fossil Lucy leaves for her first exhibition in Europe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discovered in 1974 in Ethiopia, Lucy belongs to the species Australopithecus afarensis and is more than 3 million years old. Her skeleton...</span></span></span>

</div>

That makes the occasional international loan highly significant. In 2025, the original Lucy and Selam fossils travelled to Prague for a temporary exhibition—the first time Lucy had been displayed in Europe and only the second occasion on which she had left Ethiopia. The exhibition highlighted both Ethiopia's willingness to share its scientific heritage and the continuing debate over whether such fragile specimens should travel at all. Supporters argued that the fossils belong to humanity's shared story, while critics worried about transport risks and questioned whether such national treasures should leave the country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8ba1e84b3ff1cc774264fa441f3057ff" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News Ethiopian fossil Lucy leaves for her first exhibition in Europe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discovered in 1974 in Ethiopia, Lucy belongs to the species Australopithecus afarensis and is more than 3 million years old. For most visitors to Addis Ababa, however, the replica remains the more meaningful encounter. The scientific importance of Lucy does not depend on touching the original bones. The reconstruction faithfully communicates the anatomical relationships that transformed understanding of early human evolution while allowing the authentic fossil to survive for future generations of researchers.

## Why Lucy remains Ethiopia's strangest museum experience

Among Ethiopia's remarkable collections, Lucy stands apart because she overturns expectations of what a museum curiosity can be. There is no mystery about her authenticity, no folklore surrounding supernatural powers and no elaborate treasure narrative. Her fascination comes from evidence.

A relatively small collection of fossil fragments enables visitors to visualise a creature that walked through East Africa more than three million years ago and whose anatomy still informs debates about how humans evolved. Few museum objects compress such immense spans of time into a single display.

That combination of scientific certainty, extraordinary age and public accessibility makes the National Museum's fossil gallery unlike almost any other attraction in Ethiopia. Rather than presenting something strange because it is inexplicable, it presents something astonishing because painstaking scientific work has made the seemingly impossible understandable.

      if (!normalized) continue;
      if (localeMarketMap[normalized]) {
        exactMarket = localeMarketMap[normalized];
        break;
      }
      var languageOnly = normalized.split('-')[0];
      if (!languageMarket && localeMarketMap[languageOnly]) languageMarket = localeMarketMap[languageOnly];
    }
    return { exact: exactMarket, language: languageMarket };
  }
  function inferFromLocale() {
    var matches = localeMatches();
    return matches.exact || matches.language || '';
  }
  function inferFromTimezone() {
    var tz = '';
    try { tz = String(Intl.DateTimeFormat().resolvedOptions().timeZone || ''); } catch (err) {}
    if (!tz) return '';
    for (var i = 0; i< timezoneRules.length; i += 1) {
      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {
        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
    }
    return '';
  }
  function inferMarket(available, options) {
    available = Array.isArray(available) ? available : [];
    options = options || {};
    var storageKey = options.storageKey || 'phoenix-affiliate-market';
    try {
      var saved = window.localStorage && localStorage.getItem(storageKey);
      if (saved && available.indexOf(saved) >= 0) return saved;
    } catch (err) {}
    var timezoneMarket = inferFromTimezone();
    if (timezoneMarket && available.indexOf(timezoneMarket) >= 0) return timezoneMarket;
    var localeMarkets = localeMatches();
    if (localeMarkets.exact && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.exact) >= 0) return localeMarkets.exact;
    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
    if (options.defaultMarket && available.indexOf(options.defaultMarket) >= 0) return options.defaultMarket;
    return available[0] || '';
  }
  window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation = {
    normalize: normalize,
    inferMarket: inferMarket,
    inferFromLocale: inferFromLocale,
    inferFromTimezone: inferFromTimezone
  };
})();
