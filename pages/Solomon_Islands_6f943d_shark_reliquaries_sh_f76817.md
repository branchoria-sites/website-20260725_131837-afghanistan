## Introduction

Among the most unusual sacred objects associated with the [Solomon Islands]({{ 'solomon-islands/' | relative_url }}) are carved shark reliquaries and the shrine houses that once protected them. These were not curiosities created for display but deeply significant ritual structures linked to ancestor veneration, chiefly authority and beliefs about the spiritual world. Although only a small number survive, they represent one of the most distinctive forms of religious art from the islands. Today, examples are preserved in museums in the Solomon Islands and overseas, while many original shrine houses have disappeared through colonial disruption, Christian conversion and the passage of time. Understanding these objects means looking beyond their striking appearance to the religious traditions that gave them meaning.

## What is a shark reliquary?

A shark reliquary is a carved wooden container associated with the remains of an important deceased person, usually a chief or respected ancestor. Rather than being a simple coffin, it functioned as a sacred vessel that linked the dead with powerful sea spirits and ancestral authority.

Some of the finest surviving examples are long, streamlined carvings combining features of sharks and bonito tuna. They include carefully carved fins, open jaws that originally held shell-inlaid teeth and hollow chambers designed to contain a human skull or other ancestral remains. According to museum research, these carvings were closely connected with beliefs that high-ranking individuals could ultimately become associated with sharks after completing important stages of the funerary cycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/762069" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Shark Reliquary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtShark Reliquary - Solomon Islands - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

The shark was therefore far more than decorative. Across parts of the Solomon Islands it represented strength, protection and the continuing presence of spiritually powerful ancestors.

## Why sharks held sacred importance

The significance of shark imagery varied between different island groups, but sharks frequently occupied an important place in traditional religious thought.

Historical accounts describe sharks as guardians, spirit beings or creatures linked to particular ancestral powers rather than simply dangerous animals. In some communities, ritual specialists were known for "calling" sharks to ceremonial feeding places, where the animals were treated as spiritually significant protectors instead of prey. These practices survived into the twentieth century in places such as Langalanga Lagoon on Malaita, where colonial officials and later anthropologists recorded ritual shark feeding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.solomonencyclopaedia.net/biogs/E000275b.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: solomonencyclopaedia.net">[solomonencyclopaedia.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">solomonencyclopaedia.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shark Calling</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Concept - Solomon Islands Encyclopaedia, 1893-1978...</span></span></span>

This broader religious landscape helps explain why a chief's remains might be protected within a shark-shaped reliquary. The carving symbolised an ongoing relationship between the ancestor and a respected supernatural guardian.

## The role of sacred shrine houses

The reliquaries were normally housed within sacred buildings rather than ordinary homes.

These structures, sometimes referred to by researchers as shrine houses or tambu houses, acted as repositories for the most spiritually important possessions of a lineage. They could contain:

* [ancestral skulls]({{ 'ancestral-skulls/' | relative_url }});

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* carved reliquaries;
* shell valuables;
* ritual carvings;
* ceremonial ornaments; and
* other objects associated with clan history and religious ceremonies.

</div>

Shrine houses were places where descendants maintained relationships with deceased ancestors through offerings, prayers and ritual observances. Access was often restricted according to status, gender or ritual knowledge, meaning they were never public spaces in the modern museum sense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.solomonencyclopaedia.net/biogs/E000247b.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: solomonencyclopaedia.net">[solomonencyclopaedia.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">solomonencyclopaedia.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Place - Solomon Islands Encyclopaedia, 1893-1978...</span></span></span>

Rather than displaying collections for curiosity, these buildings preserved sacred power and reinforced the authority of particular descent groups.

### More than storehouses

European collectors sometimes described these buildings simply as "skull houses", but that label understates their wider function.

They served simultaneously as religious centres, repositories of genealogy, memorials to important leaders and places where ritual specialists sought guidance from ancestral spirits. The objects inside were meaningful because of their relationships to living communities rather than because they were old or artistically impressive.

## What survives today?

Very few shrine houses remain intact.

From the late nineteenth century onwards, missionary activity, colonial administration and changing religious beliefs led to the abandonment or destruction of many traditional sacred structures. At the same time, collectors acquired large numbers of carvings and ritual objects for European museums.

As a result, many of the best-preserved shark reliquaries are now held in international collections. The Metropolitan Museum of Art possesses an exceptionally rare carved shark reliquary with a central cavity intended for the skull of a deceased chief. The museum notes that such reliquaries were traditionally suspended high within canoe houses or communal men's buildings where important discussions took place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/762069" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Shark Reliquary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtShark Reliquary - Solomon Islands - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

The British Museum also preserves remarkable fish-shaped funerary carvings, including a nineteenth-century wooden fish coffin containing a human skull from Owaraha (Santa Ana). Contemporary documentation suggested that much larger shark-shaped examples once hung inside tambu houses, where they enclosed the remains of important chiefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumcoffin; human skeletal remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumcoffin; human skeletal remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## How museums interpret them today

Modern museums increasingly present shark reliquaries within their original cultural and religious context rather than as exotic curiosities.

Interpretation now emphasises several important points:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the carvings were sacred religious objects rather than works created primarily as art;
* they formed part of wider systems of ancestor veneration;
* beliefs differed considerably between island groups;
* many objects entered museum collections during periods of colonial collecting; and
* descendant communities continue to regard aspects of these traditions with respect.

</div>

This approach reflects wider changes in museum practice that seek to explain both the craftsmanship of the carvings and the beliefs that made them spiritually significant.

## Separating evidence from legend

The appearance of shark reliquaries has sometimes encouraged exaggerated stories about mysterious cults or supernatural shark worship.

The historical evidence paints a more nuanced picture. Ethnographic records consistently show that sharks were one element within diverse local religious systems centred on ancestors, clan identity and relationships between the living and the dead. There was no single island-wide religion devoted exclusively to sharks, nor were all shark carvings funerary reliquaries. Some represented sea spirits, protective beings or fishing powers, while others decorated canoe houses or ceremonial architecture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Likewise, surviving reliquaries are extremely rare. They should not be seen as typical household objects but as exceptional ritual possessions associated with high-status individuals and important ceremonial traditions.

## Why shark reliquaries matter in the Solomon Islands' curiosity heritage

For anyone exploring the Solomon Islands' unusual collections, shark reliquaries stand out because they combine remarkable craftsmanship with complex religious meaning. They occupy the boundary between sculpture, funerary practice and sacred architecture, making them unlike almost any museum objects elsewhere in the Pacific.

They also illustrate an important feature of the Solomon Islands' curiosity landscape: many of the country's most extraordinary "collections" originally existed inside sacred shrine houses rather than purpose-built museums. Today's surviving carvings, whether displayed in Honiara or overseas institutions, are fragments of much larger ritual worlds in which architecture, ancestral remains and carved guardians formed a single integrated sacred space.
