## Introduction

The Kunstkamera in Saint Petersburg is often described as [Russia]({{ 'why-are-russias-strangest-collections-so/' | relative_url }})'s strangest museum, but its most unsettling feature is not simply its collection of preserved human bodies and congenital anomalies. The deeper question is whether these remains should be displayed at all, and if so, under what ethical conditions. Founded by Peter the Great in the early eighteenth century as Russia's first public museum, the Kunstkamera was intended to replace superstition with scientific observation. Today, however, the same specimens that once symbolised Enlightenment thinking also prompt difficult debates about consent, dignity, colonial collecting and the responsibilities of modern museums. Understanding the Kunstkamera therefore means looking beyond its famous anatomical displays to the changing ethics of collecting and exhibiting human remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhm.ac.uk/about-us/governance/human-remains-in-the-collection.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhm.ac.uk">[Natural History Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhm.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum Human remains in the collection &#124; Natural History Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum Human remains in the collection &#124; Natural History Museum</span></span></span>

## What Makes the Kunstkamera So Unsettling?

Unlike many curiosity museums that rely on theatrical presentation, the Kunstkamera remains an active academic institution. Officially known as the Peter the Great Museum of Anthropology and Ethnography, it belongs to the Russian Academy of Sciences and combines extensive ethnographic collections with anatomical preparations that have made it internationally famous.

Many visitors arrive expecting a museum of medical oddities. Instead, they encounter a much broader institution whose reputation has become closely tied to a relatively small group of preserved human and animal specimens. These include foetuses and infants with congenital conditions, anatomical preparations acquired from Dutch collections and specimens assembled during the reign of Peter the Great. Their preservation reflects early modern scientific practice rather than modern medical collecting. The contrast between their original scientific purpose and modern expectations about privacy and bodily dignity explains why these displays continue to provoke strong reactions.

## Peter the Great's Cabinet of Curiosities

Peter the Great admired scientific collections he encountered during his travels in western Europe, particularly in the Dutch Republic. Rather than treating unusual births as supernatural signs, he wanted them studied as natural phenomena. He acquired important anatomical collections from the Dutch anatomist Frederik Ruysch, whose preservation techniques were considered remarkable, alongside natural history collections assembled by Albertus Seba. These purchases became part of the foundation of the Kunstkamera. Their purpose was educational: to encourage observation instead of fear or religious superstition.

Peter also introduced policies encouraging unusual human and animal births to be reported rather than hidden. Contemporary accounts describe rewards for specimens considered scientifically valuable. Although these policies reflected Enlightenment ideals about empirical investigation, they also reveal an era in which individual consent played little role in museum collecting. Bodies became evidence for science before they were considered people with posthumous rights in the modern sense.

The museum therefore preserves two histories simultaneously. It documents the birth of public scientific education in Russia while also preserving collecting practices that many museums would now regard as ethically problematic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhm.ac.uk/about-us/governance/human-remains-in-the-collection.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhm.ac.uk">[Natural History Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhm.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Natural History Museum Human remains in the collection &#124; Natural History Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Natural History Museum Human remains in the collection &#124; Natural History Museum</span></span></span>

## Anatomical Specimens and Enlightenment Science

The anatomical displays were never intended simply to shock visitors. Their original educational purpose was to demonstrate that congenital differences resulted from natural developmental processes rather than demonic influence, witchcraft or divine punishment.

That distinction mattered in early eighteenth-century Russia. Public exhibition of preserved specimens formed part of a broader Enlightenment programme encouraging observation, classification and anatomical study. In this respect, the Kunstkamera resembles other early European cabinets of curiosity that gradually evolved into scientific museums.

Modern audiences, however, rarely experience these displays through the same intellectual framework. Visitors often see preserved infants and anatomical preparations first as human remains and only second as scientific specimens. This reversal changes the ethical balance. What once represented progress in scientific thinking can now appear emotionally disturbing, particularly because the individuals displayed never had the opportunity to consent to public exhibition.

The tension illustrates how museum objects do not possess fixed meanings. Their interpretation changes alongside wider cultural values concerning disability, medical history and the treatment of the dead.

## Empire, Provenance and Modern Museum Ethics

The ethical debate extends beyond anatomy. The Kunstkamera's ethnographic collections were assembled during centuries of Russian exploration, imperial expansion and scientific expeditions across Siberia, Central Asia, the Arctic and other regions. Like many large museums founded during imperial expansion, it contains material collected under political relationships that were often unequal.

This does not mean every object was improperly acquired. Provenance varies enormously between collections, acquisitions and historical periods. Nevertheless, modern museums increasingly examine how objects and human remains entered their collections, whether communities were consulted, and whether continued display remains appropriate.

Human remains raise especially sensitive questions because they cannot easily be treated as ordinary museum artefacts. Contemporary museum ethics increasingly distinguish between preserving remains for legitimate research and displaying them publicly for educational purposes. The two activities require separate ethical justification.

International museum guidance has steadily moved towards greater emphasis on provenance, consultation and respect. Current ethical standards recommend collaboration with descendant communities whenever possible, careful consideration of cultural or spiritual concerns, transparent documentation of acquisition histories and, where appropriate, discussion of restricted access or repatriation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icom.museum/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Code-of-Ethics_2026_EN-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icom.museum">[International Council of Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icom.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Code of Ethics 2026 EN 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Council of MuseumsICOM STANDING COMMITTEE ON ETHICSMay 7, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why Consent Is So Difficult in Historic Collections

One reason the Kunstkamera remains controversial is that applying modern ethical principles to eighteenth-century collections is inherently difficult.

Many individuals represented in historical anatomical collections were never identified by name. Records may be incomplete or lost altogether. Descendant communities are often impossible to identify, particularly in collections assembled centuries ago. Legal ownership may be clear while moral authority remains uncertain.

## How the Kunstkamera Fits International Debates

The questions raised by the Kunstkamera are no longer unique to Russia. Museums throughout Europe, North America and elsewhere are reassessing collections assembled during the age of empire, scientific expansion and colonial anthropology.

Institutions increasingly publish human-remains policies, undertake provenance research and review requests for repatriation or restricted access. International museum organisations now stress that human remains deserve particular respect because they retain cultural, spiritual and personal significance beyond their scientific value. Decisions are increasingly expected to involve consultation rather than relying solely on curatorial authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icom.museum/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Code-of-Ethics_2026_EN-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icom.museum">[International Council of Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icom.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Code of Ethics 2026 EN 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Council of MuseumsICOM STANDING COMMITTEE ON ETHICSMay 7, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2026</span></span></span>

The Kunstkamera sits within this wider international shift. Rather than being viewed simply as an eccentric cabinet of curiosities, it has become part of a broader conversation about how museums should balance scientific knowledge, historical transparency and respect for the dead.

## Why the Museum Still Matters

The Kunstkamera remains one of the world's most historically significant curiosity collections precisely because it embodies competing ideas that continue to shape museums today. It represents the optimism of the Enlightenment, when scientific observation challenged superstition. It also reflects imperial collecting practices that modern institutions increasingly scrutinise.

Its anatomical displays are therefore more than historical oddities. They ask visitors to confront a difficult question: when does preserving human remains serve education, and when does it risk turning people into exhibits? There is no universally accepted answer. Instead, the Kunstkamera has become a place where the history of science and the ethics of museum collecting meet, reminding visitors that museums preserve not only objects and bodies, but also the changing values of the societies that created them.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
