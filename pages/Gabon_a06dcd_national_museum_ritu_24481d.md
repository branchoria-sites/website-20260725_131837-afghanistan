## Introduction

Libreville's National Museum of Arts, Rites and Traditions is one of the most distinctive museums in Central Africa because many of its most important objects were never created as works of art in the modern sense. Masks, reliquary figures, musical instruments, ritual staffs and ceremonial objects originally belonged to living traditions in which sound, dance, initiation, remembrance and community participation mattered as much as the physical object itself. The museum is unusual not because it displays the bizarre, but because it attempts to preserve cultural traditions whose deepest meanings cannot be fully contained within a display case. Its collections therefore offer visitors a rare opportunity to encounter [Gabon]({{ 'where-gabons-strangest-collections-keep/' | relative_url }})'s ritual heritage while also raising important questions about how museums present sacred and still-living cultural practices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/pleins_textes_5/b_fdi_04-05/05035.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horizon.documentation.ird.fr">[Horizon Documentation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horizon.documentation.ird.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horizon DocumentationLe Musée des arts et traditions de Libreville, Gabon = the traditional art and history Museum, Libreville, GabonOcto...</span></span></span>

## From research collection to national museum

The museum began as a research project rather than a conventional public attraction. In 1960 the Gabonese government invited researchers from the French Office for Overseas Scientific and Technical Research (ORSTOM), including ethnomusicologist Herbert Pepper, followed by Pierre Sallée and ethnologist Louis Perrois, to document Gabon's cultural traditions and establish a national collection. Their work was carried out alongside Gabonese collaborators who travelled widely collecting objects, music, oral traditions and ethnographic information before rapid social change could erase many local practices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/pleins_textes_5/b_fdi_04-05/05035.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horizon.documentation.ird.fr">[Horizon Documentation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horizon.documentation.ird.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horizon DocumentationLe Musée des arts et traditions de Libreville, Gabon = the traditional art and history Museum, Libreville, GabonOcto...</span></span></span>

The earliest museum occupied Herbert Pepper's former house in Libreville. As collections expanded through the 1960s, a purpose-built museum became necessary. The institution evolved through several reorganisations before reopening in a modern building in 2019 as the National Museum of Arts, Rites and Traditions of Gabon, reflecting an expanded emphasis on ritual life as well as artistic heritage. The museum's origins continue to shape its identity today. Unlike galleries assembled primarily through purchases, much of its collection emerged from systematic field research. Objects were documented together with recordings, photographs and notes intended to preserve the cultural knowledge surrounding them rather than simply their physical appearance. ## Masks, music and everyday objects on display

The museum deliberately avoids presenting Gabon solely through spectacular ritual masks. Instead, its galleries place ceremonial objects alongside domestic tools, musical instruments, hunting equipment, baskets, pottery, jewellery and archaeological material, illustrating that ritual life formed part of everyday society rather than existing separately from it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/pleins_textes_5/b_fdi_04-05/05035.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horizon.documentation.ird.fr">[Horizon Documentation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horizon.documentation.ird.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horizon DocumentationLe Musée des arts et traditions de Libreville, Gabon = the traditional art and history Museum, Libreville, GabonOcto...</span></span></span>

Among the most striking displays are ceremonial masks associated with different communities across Gabon. In their original settings these masks were activated through movement, costume, music and carefully controlled performances. Displayed motionless in a museum, they inevitably lose part of their original meaning. The institution therefore attempts to explain the social and ceremonial contexts that gave these objects their significance rather than presenting them simply as sculpture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/pleins_textes_5/b_fdi_04-05/05035.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horizon.documentation.ird.fr">[Horizon Documentation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horizon.documentation.ird.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horizon DocumentationLe Musée des arts et traditions de Libreville, Gabon = the traditional art and history Museum, Libreville, GabonOcto...</span></span></span>

Musical heritage is equally important. The museum grew directly out of ethnomusicological research, and Pepper's fieldwork generated extensive collections of songs, instrumental performances and oral traditions. Those recordings remain a valuable companion to the physical collections because they preserve performances that cannot be represented by objects alone. Modern archival projects continue to preserve these historic recordings as part of Gabon's intangible cultural heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.crem-cnrs.fr/archives/fonds/CNRSMH_Pepper/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.crem-cnrs.fr">[archives.crem-cnrs.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.crem-cnrs.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fonds: CNRSMH_Pepper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sound archives of the CNRS and the Musee de l&#x27;Homme. Visitors should also notice how ordinary objects are displayed beside ceremonial ones. Everyday utensils, weaving, fishing equipment and household artefacts reveal that ritual traditions were embedded within wider patterns of work, family life and community identity. This broader presentation helps avoid reducing Gabon's cultures to their most visually dramatic ceremonial objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/pleins_textes_5/b_fdi_04-05/05035.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horizon.documentation.ird.fr">[Horizon Documentation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horizon.documentation.ird.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horizon DocumentationLe Musée des arts et traditions de Libreville, Gabon = the traditional art and history Museum, Libreville, GabonOcto...</span></span></span>

## Why these collections are still living heritage

One feature that distinguishes the museum from many historical collections is that much of the culture represented has not disappeared.

Many ceremonial traditions, musical practices and systems of oral knowledge continue to exist in contemporary Gabon, although often in evolving forms. UNESCO's programmes in Gabon increasingly emphasise safeguarding intangible cultural heritage—the knowledge, performance and skills surrounding cultural practices—as much as preserving physical artefacts. This reflects the understanding that a mask without its songs, dances or ceremonial knowledge represents only part of its original significance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ich.unesco.org/en/state/gabon-GA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ich.unesco.org">[UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ich.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intangible Cultural Heritage Gabon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO Intangible Cultural HeritageGabon - UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage...</span></span></span>

The museum therefore sits at the meeting point of two forms of preservation:

* **Material heritage**, including masks, carvings, musical instruments and ceremonial objects.
* **Living heritage**, including music, oral traditions, ritual knowledge and performance practices that continue outside museum walls.

This approach helps explain why exhibitions increasingly combine objects with interpretation, recordings or educational activities rather than relying on visual display alone. Educational workshops introduced after the museum's reopening also encourage younger generations to understand traditional practices as part of national cultural heritage rather than as relics of a vanished past. ## How to visit without misreading sacred heritage

For visitors interested in unusual museums, the greatest temptation is to see ritual collections as displays of mystery or magic. That approach misses much of what makes the museum important.

Many objects on display originally served specific ceremonial roles connected with remembrance, social authority, initiation, healing or communication with ancestors. Their significance depended upon who could see them, when they appeared and how they were used. Once placed in a museum, they become accessible to everyone, but their original cultural meanings do not disappear.

A thoughtful visit is therefore helped by a few principles:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Read interpretive panels before judging an object by appearance alone.
* Recognise that many masks were designed to move with music and dance rather than stand still.
* Avoid treating ritual objects as evidence of "primitive superstition"; they formed coherent religious and social systems.
* Pay as much attention to the musical instruments, recordings and everyday artefacts as to the famous masks.
* Remember that some traditions represented remain culturally important to communities today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/pleins_textes_5/b_fdi_04-05/05035.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horizon.documentation.ird.fr">[ird.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horizon.documentation.ird.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horizon DocumentationLe Musée des arts et traditions de Libreville, Gabon = the traditional art and history Museum, Libreville, GabonOcto...</span></span></span>

Seen in this way, the museum becomes less a cabinet of curiosities than a place where visitors can appreciate how museums attempt to preserve cultures that are simultaneously historical and living.

## What makes the museum unusual within Gabon's cultural landscape

Countries with extensive curiosity museums often separate folklore, religion, archaeology and ethnography into different institutions. Gabon instead concentrates much of this material within one national museum, making it the country's principal public gateway to ritual culture.

Its distinctiveness comes not from displaying the macabre or sensational but from revealing the challenge of exhibiting objects that once depended on performance, sound and restricted cultural knowledge. The museum preserves remarkable artefacts, yet its most valuable collection may be the relationship it maintains between those objects and the living traditions that continue to give them meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-supports-gabons-national-museum-arts-and-traditions-through-workshop-documentation-and" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">supports Gabon’s national museum of arts and traditions through</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO supports Gabon’s national museum of arts and traditions through...</span></span></span>
