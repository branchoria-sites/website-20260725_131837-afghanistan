## Introduction

[Somalia]({{ 'where-somalia-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s rebuilt museums are unusual not because they display bizarre collections, but because they attempt something far more difficult: reconstructing a national memory after buildings were destroyed, archives scattered and thousands of objects disappeared during decades of conflict. Since the collapse of the Somali state in 1991, museum professionals, government agencies and international organisations have worked to restore institutions that had lost not only their collections but also much of their staff, documentation and infrastructure. For visitors, these museums are therefore best understood as living recovery projects. Empty cases, newly acquired artefacts and reconstructed exhibitions all tell part of the story of what was lost and what can realistically be recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/culture-emergencies/heritage-emergency-fund/post-conflict-rehabilitation-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Post-conflict rehabilitation of the National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Post-conflict rehabilitation of the National Museum - UNESCO Heritage Emergency Fund...</span></span></span>

## Can a Museum Rebuild a Lost National Memory?

A museum normally preserves an existing collection. Somalia's experience reverses that process: the buildings have often been restored before the collections themselves.

When the civil war began in 1991, the National Museum in Mogadishu suffered severe damage. Much of its archaeological, ethnographic and historical collection was looted or dispersed, museum professionals were killed or fled the country, and catalogues that would have identified missing objects were also affected. Reopening therefore required much more than repairing walls. Curators first had to rebuild the institution itself, creating management systems, conservation policies and collection strategies almost from scratch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/culture-emergencies/heritage-emergency-fund/post-conflict-rehabilitation-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Post-conflict rehabilitation of the National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Post-conflict rehabilitation of the National Museum - UNESCO Heritage Emergency Fund...</span></span></span>

This makes the museum an unusual cultural attraction. Visitors are not simply seeing surviving artefacts; they are witnessing an ongoing effort to restore the idea of a national collection after decades in which the country lacked a functioning central museum.

## The Pillaging and Reopening of the National Museum

The National Museum has experienced several distinct lives rather than one continuous history.

Originally established during the Italian colonial period in 1933, it later became part of the Mogadishu Cultural Centre alongside the National Theatre and National Library. During the civil war, all three institutions ceased normal operation, and the museum's collections were extensively pillaged while the building deteriorated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/culture-emergencies/heritage-emergency-fund/post-conflict-rehabilitation-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Post-conflict rehabilitation of the National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Post-conflict rehabilitation of the National Museum - UNESCO Heritage Emergency Fund...</span></span></span>

Restoration of the building began in 2019, culminating in its official reopening on 1 July 2020 during Somalia's Independence Day celebrations. Yet UNESCO repeatedly emphasised that reopening the doors did not mean the museum had fully recovered. The renovated structure initially stood largely without collections, trained staff or modern administrative systems. International support therefore shifted towards practical museum work rather than construction alone:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* establishing governance and management structures;
* training museum staff and curators;
* developing policies for acquisitions and conservation;
* planning educational programmes;
* identifying Somali heritage held elsewhere and encouraging future restitution where appropriate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/2207" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSomalia seeks to strengthen management of its newly rehabilitated National Museum - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

This emphasis reveals an important distinction between rebuilding a museum building and rebuilding a museum institution. In Somalia's case, the second task is proving much longer and more complex.

## Hargeisa's Ruined and Replacement Museums

Northern Somalia offers a parallel story that differs from Mogadishu's experience.

The former Hargeisa Provincial Museum, opened in 1977, developed a substantial ethnographic collection with thousands of locally donated objects representing everyday Somali life. During the destruction of Hargeisa in 1988, however, the museum itself was destroyed along with much of its collection. Later reconstruction efforts focused on creating replacement museum facilities rather than recovering the original displays, many of which had been permanently lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hargeisa_Provincial_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hargeisa Provincial Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hargeisa Provincial Museum</span></span></span>

As a result, museums in Hargeisa today are not straightforward continuations of pre-war institutions. Instead, they combine newly assembled collections with surviving material, reflecting changing understandings of local history, identity and heritage. Rather than recreating the exact exhibitions visitors would have seen before the conflict, they inevitably present a different historical narrative shaped by what survived, what could be donated again and what communities now consider important to preserve.

This illustrates a broader feature of post-conflict museums: rebuilding almost always involves reinterpretation as well as restoration.

## What Visitors Can Realistically Expect Today

Travellers expecting galleries packed with famous masterpieces may be surprised.

Somalia's rebuilt museums are generally modest by international standards, and access can vary depending on security conditions, government activity and local arrangements. The National Museum's reopening represented an important symbolic achievement, but the institution continues to develop its collections and visitor services rather than operating as a fully mature museum with decades of uninterrupted collecting behind it. UNESCO's post-reopening work has concentrated heavily on institutional capacity because sustainable museum practice depends on trained personnel and long-term management as much as on exhibition spaces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/2207" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSomalia seeks to strengthen management of its newly rehabilitated National Museum - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Visitors who are able to enter should therefore expect:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* exhibitions assembled from surviving and newly acquired material rather than complete historical collections;
* displays reflecting archaeology, traditional culture, history and contemporary artistic expression;
* evidence of ongoing reconstruction rather than a finished museum experience;
* a strong emphasis on cultural resilience and national recovery.

</div>

In many ways, the story surrounding the exhibits is as significant as the exhibits themselves.

## Why These Museums Matter Beyond Their Collections

Somalia's rebuilt museums occupy an unusual place within the country's cultural landscape because they preserve absence as much as presence.

Every missing object points to looting, displacement or destruction. Every newly catalogued artefact represents an attempt to reconnect present-day Somalis with parts of their cultural heritage that nearly disappeared from public view. This makes the museums important not only for historians but also for education, reconciliation and nation-building.

UNESCO has consistently described the National Museum as part of a broader peacebuilding effort alongside the revival of other cultural institutions. Rather than functioning solely as repositories of old objects, rebuilt museums are intended to become places where younger generations encounter a shared cultural history that survived conflict, even when many of the original collections did not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://somalia.un.org/en/99477-unesco-heritage-emergency-fund-supports-revival-somalia-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: somalia.un.org">[un.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">somalia.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on un.org.</span></span></span>

Within Somalia's landscape of unusual heritage sites, these museums stand out because their greatest curiosity is not a strange artefact but the remarkable process of reconstructing public memory after so much of the original material record was lost.
