## Introduction

Havana's Napoleonic Museum is one of Cuba's most unusual cultural collections and among the most significant museums dedicated to Napoleon Bonaparte anywhere in the Americas. Rather than celebrating Cuban history, it preserves the remarkable obsession of one twentieth-century collector whose passion for the French emperor eventually became a national museum. Visitors encounter imperial portraits, military uniforms, furniture, books, medals, weapons and one of the museum's most discussed objects: a death mask associated with Napoleon's final physician. The result is not simply a museum about Napoleon, but a rare example of private collecting transformed into public heritage, revealing how European imperial history found an unexpected home in revolutionary Cuba.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/havanas-museo-napoleonico-reopens-30-march-2011/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">havanas museo napoleonico reopens 30 march 2011</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Havana&#x27;s Museo Napoleonico reopens (30 March, 2011) - napoleon.orgMarch 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 2011</span></span></span>

## Why Is Napoleon's Death Mask in Havana?

The museum's best-known object is a plaster death mask linked to Francesco Antommarchi, Napoleon's personal physician during his exile on Saint Helena. Antommarchi later travelled to the Caribbean and eventually settled in eastern Cuba, where he died in 1838. His connection with the island created an unusual historical bridge between Napoleonic Europe and Cuba, helping explain why objects associated with him entered Cuban collections rather than remaining exclusively in France.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/havanas-museo-napoleonico-reopens-30-march-2011/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">havanas museo napoleonico reopens 30 march 2011</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Havana&#x27;s Museo Napoleonico reopens (30 March, 2011) - napoleon.orgMarch 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 2011</span></span></span>

The Havana museum displays two nineteenth-century Antommarchi-model death masks rather than claiming possession of the unique original cast. That distinction matters because the history of Napoleon's death mask is one of the most disputed episodes in Napoleonic collecting. Historians generally agree that a mould was taken shortly after Napoleon's death in May 1821, but disagreement continues over exactly who controlled the original cast, which sections survived and how later copies descended from it. Modern scholarship treats the various surviving masks as a complex family of related objects rather than accepting any single example as unquestionably authentic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/havanas-museo-napoleonico-reopens-30-march-2011/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">havanas museo napoleonico reopens 30 march 2011</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Havana&#x27;s Museo Napoleonico reopens (30 March, 2011) - napoleon.orgMarch 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 2011</span></span></span>

This uncertainty actually increases the museum's interest. Instead of presenting an unquestionable relic, the death mask illustrates how historical memory, collecting and prestige can become intertwined. Visitors are looking not only at a physical object but also at two centuries of debate over provenance, replication and authenticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/close-up/napoleons-death-mask/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s death mask</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Napoleon&#x27;s death mask - napoleon.org...</span></span></span>

## How Cuban Collectors Built the Napoleonic Collection

The museum exists largely because of Julio Lobo, the wealthy Cuban sugar entrepreneur often called the "Sugar King". During the first half of the twentieth century he assembled one of the world's largest private Napoleonic collections, purchasing paintings, sculptures, books, furniture, medals, military equipment and decorative arts through European dealers and auction houses. His enthusiasm became famous among Parisian antiquarian booksellers and collectors because he pursued Napoleonic material with unusual determination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/havanas-museo-napoleonico-reopens-30-march-2011/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">havanas museo napoleonico reopens 30 march 2011</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Havana&#x27;s Museo Napoleonico reopens (30 March, 2011) - napoleon.orgMarch 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 2011</span></span></span>

Following the Cuban Revolution, Lobo left the country. His collection remained in Cuba and became the foundation of the Museo Napoleónico, established in 1961. Instead of being dispersed through auctions or inherited by private owners, thousands of objects entered a public institution, giving Cuba an internationally distinctive museum despite the country's limited direct connection with Napoleon himself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/havanas-museo-napoleonico-reopens-30-march-2011/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">havanas museo napoleonico reopens 30 march 2011</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Havana&#x27;s Museo Napoleonico reopens (30 March, 2011) - napoleon.orgMarch 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 2011</span></span></span>

The museum occupies an elaborate Florentine Renaissance-style mansion built during the 1920s, creating another layer of collecting history. Visitors therefore encounter not only Napoleonic artefacts but also a grand Havana residence adapted to display European imperial culture in a Caribbean setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/havanas-museo-napoleonico-reopens-30-march-2011/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">havanas museo napoleonico reopens 30 march 2011</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Havana&#x27;s Museo Napoleonico reopens (30 March, 2011) - napoleon.orgMarch 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 2011</span></span></span>

## The Death Mask and Its Contested Copies

Death masks occupied an important place in nineteenth-century memorial culture, allowing admirers and sculptors to preserve the appearance of famous individuals before photography became widespread. Napoleon's case became exceptionally controversial because several competing lineages of masks emerged during the nineteenth century.

Current specialist research argues that no single surviving mask can be accepted without qualification as "the" original. Instead, scholars distinguish between different families of casts, including those associated with Dr Francis Burton, Count Bertrand and Antommarchi. Their relationships remain only partly documented, and some later reproductions were commercially produced decades after Napoleon's death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/close-up/napoleons-death-mask/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s death mask</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Napoleon&#x27;s death mask - napoleon.org...</span></span></span>

For visitors to Havana, this means the museum's masks should be understood as historically significant Napoleonic artefacts connected to the Antommarchi tradition rather than unquestionable first-generation originals. That careful distinction reflects current historical scholarship more accurately than older descriptions that simply referred to "Napoleon's death mask".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/close-up/napoleons-death-mask/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s death mask</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Napoleon&#x27;s death mask - napoleon.org...</span></span></span>

## What the Museum Reveals About Private Collecting

The Museo Napoleónico demonstrates how private collecting can preserve an entire historical world rather than isolated souvenirs. Lobo did not merely acquire relics. He assembled paintings, furniture, decorative arts, military objects, manuscripts and printed works that collectively recreated the political and cultural universe surrounding Napoleon.

This approach differs from curiosity cabinets built around bizarre objects alone. The museum instead shows how obsession itself can become historically valuable. A collector's determination to gather every conceivable category of Napoleonic material produced a coherent public collection that later generations could study, even after its original owner disappeared from Cuban public life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/havanas-museo-napoleonico-reopens-30-march-2011/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: napoleon.org">[napoleon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">napoleon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">havanas museo napoleonico reopens 30 march 2011</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Havana&#x27;s Museo Napoleonico reopens (30 March, 2011) - napoleon.orgMarch 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 2011</span></span></span>

The collection also reflects the international circulation of prestige objects during the twentieth century. French imperial memorabilia travelled through European dealers before reaching a Caribbean collector, only to become part of a state museum after the Revolution. Few museums demonstrate this chain of collecting, politics and cultural preservation so clearly.
