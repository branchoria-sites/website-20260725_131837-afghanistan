## Introduction

India’s strangest museums are rarely strange for strangeness’s sake. A museum of toilets in Delhi is really about [sanitation]({{ 'sanitation/' | relative_url }}) and social reform. A collection of preserved brains in Bengaluru teaches neuroscience and public health. A village museum in Assam turns traditions of magic, healing and folklore into local heritage. Elsewhere, Egyptian mummies, prison cells, ritual masks, antique cooking vessels and vehicles shaped like shoes or hamburgers reveal how unusually broad the idea of a museum can become.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/India_967ce3-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for What Makes India&#x27;s Museums So Unexpected?" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

There is no single national circuit of “weird museums”. India’s curiosity culture is scattered among government institutions, universities, charitable organisations, private collectors, restaurants, historic prisons and community museums. Some collections are carefully researched; others blend scholarship with tourism, family memory, religious belief or local legend. The most rewarding visits therefore involve more than finding an eccentric object. They ask who collected it, what purpose the display serves and where documented history ends and storytelling begins.

## Mummies, brains and the uneasy display of bodies

India’s unusual collections include several displays of human remains, but they differ sharply in origin and purpose. The Egyptian Gallery at the Indian Museum in Kolkata contains an Egyptian mummy and a mummified hand. The museum presents these within an archaeological collection, alongside material intended to explain ancient Egyptian funerary culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://indianmuseumkolkata.org/gallery/egypt-gallery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indianmuseumkolkata.org">[indianmuseumkolkata.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indianmuseumkolkata.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on indianmuseumkolkata.org.</span></span></span>

The mummy is one of the Indian Museum’s most popular exhibits, together with fossils, dinosaur eggs and large zoological specimens. Its presence also reflects the global collecting practices of nineteenth-century museums, when Egyptian bodies and funerary objects were transported internationally and displayed as scientific or archaeological specimens. The Indian Museum itself developed within that colonial museum tradition, making the mummy both an ancient person and an artefact in the history of institutional collecting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://indianmuseumkolkata.org/gallery/egypt-gallery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indianmuseumkolkata.org">[indianmuseumkolkata.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indianmuseumkolkata.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on indianmuseumkolkata.org.</span></span></span>

An Egyptian mummy at the Telangana State Archaeology Museum in Hyderabad illustrates a different problem: preservation. Reporting in July 2025 described visible deterioration and a need for substantial conservation work, despite earlier gallery renovation. The case shows why the existence of an extraordinary object does not necessarily mean that it is securely funded, well interpreted or displayed under ideal conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/city/hyderabad/in-dire-straits-at-telangana-archaeology-museum-2100-year-old-egyptian-mummy-cries-out-for-help/articleshow/122304815.cms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: timesofindia.indiatimes.com">[The Times of India]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">timesofindia.indiatimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on indiatimes.com.</span></span></span>

Medical displays such as the NIMHANS brains raise related questions. Human tissue can teach visitors about disease in a way that illustrations cannot, but it also requires careful consent, respectful language and clear educational framing. International museum practice increasingly treats human remains as a sensitive category rather than merely another form of specimen. Visitors should therefore judge these galleries not by how shocking they are, but by whether they explain provenance, medical purpose and the dignity owed to the dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumsassociation.org/museums-journal/features/2024/06/perspectives-on-human-remains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumsassociation.org">[Museums Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumsassociation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">perspectives on human remains</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">perspectives on human remains</span></span></span>

## Where folklore becomes a collection

India’s folklore museums often provide the country’s closest equivalent to collections of monsters, spirits, witchcraft and supernatural belief. They may not contain “proof” of uncanny beings, but they preserve the masks, costumes, ritual objects, stories and performance traditions through which such beings acquire cultural form.

The Folklore Museum at the University of Mysore was established as an academic collection of Karnataka’s folk arts and crafts. Its significance lies in bringing together material that might otherwise remain dispersed among performers, villages and private households. As a university museum, it is more closely connected to documentation and scholarship than most tourist-led collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folklore_Museum_%28Mysuru%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folklore Museum (Mysuru</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folklore Museum (Mysuru</span></span></span>

In Kochi, the privately run Kerala Folklore Museum is known for densely arranged architecture, sculpture, masks, ritual art and performance-related objects. Such a collection can be visually overwhelming, and labels may not always answer every question about date, locality or ritual use. Nevertheless, its masks and carved figures offer an immediate route into traditions in which gods, demons, ancestors and legendary characters are made physically present through costume and performance.

The Loktak Folklore Museum on Thanga Island in Manipur represents a more local model. Its collection is tied to the communities, fishing practices, beliefs, medicines and oral literature surrounding Loktak Lake. Rather than presenting folklore as a national category, it connects stories and objects to one ecological landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loktak_Folklore_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loktak Folklore Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Loktak Folklore Museum</span></span></span>

These museums are important because supernatural traditions are easily distorted when separated from performance and community life. A ritual mask is not simply a frightening face; its meaning depends on who wears it, during which ceremony, before which audience and under what religious authority. The best folklore interpretation restores those connections instead of reducing sacred or ceremonial objects to decorative curiosities.

## Prisons, punishment and national memory

Not every strange museum depends on bizarre objects. Historic prisons can be among the most unsettling collections because the building itself is the main exhibit.

The Cellular Jail in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands was constructed by British colonial authorities around a system of individual cells and surveillance. Indian political prisoners were exiled there and subjected to isolation and harsh treatment. It is now a National Memorial with museum, photographic and art displays, while a sound-and-light presentation turns the site’s history into an evening visitor experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tourism.andamannicobar.gov.in/museums_details.php?data=3FrijLjZQnTDeT1B68Z1BNb31UPYTtkjlaIbLgKfgchL7VILE_uvVVM" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tourism.andamannicobar.gov.in">[andamannicobar.gov.in]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tourism.andamannicobar.gov.in</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on andamannicobar.gov.in.</span></span></span>

The site occupies an awkward position between memorial, patriotic destination and tourist attraction. Its surviving cells and architecture communicate confinement more directly than conventional display cases can. At the same time, theatrical narration and heroic national framing can simplify the diversity of prisoners, political movements and experiences associated with the jail.

A thoughtful visit should therefore separate three layers: the documented function of the prison, the individual histories of those confined there and the later construction of the jail as a national symbol. The official presentation understandably centres resistance to colonial rule, but the physical site also raises wider questions about surveillance, forced labour, transportation and the use of remote territory as an instrument of punishment.

## Older museums with cabinets of curiosity inside them

Some of India’s most unusual exhibits are hidden within otherwise conventional institutions. The Indian Museum in Kolkata is the clearest example. Alongside archaeology and art are an Egyptian mummy, fossils, zoological specimens, dinosaur eggs and monumental skeletal displays. Its appeal resembles an old encyclopaedic cabinet: many branches of knowledge gathered under one roof, sometimes with the atmosphere of an earlier age of collecting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://indianmuseumkolkata.org/gallery/egypt-gallery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indianmuseumkolkata.org">[indianmuseumkolkata.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indianmuseumkolkata.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on indianmuseumkolkata.org.</span></span></span>

Natural-history and medical museums can contain malformed specimens, preserved animals, skeletons and pathological material, although access is often restricted because many belong to universities or teaching hospitals. Public information may be limited, opening arrangements may depend on staff availability and some collections are intended primarily for students. They should not automatically be treated as tourist attractions simply because older travel articles mention them.

The same caution applies to archaeological stores and small regional museums. A striking object may be real, misidentified, poorly labelled or detached from its excavation history. Claims about impossible artefacts, giants, miraculous relics or lost civilisations should be checked against institutional catalogues and archaeological research. An object’s mystery often reflects incomplete documentation rather than a genuine anomaly.

## How credible are the displays?

India’s unusual museums range from national institutions to one-person collections, so standards of documentation vary considerably. A useful credibility test is to ask four questions.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Who runs the collection?** A government museum, university laboratory, charitable foundation, private business and village heritage centre will have different goals and resources.
* **Are objects labelled with provenance?** Dates, places of origin, makers, donors and collection histories allow claims to be checked.
* **Is belief distinguished from evidence?** Folklore museums can present magical traditions responsibly without claiming that supernatural events have been scientifically proved.
* **What is the visitor experience designed to achieve?** Education, devotion, commemoration, entertainment and commercial promotion can overlap, but they should not be confused.

</div>

Sulabh, for example, uses an eccentric subject to advocate sanitation reform. NIMHANS uses visually confronting specimens to teach neuroscience. Mayong preserves and markets a supernatural reputation whose historical basis is partly oral and contested. Sudha Cars celebrates an individual maker’s imagination. Cellular Jail uses a preserved site to support national remembrance. Each is credible in a different way, provided its claims are judged according to its actual purpose.

## Planning an unusual-museum journey

India’s curiosity collections are geographically dispersed, and several maintain limited or changeable opening arrangements. Official museum or tourism pages should be checked shortly before visiting, especially for university museums, medical institutions and small private collections.

Delhi offers the most straightforward single stop in the Sulabh museum, while Bengaluru combines the NIMHANS collection with larger scientific and cultural institutions. Ahmedabad’s Vechaar Museum works well for visitors interested in design, crafts and overlooked domestic technologies. Hyderabad offers the playful Sudha Cars collection as well as an Egyptian mummy whose conservation condition has attracted concern. Kolkata’s Indian Museum is the best choice for an old-fashioned mixture of mummies, fossils, skeletons, archaeology and natural history.

Mayong requires more interpretive care and more travel planning. Its appeal lies not only in the museum but in the relationship between the village, its stories and Assam’s tourism economy. The Cellular Jail is more solemn and historically substantial, and should be approached as a memorial rather than an eccentric photo opportunity.

Visitors should also be prepared for uneven display quality. Smaller museums may have dense cases, limited English labelling, uncertain photography rules or no sophisticated digital guide. That does not make them worthless. In many cases, their idiosyncratic arrangement is part of what reveals the collector’s priorities. It does, however, mean that confident claims heard from guides or repeated online should sometimes be treated as local interpretation rather than settled fact.

## Why India’s curiosity culture matters

India’s unusual museums show that collecting is not confined to royal treasure, fine art or national archaeology. Toilets can tell a history of public health. Cooking pots can preserve lost skills and domestic labour. Brains can make disease visible. Masks can hold relationships between performance, belief and community. A novelty car can become both sculpture and functioning machine. A prison cell can preserve political memory more powerfully than a ceremonial monument.

They also reveal tensions within museum culture. Private enthusiasm can rescue objects that official institutions overlook, but may produce weak documentation. Tourism can sustain a folklore collection while encouraging exaggerated legends. Medical and archaeological specimens can educate while raising ethical questions. National memorials can preserve suffering while compressing complex lives into a single heroic story.

The best of India’s strange collections do not merely display unusual things. They expose the systems that decide what deserves to survive: scholarship, devotion, family collecting, political memory, social reform, commercial entertainment and local pride. Their oddness is therefore not peripheral to Indian history. It is one of the clearest ways to see how the country classifies the sacred, the shameful, the ordinary, the wondrous and the difficult to explain.
