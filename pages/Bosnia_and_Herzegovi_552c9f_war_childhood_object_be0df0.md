## Introduction

The most unusual objects in Sarajevo’s War Childhood Museum are often the least remarkable at first glance: a worn toy, a school notebook, a hand-sewn doll, a letter, a pair of shoes or an improvised game. None of these items is historically important because of its rarity or monetary value. Instead, each becomes evidence when it is paired with the first-person testimony of the child who owned it. The object and the memory are treated as inseparable, transforming everyday belongings into records of lived experience rather than anonymous museum pieces. This approach distinguishes the museum from conventional military collections, shifting attention away from weapons and political leaders towards the experiences of children whose lives were shaped by the Bosnian War. The result is one of [Bosnia and Herzegovina]({{ 'bosnia-and-herzegovina/' | relative_url }})'s most distinctive contributions to the preservation of difficult history.

## Why personal objects carry historical weight

Most war museums rely heavily on official documents, military equipment or chronological narratives. The War Childhood Museum deliberately reverses that perspective. It starts with the individual child rather than the conflict itself, asking what object best captures a person's experience of growing up during war.

A teddy bear is therefore not exhibited because it survived a siege. It is exhibited because its owner explains why it mattered: perhaps it was carried into shelters every night, repaired repeatedly because replacements were impossible to obtain, or became a source of comfort when family life was disrupted. Without the accompanying story, it would simply be an ordinary toy.

This pairing of object and testimony turns familiar belongings into first-person historical evidence. Visitors are encouraged to understand that the object's significance lies not in its physical characteristics but in its relationship to memory. A broken pencil case, a birthday card or an improvised football can reveal daily routines, fear, resilience and creativity that rarely appear in official wartime archives. The museum explicitly collects books, letters, photographs, broken toys, improvised clothing and other childhood possessions because their meaning comes from the experiences attached to them rather than from their appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warchildhood.org/contribute-your-memories-to-the-wcm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warchildhood.org">[War Childhood]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warchildhood.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">War Childhood Contribute Your Memories to the WCM</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">War ChildhoodContribute Your Memories to the WCM - War Childhood Museum...</span></span></span>

The collection therefore documents aspects of conflict that military history often overlooks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* maintaining friendships despite siege conditions;
* inventing games during bombardment;
* preserving family rituals;
* adapting ordinary childhood activities to extraordinary danger;
* remembering moments of happiness alongside trauma.

</div>

These experiences become historically visible only because contributors explain what the objects meant to them.

## How testimony changes the meaning of a collection

The museum's defining curatorial decision is that stories are not labels attached after an object has entered the collection. Instead, testimony is treated as part of the object itself.

Every donated item is accompanied by a written personal account, and many contributors also provide recorded oral histories. Researchers document these memories using collection procedures adapted from international museum standards while recognising that emotionally sensitive interviews require different methods from conventional object cataloguing. Psychologists are involved in developing interview practice, reflecting the fact that contributors are recalling deeply personal experiences rather than simply identifying artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warchildhood.org/research-methodology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warchildhood.org">[War Childhood]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warchildhood.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">War Childhood Research methodology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">War ChildhoodResearch methodology - War Childhood Museum...</span></span></span>

This changes how visitors interpret even the simplest exhibit.

A visitor might first notice:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* a marble;
* a handwritten letter;
* a school exercise book;
* a homemade toy.

</div>

Only after reading the testimony does the item acquire its historical meaning. The evidence is therefore cumulative: the physical object demonstrates survival, while the accompanying narrative explains its emotional, social and practical importance.

Unlike collections where curators interpret anonymous artefacts on behalf of the public, the primary voice belongs to the former child. The museum deliberately reduces the distance between witness and visitor by allowing contributors to explain their own memories in the first person.

## Curating childhood without sensationalising war

One of the museum's most distinctive achievements is its refusal to compete with more graphic war museums. Rather than relying on shocking imagery or displays of violence, it demonstrates the effects of conflict through ordinary domestic life.

This approach avoids presenting childhood solely through victimhood. Many testimonies describe humour, friendships, games, family celebrations or small moments of normality that persisted despite siege conditions. The collection therefore presents wartime childhood as complex rather than uniformly tragic.

Annual exhibition rotations reinforce this philosophy. Only a small proportion of the museum's thousands of donated objects can be displayed at one time, so exhibitions are organised around different dimensions of childhood experience rather than attempting to tell a complete military history. Visitors encounter roughly forty carefully selected stories during each rotation, allowing each object sufficient space for reflection instead of overwhelming audiences with large quantities of material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warchildhood.org/permanent-exhibition-sarajevo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warchildhood.org">[War Childhood]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warchildhood.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">War Childhood Permanent Exhibition Sarajevo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">War ChildhoodPermanent Exhibition Sarajevo - War Childhood Museum...</span></span></span>

The emphasis remains on individual lives rather than spectacle.

## From personal memory to historical evidence

The museum demonstrates that first-person evidence can consist of far more than written memoirs. Material culture—the objects people keep, repair and carry through difficult periods—can preserve historical knowledge that would otherwise disappear.

Many contributors donated possessions decades after the Bosnian War because they had quietly kept them at home as reminders of childhood. The museum's founders recognised that these fragile collections were at constant risk of being discarded during house moves, damaged through age or simply forgotten as generations changed. Creating a formal museum collection transformed private keepsakes into a protected historical archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warchildhood.org/the-idea-mission-and-vision/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warchildhood.org">[War Childhood]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warchildhood.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">War Childhood The Idea, Mission and Vision &#124; War Childhood Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">War ChildhoodThe Idea, Mission and Vision &#124; War Childhood MuseumJuly 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 27, 2015</span></span></span>

The institution now preserves thousands of objects alongside photographs, letters, documents and hundreds of hours of recorded testimony, allowing researchers to study not only the physical artefacts but also the memories attached to them. Collection management follows recognised museum standards while adapting acquisition procedures to ensure that personal stories remain inseparable from the donated belongings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warchildhood.org/research-methodology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warchildhood.org">[War Childhood]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warchildhood.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">War Childhood Research methodology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">War ChildhoodResearch methodology - War Childhood Museum...</span></span></span>

## Why this collection stands out in Bosnia and Herzegovina

Within Bosnia and Herzegovina's landscape of unusual museums and collections, the War Childhood Museum is distinctive because its principal artefacts are neither rare nor mysterious. Their significance comes entirely from human experience.

Where other museums preserve military hardware, political documents or archaeological treasures, this collection preserves the evidence of ordinary childhood. A toy, letter or handmade object becomes historically valuable only because someone who lived through war explains why it mattered.

That combination of physical object and first-person testimony gives the collection an unusual authority. It records not simply what happened during conflict, but how war was experienced by children in their everyday lives. In doing so, it turns the most ordinary possessions into some of the most powerful evidence of Bosnia and Herzegovina's recent past.
