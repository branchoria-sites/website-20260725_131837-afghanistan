## Introduction

[Luxembourg]({{ 'where-luxembourg-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s wartime museums are unusual because they rely heavily on immersive reconstruction rather than rows of display cases. Life-size mannequins, recreated streets, battlefield scenes and domestic interiors place visitors inside the experience of occupation, combat and persecution. Far from being entertainment, these reconstructions are intended to preserve memories that are rapidly passing beyond living witness. They also raise difficult questions: how realistic should museums become, where is the boundary between education and spectacle, and how can dramatic displays remain faithful to historical evidence?

## Life-size Battle of the Bulge scenes in Diekirch

The National Museum of Military History in Diekirch is internationally known for its extensive use of full-scale dioramas. Rather than placing uniforms or equipment in isolated glass cabinets, the museum reconstructs moments from the winter of 1944–45 when Luxembourg became one of the principal battlegrounds of the Battle of the Bulge.

Around fifty life-size scenes depict soldiers, medics, refugees and civilians caught in the fighting. Many are built around original equipment, authentic buildings or carefully researched reproductions, with mannequins arranged to recreate specific incidents. The intention is not simply to display military hardware but to show how combat unfolded at human scale. Visitors move through ruined streets, command posts, aid stations and battlefield positions that combine thousands of original objects with carefully staged environments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mnhm.net/cms/index.php/explore/battle-of-the-bulge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnhm.net">[mnhm.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnhm.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BATTL E OF THE BULGE</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BATTL E OF THE BULGE</span></span></span>

What distinguishes Diekirch from many military museums is the way these reconstructions were developed. Museum staff worked for decades with veterans from both Allied and German forces alongside civilian witnesses from Luxembourg. Their recollections informed the positioning of equipment, uniforms, gestures and even facial expressions within the displays. The museum presents these reconstructions as acts of historical interpretation rather than theatrical fiction, recognising that memory itself is incomplete and must be checked against documents, photographs and surviving artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visit-diekirch.lu/en/fiche/museum/national-museum-of-military-history-diekirch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit-diekirch.lu">[Tourist Office Diekirch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit-diekirch.lu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tourist Office Diekirch National Museum of Military History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tourist Office DiekirchNational Museum of Military History - Tourist Office Diekirch...</span></span></span>

The museum also deliberately broadens the perspective beyond military operations. Its stated aim is to represent American, German and civilian experiences objectively, avoiding a simplistic narrative of heroes and villains while still acknowledging the realities of Nazi occupation and the suffering it caused. This balanced approach is unusual among battlefield museums, where displays often concentrate almost exclusively on tactics or equipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mnhm.net/mnhm/index.php/explore/battle-of-the-bulge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnhm.net">[mnhm.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnhm.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Battle of the Bulge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Battle of the Bulge</span></span></span>

## Resistance, deportation and survivor-led collecting

Luxembourg's difficult wartime memory extends well beyond battlefield reconstruction. Other museums concentrate on occupation, repression and the experiences of ordinary people whose stories cannot easily be represented through military collections alone.

The National Museum of Resistance and Human Rights in Esch-sur-Alzette traces Luxembourg's experience under Nazi rule between 1940 and 1945. Rather than recreating combat, it focuses on resistance movements, forced labour, compulsory military service, concentration camps and the persecution of Luxembourg's Jewish population. Photographs, personal possessions, artworks and documentary material place individual lives at the centre of the narrative, showing how occupation affected families and communities rather than only armies. Musée national de la Résistance<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://musee-resistance.lu/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: musee-resistance.lu">[musee-resistance.lu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">musee-resistance.lu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Musée national de la Résistance National Museum of Résistance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Musée national de la RésistanceNational Museum of Résistance - Esch-sur-Alzette, Luxemburg...</span></span></span>

Another important site is the Museum-Memorial of Deportation at the former Hollerich railway station in Luxembourg City. The location itself is the principal artefact. During the occupation it became the departure point for thousands of Luxembourgers who were forcibly relocated or conscripted into German military service, while the exhibition also addresses the deportation of Jews from Luxembourg. The museum's restrained presentation contrasts with the immersive battle scenes at Diekirch, illustrating how places of memory sometimes require preservation rather than reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitluxembourg.com/place/museum-memorial-of-deportation-luxembourg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitluxembourg.com">[Visit Luxembourg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitluxembourg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Luxembourg Museum-Memorial of Deportation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit LuxembourgMuseum-Memorial of Deportation - Visit Luxembourg...</span></span></span>

Together these institutions demonstrate different approaches to remembrance. One recreates physical environments to help visitors visualise events, while the others rely more heavily on authentic documents, survivor testimony and historically significant locations. Both methods seek to preserve experiences that become harder to communicate as eyewitnesses disappear.

## Where reconstruction ends and responsible remembrance begins

Life-size mannequins inevitably provoke debate. Hyper-realistic reconstructions can make history emotionally accessible, but they also risk blurring the line between historical interpretation and dramatic spectacle.

The museums themselves openly acknowledge this challenge. The National Museum of Military History describes its mission as keeping wartime memory alive through an informative, objective and impartial presentation rather than through simplistic narratives or dramatic exaggeration. Dioramas are therefore supported by archival photographs, personal belongings, official documents and extensive contextual interpretation rather than standing alone as visual attractions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mnhm.net/mnhm/index.php/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnhm.net">[mnhm.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnhm.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mnhm.net.</span></span></span>

This approach reflects a broader shift within European remembrance culture. As veterans and survivors become fewer, museums increasingly depend on material culture and carefully researched reconstruction to communicate experiences that can no longer be explained directly by eyewitnesses. In Luxembourg, the reconstruction of a field hospital or a snow-covered battlefield is intended to act as a bridge between documentary evidence and human imagination, not as a substitute for historical scholarship.

Visitors therefore encounter two complementary forms of authenticity. Original artefacts provide factual evidence of the past, while reconstructed scenes help explain how those objects were actually used and what they meant to the people who experienced the war. The effectiveness of the display depends on maintaining a clear distinction between original evidence and interpretative reconstruction.

## Why these museums matter within Luxembourg's curiosity landscape


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Among Luxembourg's more unusual visitor attractions, wartime museums occupy a distinctive position. Their curiosity lies not in supernatural claims, bizarre collections or sensational stories, but in the extraordinary density of carefully assembled human memory.

</div>

The mannequins at Diekirch are memorable because they recreate moments of fear, exhaustion and survival with unusual realism. Yet they are always anchored by original artefacts, documented events and decades of collecting informed by veterans and local communities. Elsewhere, museums devoted to resistance, deportation and persecution show that some histories are better conveyed through testimony, documents and preserved places than through reconstruction.

Together these collections reveal how Luxembourg has transformed difficult national memories into public heritage. Rather than presenting war as spectacle, they use immersive techniques to encourage reflection on occupation, violence and civilian experience, making them among the country's most distinctive and thought-provoking museums.
