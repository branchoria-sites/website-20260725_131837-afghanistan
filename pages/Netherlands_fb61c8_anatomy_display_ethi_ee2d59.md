## Introduction

The [Netherlands]({{ 'netherlands/' | relative_url }}) is home to some of Europe's most important anatomical collections, but today they are valued as much for the ethical questions they raise as for their scientific importance. Museums in Amsterdam and Leiden preserve human remains that were collected over centuries for medical teaching, anatomical research and, at times, racial science. Modern visitors therefore encounter two stories at once: the history of anatomy and the changing understanding of consent, dignity and human rights. Rather than presenting preserved bodies simply as medical curiosities, Dutch institutions increasingly explain who collected them, why they were acquired, whose voices were ignored and how museums now address restitution, provenance research and respectful display.

## When Does Anatomy Become an Ethical Problem?

The ethical debate begins with a simple question: when does a scientific specimen stop being an educational object and remain, above all, a person?

For much of European medical history, anatomy depended on a limited supply of bodies. Universities often dissected the remains of executed criminals, while hospitals and medical schools acquired unclaimed bodies or anatomical specimens under legal and social systems that would not meet modern standards of informed consent. Collections expanded because they were useful for teaching, but they also became symbols of prestige for professors and institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumvrolik.nl/en/about-the-museum/human-remains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumvrolik.nl">[Museum Vrolik]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumvrolik.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Vrolik Human remains</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum VrolikHuman remains - Museum VrolikMarch 16, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2026</span></span></span>

Today, Dutch museums generally frame these collections differently. Human remains are displayed only where they serve a clear educational purpose, and institutions increasingly explain how older collecting practices reflected unequal power, colonial expansion and changing scientific beliefs rather than timeless medical necessity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rmo.nl/en/collection/provenance/human-remains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rmo.nl">[Rijksmuseum van Oudheden]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rmo.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rijksmuseum van Oudheden Human Remains &#124; Rijksmuseum van Oudheden</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rijksmuseum van OudhedenHuman Remains &#124; Rijksmuseum van OudhedenMarch 2, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2023</span></span></span>

## What Visitors See at Museum Vrolik

Museum Vrolik in Amsterdam is perhaps the Netherlands' most direct encounter with the ethics of anatomical collecting. Its collection contains around 25,000 anatomical preparations, including preserved organs, skeletons, embryos, foetal specimens and pathological material assembled largely between the eighteenth and twentieth centuries. Many visitors come because the displays are visually extraordinary, but the museum deliberately encourages them to think beyond shock or curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumvrolik.nl/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumvrolik.nl">[Museum Vrolik]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumvrolik.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Vrolik Home</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum VrolikHome - Museum Vrolik...</span></span></span>

Roughly half of the collection consists of human remains. The museum explains that these were collected for medical education and research, although nineteenth-century anatomists also gained professional status through building such collections. Rather than hiding this history, Museum Vrolik openly discusses how scientific ambition, collecting culture and social inequality became intertwined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumvrolik.nl/en/about-the-museum/human-remains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumvrolik.nl">[Museum Vrolik]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumvrolik.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Vrolik Human remains</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum VrolikHuman remains - Museum VrolikMarch 16, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2026</span></span></span>

Several visitor policies reflect this approach:

* Photography is prohibited out of respect for the deceased and to discourage decontextualised sharing on social media.
* Human remains are presented within medical and historical interpretation rather than as entertainment or spectacle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumvrolik.nl/en/about-the-museum/human-remains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumvrolik.nl">[Museum Vrolik]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumvrolik.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Vrolik Human remains</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum VrolikHuman remains - Museum VrolikMarch 16, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2026</span></span></span>

These policies illustrate a broader shift in museum practice. The remains are treated as individuals who continue to contribute to medical knowledge, not simply as unusual objects.

## Anatomical Theatres, Punishment and Public Spectacle

The Netherlands also preserves the memory of an earlier culture in which anatomy was public performance as well as scientific investigation.

Rijksmuseum Boerhaave in Leiden contains a reconstruction of the famous anatomical theatre established by Leiden University in 1594. In the original theatre, the bodies of executed criminals were dissected before audiences that included students, surgeons and members of the public who purchased admission. Winter dissections became educational events as well as civic spectacles, while [cabinets]({{ 'cabinets/' | relative_url }}) of curiosities surrounded the theatre during the rest of the year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rijksmuseumboerhaave.nl/en/our-worlds/anatomical-theatre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rijksmuseumboerhaave.nl">[rijksmuseumboerhaave.nl]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rijksmuseumboerhaave.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anatomical Theatre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rijksmuseum Boerhaave...</span></span></span>

Modern interpretation places these events in historical context rather than celebrating them. Executed criminals were among the few legally available bodies, making punishment part of medical education. The theatre therefore illustrates how criminal justice, scientific progress and public entertainment once overlapped in ways that would now appear ethically troubling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rijksmuseumboerhaave.nl/en/our-worlds/anatomical-theatre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rijksmuseumboerhaave.nl">[rijksmuseumboerhaave.nl]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rijksmuseumboerhaave.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anatomical Theatre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rijksmuseum Boerhaave...</span></span></span>

For visitors, the reconstructed theatre demonstrates that ethical standards have changed dramatically. What seventeenth-century audiences regarded as legitimate public instruction is now interpreted through questions of dignity, coercion and unequal treatment after death.

## Consent, Racial Science and the Return of Remains

The most difficult ethical questions no longer concern anatomy alone but the origins of many museum collections.

Museum Vrolik acknowledges that, for many individuals represented in its collections, it is impossible to determine whether they or their families ever consented to their remains being collected. The museum therefore treats provenance research—the investigation of where remains came from and how they were acquired—as an ongoing responsibility rather than a completed historical exercise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumvrolik.nl/en/about-the-museum/human-remains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumvrolik.nl">[Museum Vrolik]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumvrolik.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Vrolik Human remains</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum VrolikHuman remains - Museum VrolikMarch 16, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2026</span></span></span>

Particular attention has focused on remains gathered during the colonial period. Some of the museum's nineteenth- and early twentieth-century anatomists conducted physical anthropology intended to classify human populations into supposedly distinct "races". Although these researchers reflected ideas common in their era, the museum explicitly recognises that such work also helped reinforce racist and colonial ideologies whose effects continue today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumvrolik.nl/en/about-the-museum/human-remains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumvrolik.nl">[Museum Vrolik]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumvrolik.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Vrolik Human remains</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum VrolikHuman remains - Museum VrolikMarch 16, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2026</span></span></span>

This acknowledgement has practical consequences. Museum Vrolik has undertaken dedicated provenance research into colonial-era human remains while working with descendant communities to determine appropriate futures for those collections. Where justified, restitution and reburial are recognised as legitimate outcomes rather than losses to science.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumvrolik.nl/en/about-the-museum/human-remains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumvrolik.nl">[Museum Vrolik]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumvrolik.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Vrolik Human remains</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum VrolikHuman remains - Museum VrolikMarch 16, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2026</span></span></span>

Its recent exhibition *Imagine: The Future of Human Remains from Colonial Contexts* makes this ethical shift visible. Instead of celebrating historical collecting, the exhibition deliberately includes empty display supports representing remains that have been or may eventually be returned. The absence itself becomes part of the interpretation, reminding visitors that museums can acknowledge historical injustice without erasing its history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumvrolik.nl/en/visit-the-museum/exhibition-imagine/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumvrolik.nl">[Museum Vrolik]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumvrolik.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Vrolik Exhibition: Imagine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum VrolikExhibition: Imagine - Museum Vrolik...</span></span></span>

## A Wider Dutch Approach to Human Remains

## Why These Collections Still Matter

The Dutch anatomy collections remain internationally important because they preserve material that transformed medical education and the understanding of disease and human development. Removing every human remain from public view would also erase evidence of how medicine developed and how scientific institutions sometimes contributed to unequal treatment.

Instead, Dutch museums increasingly invite visitors to examine both achievements and failures together. A preserved specimen can simultaneously demonstrate anatomical knowledge, reveal nineteenth-century collecting practices, expose the influence of racial science and prompt discussion about consent and restitution.

Within the Netherlands' wider landscape of unusual museums and curiosity collections, anatomy museums therefore stand apart. Their displays are striking not simply because they contain preserved human bodies, but because they ask visitors to consider how scientific knowledge was created, who paid the human cost and how museums can present difficult collections with honesty, dignity and accountability.
