## Introduction

The important distinction is credibility. Some Danish institutions present documented history and archaeological evidence; others deliberately imitate cabinets of curiosity and ask visitors to decide what they believe. Folklore museums preserve stories without claiming that their supernatural creatures literally existed. Commercial attractions may favour spectacle, while university and state museums increasingly discuss provenance, ethics and the responsibilities involved in displaying bodies or culturally sensitive objects. Taken together, these places show a national curiosity culture that is playful but unusually interested in how knowledge is constructed.

## Where Denmark’s strangest collections are found

Denmark does not have one dominant “museum of the weird”. Its unusual collections are distributed across several types of institution:

* **Historically grounded museums**, including the HEX! Museum of Witch Hunt in Ribe, Køge Museum’s witch-trial exhibition and the Prison Museum in Horsens.
* **Scientific and archaeological collections**, especially Medical Museion in Copenhagen and the Grauballe Man display at Moesgaard Museum.
* **Folklore and fantasy attractions**, such as Museum [Obscurum]({{ 'obscurum/' | relative_url }}), Stevns Folklore Museum and the Troll Museum at Gjøl.
* **Atmospheric heritage sites**, including the Tirpitz bunker complex, where architecture and landscape are as important as the displayed objects.
* **Commercial curiosity attractions**, including record-breaking and “believe it or not” displays aimed more directly at entertainment.

This variety matters because the word *strange* can conceal very different purposes. A preserved foetus used in medical teaching, an invented werewolf specimen and a local tale about a supernatural king may all appear uncanny, but they carry different evidential and ethical weight. Denmark’s better institutions make those differences part of the visit rather than treating every odd object as an unexplained marvel.

## Witchcraft without celebrating the myth

The HEX! Museum of Witch Hunt in Ribe is Denmark’s clearest museum devoted to historical witch persecution. The value of these museums lies in their refusal to turn victims into decorative “witches”. Their darker subject is not magic itself but institutionalised belief: courts accepted stories about curses and devils as actionable evidence. For visitors interested in occult history, this distinction is essential. Denmark’s strongest witch museums preserve the history of belief while rejecting the judicial assumptions that made persecution possible.

## Museum Obscurum and the pleasure of doubtful objects

Museum Obscurum in Nykøbing Falster offers almost the opposite experience. It presents the supposedly secret collection of Cornelius S. C. Rödder, an eccentric collector whose rooms contain strange creatures, mysterious documents, magical objects and cryptozoological specimens. Werewolves and other impossible beings appear within a carefully staged historical environment that resembles a private cabinet discovered intact behind a forgotten door.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumlollandfalster.dk/en/museum-obscurum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumlollandfalster.dk">[Museum Lolland-falster]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumlollandfalster.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum Lolland-falsterMuseum Obscurum in Nykøbing Falster – a secret collectionAt MUSEUM OBSCURUM in Nykøbing Falster you can experience...</span></span></span>

The collection is not best understood as zoological evidence. It is an immersive fiction built around the appearance of evidence. Labels, cases and collector mythology reproduce the authority of an old museum, while the institution repeatedly asks visitors what is real, what is superstition and who has the power to decide. Its official interpretation encourages scepticism even while inviting guests to enjoy the fantasy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumlollandfalster.dk/en/museum-obscurum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumlollandfalster.dk">[Museum Lolland-falster]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumlollandfalster.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum Lolland-falsterMuseum Obscurum in Nykøbing Falster – a secret collectionAt MUSEUM OBSCURUM in Nykøbing Falster you can experience...</span></span></span>

That makes Museum Obscurum one of Denmark’s most useful examples of a modern curiosity cabinet. A traditional cabinet mixed natural specimens, artworks, antiquities, alleged marvels and misunderstood objects before modern museums divided knowledge into specialist categories. Museum Obscurum recreates that confusion deliberately. Its cryptids are memorable, but the deeper subject is the visual language through which dubious claims acquire credibility.

Visitors who want authenticated monsters will not find them. Those interested in hoaxes, fabricated provenance, collector psychology and the theatrical power of museums will find something more rewarding. The uncertainty is the exhibit.

## Bodies preserved, collected and interpreted

At Moesgaard Museum near Aarhus, Denmark’s most arresting human display is Grauballe Man. Found in a Jutland bog in 1952, he lived around 390 BC and survived in extraordinary condition because of the preserving environment of the peat. Skin, hair, nails and facial features remain visible, making the encounter feel much more immediate than viewing a skeleton or reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://moesgaardmuseum.dk/en/exhibitions/current-exhibitions/iron-age-exhibition/the-grauballe-man/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: moesgaardmuseum.dk">[moesgaardmuseum.dk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">moesgaardmuseum.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on moesgaardmuseum.dk.</span></span></span>

Moesgaard places the body in an Iron Age exhibition about sacred wetlands, warfare and sacrifice. Research indicates that Grauballe Man died violently, but the precise reason for his killing cannot be known with certainty. The museum presents human sacrifice as a leading interpretation while retaining an element of mystery around his identity and death. Nearby material includes objects deposited in wetlands and evidence of ritualised violence, allowing the body to be understood within a broader archaeological pattern rather than as an isolated “mummy”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://moesgaardmuseum.dk/en/exhibitions/current-exhibitions/iron-age-exhibition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: moesgaardmuseum.dk">[moesgaardmuseum.dk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">moesgaardmuseum.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Iron Age exhibition Open today 10:00</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Iron Age exhibition Open today 10:00</span></span></span>

The display is unusually restrained. Grauballe Man rests alone in a dark, quiet space intended to encourage contemplation rather than shock. That curatorial decision matters: a genuine human body is not treated as a carnival oddity, even though its preservation is extraordinary. Visitors are invited to look closely, but also to consider the distance between scientific evidence and imaginative reconstruction.

Medical Museion in Copenhagen addresses the display of human material more directly. Based in the former Royal Surgical Academy and operated by the University of Copenhagen, it combines medical history, current research and public exhibitions on bodies, disease and health. Its collections document more than two centuries of medical science, while its university status gives it a more analytical role than a conventional collection of antique instruments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museion.ku.dk/en/about-us/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museion.ku.dk">[Medicinsk Museion]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museion.ku.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Medicinsk Museion About us</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medicinsk Museion About us</span></span></span>

The exhibition *The Body Collected* includes foetuses, skeletons, organs, tissue samples, cells and DNA. It follows the changing scale of medical investigation, from whole bodies to organs, microscopic tissue and modern biobanks. The displays are unquestionably unsettling, but their purpose is to examine how human remains became research material and how changing technology altered what medicine chose to preserve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museion.ku.dk/en/exhibitions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museion.ku.dk">[Medicinsk Museion]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museion.ku.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Medicinsk Museion Exhibitions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medicinsk Museion Exhibitions</span></span></span>

Medical Museion also foregrounds ethical questions. Its guided interpretation discusses nineteenth-century collecting in maternity wards, forensic examination and modern biological databases. Other parts of the institution cover subjects ranging from dentistry and epidemics to the relationship between the brain and digestive system. The result is not a chamber of medical horrors, although some objects may be difficult to view. It is a research-led museum asking how scientific knowledge depends on collecting parts of people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museion.ku.dk/en/book-a-guided-group-tour/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museion.ku.dk">[Medicinsk Museion]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museion.ku.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ku.dk.</span></span></span>

## Folklore as local heritage rather than proof

Stevns Folklore Museum demonstrates how supernatural traditions can be preserved without being presented as literal history. One of Denmark’s smaller museums, it concentrates on stories associated with the Stevns peninsula, including tales of the Cliff King and other beings connected with particular features of the landscape. Photographs, storytelling and interactive elements link legendary creatures to the places where local people believed they appeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folkloremuseum.dk/en/exhibitions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folkloremuseum.dk">[Stevns Folklore Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folkloremuseum.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stevns Folklore Museum Exhibition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stevns Folklore Museum Exhibition</span></span></span>

This is an important form of collection even when there are few old artefacts to display. Folklore is carried through stories, customs, beliefs and repeated associations between landscape and memory. The museum’s stated purpose is to preserve this local tradition, including superstition and inherited wisdom, rather than to prove that the beings in its stories were physically real.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folkloremuseum.dk/en/about-us/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folkloremuseum.dk">[Stevns Folklore Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folkloremuseum.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stevns Folklore Museum About Stevns Folklore Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stevns Folklore Museum About Stevns Folklore Museum</span></span></span>

At Gjøl, the Troll Museum preserves a very different kind of Danish troll culture. It occupies the former factory connected with Thomas Dam, creator of the wild-haired Good Luck Troll dolls that became an international toy phenomenon. The museum is therefore part design archive, part industrial history and part record of how an old supernatural figure was transformed into a cheerful commercial character.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitdenmark.com/denmark/plan-your-trip/troll-museum-troldemuseet-gdk1084220" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitdenmark.com">[VisitDenmark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitdenmark.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitdenmark.com.</span></span></span>

The distinction between folkloric trolls and manufactured troll dolls is revealing. One belongs to unstable regional storytelling; the other has a named designer, production history and global market. Denmark’s troll collections show how monsters can be domesticated, branded and sold without losing their connection to older ideas about hidden beings.

A further transformation is visible in Thomas Dambo’s large sculptures made from discarded materials. His trolls function as public art, environmental messages and outdoor treasure hunts rather than museum specimens. In 2026, ARKEN Museum of Contemporary Art brought this work indoors for an exhibition in which troll characters appeared to occupy and alter the museum using waste. The project shows how readily Danish troll imagery moves between folklore, toys, tourism and contemporary art.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/5cfcea0d588c5986aa238c5d607924af" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

## Prisons and bunkers as collections of atmosphere

The Prison Museum in Horsens occupies the former Horsens State Penitentiary, which held prisoners from 1853 until 2006. Its unusual power comes from the survival of the institution itself: cells, corridors, locks and enclosed yards remain part of the evidence. Rather than placing prison objects in a neutral gallery, the museum allows visitors to move through the architecture that controlled inmates’ daily lives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumhorsens.dk/english" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumhorsens.dk">[Museum Horsens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumhorsens.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumhorsens.dk.</span></span></span>

Its best-known feature is the reconstructed route of Carl August Lorentzen’s 1949 escape. Lorentzen dug an approximately 18-metre tunnel beneath the prison yard, and the museum now allows visitors to experience the story in the place where it happened. Displays also address punishment, executions, isolation and repeated attempts to escape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faengslet.dk/en/experiences/the-prison-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faengslet.dk">[Fængslet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faengslet.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faengslet.dk.</span></span></span>

The museum is visitor-facing and theatrical, but its strangeness is not invented. It comes from encountering confinement as a physical system. The most memorable objects are not necessarily weapons or restraints; they are doors, narrow spaces and the improvised technologies through which prisoners attempted to recover some control.

Tirpitz, near Blåvand on Denmark’s west coast, creates a similarly atmospheric encounter with military heritage. The museum is built beside and beneath an unfinished German coastal bunker dating from the Second World War. Modern galleries have been cut into the dunes, leaving much of the protected landscape visually undisturbed while the original concrete structure remains as an oppressive historical object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tirpitz.dk/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tirpitz.dk">[Tirpitz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tirpitz.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tirpitz.dk.</span></span></span>

Tirpitz is broader than a bunker museum. Its displays cover the Atlantic Wall, civilian lives during wartime, west-coast history and amber, including insects preserved in material formed millions of years ago. This combination produces an unusual sequence: visitors move from modern fortification and occupation history to deep geological time and tiny organisms trapped in fossilised resin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tirpitz.dk/en/varde-museums/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tirpitz.dk">[Tirpitz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tirpitz.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Varde Museums</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Varde Museums</span></span></span>

The site shows how Denmark often turns unsettling ruins into layered museums rather than leaving them as single-theme memorials. The bunker is authentic, but the visitor experience is highly designed. Architecture guides emotion while the exhibitions prevent the structure from becoming an empty monument to military power.

## From royal curiosity cabinet to national collections

Denmark’s modern museums also descend from a much older culture of collecting marvels. The Danish royal cabinet of curiosities assembled antiquities, natural objects, artworks and ethnographic material before these fields were divided among specialist institutions. Scholarship maintained by the National Museum traces the movement of material from this royal collection into Copenhagen’s later museums.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://natmus.dk/fileadmin/user_upload/Editor/natmus/etnografisksamling/dokumenter/BenteGundestrup_Selected_Publ.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: natmus.dk">[Nationalmuseet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">natmus.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nationalmuseet Bente Gundestrup</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nationalmuseet Bente Gundestrup</span></span></span>

Some objects now regarded as national archaeological treasures passed through this cabinet system. The National Museum’s account of Danish treasure finds, for example, notes that an important discovery was transferred into the King’s Cabinet of Curiosities. Such collections mixed systematic preservation with royal prestige and the desire to possess rare things.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nationalmuseet.dk/en/exhibitions/the-hunt-for-the-danish-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalmuseet.dk">[National Museum of Denmark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalmuseet.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of Denmark The Hunt for Danish History: Treasures from the Past</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of Denmark The Hunt for Danish History: Treasures from the Past</span></span></span>

The legacy is not entirely comfortable. Ethnographic collections accumulated objects from non-European and Arctic cultures under unequal colonial and scholarly conditions. The National Museum continues to hold a large ethnographic collection, while recent debates and returns have underlined that sacred or culturally important material cannot be treated merely as exotic curiosities. In 2024, Denmark returned a sacred Indigenous cloak to Brazil; other related pieces remained in European collections, including Denmark’s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.natmus.dk/organisation/management-secretariat-and-research-administration/modern-history-and-world-cultures/ethnographic-collection/?force=desktop" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.natmus.dk">[National Museum of Denmark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.natmus.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on natmus.dk.</span></span></span>

This history complicates the romantic image of the cabinet of wonders. Curiosity generated knowledge, but it also separated objects from communities and converted cultural authority into European ownership. Denmark’s strange-collection landscape is most interesting when institutions acknowledge that tension rather than presenting every inherited object as an uncomplicated treasure.

## What visitors should believe

Denmark’s unusual museums reward different levels of trust.

**Strongly evidence-led:** HEX!, Køge Museum, Moesgaard, Medical Museion, the Prison Museum and Tirpitz work from historical, archaeological, scientific or architectural evidence. Interpretation may be dramatic, but the core objects and events are documented.

**Folklore-led:** Stevns Folklore Museum preserves beliefs, legends and associations with place. The stories are culturally real even when their creatures are not treated as zoological facts.

**Fiction-led:** Museum Obscurum intentionally blurs authentic-looking display with invented collecting history. Its value depends on visitors recognising that ambiguity and examining their own willingness to believe museum labels.

**Commercial or popular-culture-led:** Troll dolls, giant troll trails and record-based attractions transform strangeness into entertainment, design history or tourism. They can still be culturally revealing, but spectacle is central to their appeal.

The country’s most memorable oddities are therefore not united by a single aesthetic. Some are grim, some playful and some ethically uncomfortable. What connects them is a recurring concern with the authority of display: why a labelled object seems believable, why a preserved body feels different from a replica, how folklore becomes heritage, and how fear or institutional power can turn an accusation into accepted fact.

## Why Denmark matters to strange-museum culture


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Denmark offers an unusually coherent route through the history of curiosity. The royal cabinet represents an era when marvels were collected before clear disciplinary boundaries existed. Witch museums show the deadly consequences of treating supernatural accusations as evidence. Medical and archaeological displays reveal how real human bodies become sources of knowledge. Folklore museums preserve beliefs without requiring literal acceptance, while Museum Obscurum turns fabricated evidence into a lesson in scepticism.

</div>

Its prisons and bunkers add another dimension: buildings themselves become large-scale collection objects, preserving systems of confinement and conflict. Meanwhile, trolls move freely between legend, factory production, public sculpture and art museum, demonstrating how old monsters can be repeatedly commercialised and reinterpreted.

The result is not a country packed with lurid freak shows. Denmark’s distinctive contribution is more thoughtful and, in many cases, stranger: museums that ask not only what an object is, but why institutions collected it, why audiences trust it and what responsibilities come with putting it on display.

    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
