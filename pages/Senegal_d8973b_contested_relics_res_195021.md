## Introduction

The most unusual objects at Dakar's Museum of Black Civilisations are not necessarily the rarest or the oldest. They are the ones whose ownership remains disputed. In [Senegal]({{ 'where-senegal-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }}), debates over restitution are about far more than recovering valuable artefacts from Europe. They ask who has the authority to interpret African history, whether colonial collecting can ever be separated from colonial power, and how museums should present objects whose legal status, cultural meaning or provenance remains contested. The Museum of Black Civilisations has deliberately positioned itself as a place where these debates are visible rather than hidden, making restitution part of the visitor experience instead of treating it as a purely legal issue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africarenewal.un.org/en/magazine/reclaiming-stolen-artefacts-africas-landmark-museum-heart-global-discussion-about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africarenewal.un.org">[Africa Renewal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africarenewal.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Africa RenewalAfrica&#x27;s landmark museum at the heart of global discussion...21 Aug 2025 — Only one major object has officially entered it...</span></span></span>

## Sacred objects in African-led displays

Many historic African objects held in European museums were originally created for religious practice, royal authority or community ceremonies rather than permanent exhibition. Once removed from their original settings, they often became classified as ethnographic specimens, military trophies or works of art, with little attention paid to the communities that had used or protected them.

The Museum of Black Civilisations approaches these objects differently. Instead of presenting them simply as aesthetic masterpieces, its exhibitions place them within wider histories of African civilisation, spirituality and political authority. This does not automatically resolve questions of ownership, but it changes the interpretive framework. Visitors encounter sacred and historic material as part of living African history rather than as detached examples of "primitive art", a category widely criticised for reflecting colonial-era assumptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/decolonizing-heritage/museum-of-black-civilizations/4301081F1C72233CEB74610FF30B46BA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Museum of Black Civilizations (Chapter 7)Funded by the Republic of China, the Museum of Black...</span></span></span>

This approach also challenges an old argument often made by European institutions: that African countries lacked the facilities needed to conserve returned artefacts. Since opening in 2018, the museum has repeatedly presented itself as evidence that modern conservation, climate control and professional collections management are available within Senegal. The institution therefore functions not only as a museum but also as a practical response to objections frequently raised during restitution debates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africasacountry.com/2019/03/the-museum-of-black-civilizations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africasacountry.com">[Africa Is a Country]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africasacountry.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Africa Is a CountryThe Museum of Black CivilizationsFrance has lent the museum the sword of nineteenth century political and religious le...</span></span></span>

## How provenance changes an exhibit

A visitor may see an object in two museums and encounter two entirely different stories.

Provenance—the documented history of an artefact's ownership and movement—has become one of the most important parts of museum interpretation. At the Museum of Black Civilisations, provenance is not merely a catalogue entry but a central element of the display. Questions such as who removed an object, under what circumstances, whether consent existed and whether colonial military campaigns influenced acquisition can become as significant as the physical object itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/decolonizing-heritage/museum-of-black-civilizations/4301081F1C72233CEB74610FF30B46BA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Museum of Black Civilizations (Chapter 7)Funded by the Republic of China, the Museum of Black...</span></span></span>

This means that uncertainty is sometimes acknowledged rather than concealed. Some artefacts have incomplete documentation, conflicting ownership claims or changing historical interpretations. Rather than presenting certainty where none exists, modern restitution debates increasingly encourage museums to explain these ambiguities openly.

For visitors interested in unusual collections, this creates a different type of curiosity. Instead of asking, "What is this object?", they are encouraged to ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Who originally owned it?
* How did it reach a European museum?
* Was it purchased, gifted, confiscated or taken during military conquest?
* Who has the strongest claim to it today?
* Does legal ownership match moral ownership?

</div>

Those questions often become more memorable than the object itself because they expose the political history behind museum collections.

## The disputed sword of Oumar Tall

No object illustrates these tensions better than the sword associated with El Hadj Oumar Tall, the nineteenth-century Muslim scholar, political leader and military commander remembered across much of Senegal as an important figure of resistance to French expansion.

French forces seized the sword after defeating Tall's son Ahmadou in 1893, and it eventually entered the collection of the Musée de l'Armée in Paris. For more than a century it remained in France before becoming one of the first high-profile African heritage objects transferred to Dakar during France's recent restitution initiative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.artnet.com/art-world-archives/france-restitutes-senegal-saber-1707042" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.artnet.com">[artnet.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.artnet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">It had been part of the collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artnet NewsFrance Returns to Senegal a 19th-Century Saber That It...November 18, 2019 — 18 Nov 2019 — The sword was seized from Tall&#x27;s s...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2019</span></span></span>

The object's display, however, immediately revealed why restitution is rarely straightforward.

Although widely described as a "return", the transfer was initially structured as a renewable five-year loan because French law historically treated items in national museum collections as inalienable state property. In practical terms, visitors could see the sword in Dakar, but its legal ownership remained unresolved. This distinction transformed the artefact itself into evidence of an ongoing political negotiation rather than the conclusion of one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theartnewspaper.com/news/france-returns-a-historic-sword-to-senegal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theartnewspaper.com">[theartnewspaper.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theartnewspaper.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">france returns a historic sword to senegal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Art NewspaperFrance returns a historic sword to Senegal18 Nov 2019 — The sword, whose leather handle is trimmed with a base shaped like a...</span></span></span>

The sword is also historically complex. While celebrated in Senegal as a powerful national symbol, Oumar Tall's legacy extends across modern Senegal, Mali and Guinea, and historians interpret his campaigns differently depending on regional perspectives. As a result, the sword represents overlapping histories of anti-colonial resistance, empire-building, religion and memory rather than a single uncontested national narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.artnet.com/art-world-archives/france-restitutes-senegal-saber-1707042" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.artnet.com">[Artnet News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.artnet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">It had been part of the collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artnet NewsFrance Returns to Senegal a 19th-Century Saber That It...November 18, 2019 — 18 Nov 2019 — The sword was seized from Tall&#x27;s s...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2019</span></span></span>

## Why one object carries so much weight

The prominence given to Oumar Tall's sword reflects more than its military history.

It has become a test case for wider restitution policy. If one symbolic object can be transferred, observers naturally ask what happens to thousands of other African artefacts acquired during the colonial period and now held in European museums. France alone holds tens of thousands of objects originating from sub-Saharan Africa, making any individual restitution part of a much larger discussion about legal reform, diplomatic cooperation and historical responsibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.artnet.com/art-world-archives/france-restitutes-senegal-saber-1707042" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.artnet.com">[artnet.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.artnet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">It had been part of the collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artnet NewsFrance Returns to Senegal a 19th-Century Saber That It...November 18, 2019 — 18 Nov 2019 — The sword was seized from Tall&#x27;s s...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2019</span></span></span>

Museum leaders in Dakar have therefore used the sword not simply as a prized exhibit but as a conversation starter. Its display demonstrates that restitution is not only about recovering physical artefacts.
