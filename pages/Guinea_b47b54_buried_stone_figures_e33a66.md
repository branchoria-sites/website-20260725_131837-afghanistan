## Introduction

Across the forested borderlands of [Guinea]({{ 'what-makes-guineas-collections-so/' | relative_url }}), Sierra Leone and Liberia, farmers and miners have occasionally uncovered small carved stone figures while clearing fields, digging wells or working the soil. These objects are among West Africa's most enduring archaeological mysteries. Although they are often grouped with the better-known *nomoli* figures of Sierra Leone, Guinea has its own related tradition of stone carvings, especially those associated with the Kissi people. Their original makers, age and purpose remain uncertain, yet many of the figures acquired new lives after their discovery, becoming ancestral objects, ritual protectors or symbols believed to promote fertile harvests. Rather than belonging to a single lost civilisation, they represent a family of related stone sculptures whose archaeological context was largely lost because most were found accidentally rather than through controlled excavation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/stone-sculptures-of-the-upper-guinea-coast/CB3CECBB91AB9D1EE26091BD3036DA3E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Stone Sculptures of the Upper Guinea Coast* &#124; History in Africa &#124; Cambridge CoreMay 13, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2014</span></span></span>

## Who carved Guinea's buried stone figures?

The greatest mystery is not that the figures exist, but that they usually appear without the archaeological evidence that would explain them. Most were recovered from cultivated land, stream beds or mining areas long after they had become separated from any original settlement, burial or shrine.

In Guinea, many examples come from the Kissi region near the border with Sierra Leone. Similar discoveries have also been reported among neighbouring Mende communities, suggesting that the tradition crossed modern national boundaries. The carvings are usually made from soft steatite (soapstone), which is relatively easy to shape but survives well underground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/stone-sculptures-of-the-upper-guinea-coast/CB3CECBB91AB9D1EE26091BD3036DA3E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Stone Sculptures of the Upper Guinea Coast* &#124; History in Africa &#124; Cambridge CoreMay 13, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2014</span></span></span>

Scholars have debated their origins for more than a century. One influential interpretation argues that the figures were created by peoples known in early European accounts as the Sapes, who occupied parts of the Upper Guinea Coast before later migrations. This remains a hypothesis rather than an established fact because there are few securely excavated examples that can be dated directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/nomoli/FA25A3EAE9D80C1C3A7DD93B043A4DAC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentNomoli &#124; The Journal of African History &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

The absence of reliable archaeological contexts is the central obstacle. Because many figures were discovered individually by chance, archaeologists cannot easily reconstruct the settlements, rituals or social systems that originally surrounded them.

## Where the figures are found and what they look like

Unlike statues displayed in museums after planned excavations, these carvings often entered public awareness through ordinary agricultural work. Farmers turning over fields, clearing new farmland or digging wells occasionally encountered them buried beneath the soil.

Common characteristics include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* Human or human-like forms carved from soapstone.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1963" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* Rounded heads with pronounced facial features.
* Cylindrical bodies, particularly among figures associated with the Kissi region.
* Carefully carved hairstyles, jewellery or collars on some examples.
* Sizes ranging from small hand-held figures to larger sculptures.

</div>

Museum collections preserve examples showing crossed hands, protruding eyes, carefully carved teeth and traces of white kaolin pigment, indicating that at least some figures continued to receive ritual treatment long after they were unearthed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africa.si.edu/collection/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africa.si.edu">[si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africa.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of African Art Male figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of African Art Male figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span></span></span>

Researchers generally distinguish several related sculptural traditions across the Upper Guinea Coast. The famous *nomoli* figures dominate southeastern Sierra Leone, while the Kissi area of Guinea is associated with cylindrical soapstone figures often called *pomdo* or *pomtan*. A third related group consists mainly of carved stone heads rather than complete figures. These categories overlap geographically and stylistically rather than forming completely separate traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/stone-sculptures-of-the-upper-guinea-coast/CB3CECBB91AB9D1EE26091BD3036DA3E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Stone Sculptures of the Upper Guinea Coast* &#124; History in Africa &#124; Cambridge CoreMay 13, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2014</span></span></span>

## Why their original purpose remains uncertain

Despite decades of study, there is still no consensus about why these sculptures were first made.

Several factors limit certainty:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Few excavated examples.** Most pieces lack their original archaeological setting.
* **Little direct dating.** Proposed dates often rely on stylistic comparison rather than laboratory dating.
* **Changing meanings over time.** Communities that found the figures may not have inherited the beliefs of the people who carved them.
* **Regional diversity.** Similar-looking sculptures may have served different purposes across different societies.

</div>

Researchers have proposed that some figures were ancestral images, ritual guardians or commemorative sculptures. Others may have been associated with religious shrines or political authority. None of these interpretations can yet be demonstrated conclusively for the entire group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/stone-sculptures-of-the-upper-guinea-coast/CB3CECBB91AB9D1EE26091BD3036DA3E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Stone Sculptures of the Upper Guinea Coast* &#124; History in Africa &#124; Cambridge CoreMay 13, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2014</span></span></span>

This uncertainty makes the carvings unusual within Guinea's archaeological heritage. Their mystery comes not from sensational claims about vanished civilisations but from the genuine lack of evidence needed to connect them confidently with specific historical communities.

## How later communities gave the discoveries new meanings

Although their original function remains unknown, the figures did not remain meaningless after being rediscovered.

Among Kissi, Mende and neighbouring communities, unearthed stone figures were frequently understood as belonging to earlier inhabitants of the landscape or to powerful ancestral spirits. Rather than treating them simply as curiosities, people incorporated them into living ritual traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africa.si.edu/collection/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africa.si.edu">[National Museum of African Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africa.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of African Art Male figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of African Art Male figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span></span></span>

Historical and museum records describe several later uses:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Placing figures on ancestral shrines.
* Making offerings and sacrifices to them.
* Using them in agricultural rituals intended to encourage successful harvests.
* Employing them in oath-taking or divination.
* Treating them as protective spiritual objects for particular communities or initiates.

</div>

These later practices should not automatically be assumed to reflect the sculptures' original purpose. Instead, they illustrate how communities can reinterpret ancient objects whose creators have long disappeared. An artefact recovered from a field became part of a new religious and social landscape, acquiring fresh meanings without preserving a complete memory of its first one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/nomoli/FA25A3EAE9D80C1C3A7DD93B043A4DAC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentNomoli &#124; The Journal of African History &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

## Why the figures matter in Guinea's curiosity landscape

For visitors interested in Guinea's unusual heritage, these stone figures occupy a distinctive place between archaeology, folklore and museum collecting.

They are unusual because they combine three different histories:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* **An archaeological mystery**, since their creators remain unidentified.
* **A living cultural story**, because communities continued to use many of the discoveries in ritual life.
* **A museum story**, as examples now appear in collections across Africa, Europe and North America, often far from the landscapes where they were found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1970" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

</div>

Unlike spectacular monuments or famous ruins, Guinea's buried stone figures are remarkable precisely because they emerged unexpectedly from ordinary farmland. Each discovery connected present-day communities with an unknown past while creating new traditions of interpretation. Their enduring fascination lies not in supernatural claims but in the rare combination of genuine archaeological uncertainty and well-documented cultural reuse, making them one of the most intriguing categories of unusual objects associated with Guinea and the wider Upper Guinea Coast.
