## Introduction

Congolese power figures and masks are among the most recognisable objects in African museum collections, yet they are also among the most frequently misunderstood. Visitors often encounter statues studded with nails or carved masks displayed behind glass and assume they represent "witchcraft", fear or mysterious magic. In reality, these objects originally belonged to complex systems of law, healing, protection, initiation and community life. Their meanings depended not simply on their appearance, but on the people who activated them, the ceremonies in which they appeared and the social responsibilities they embodied. Over the past century, colonial collecting practices and the widespread use of labels such as "fetish" detached many of these objects from their original context, encouraging interpretations that continue to shape popular imagination today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Institution Male figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Institution Male figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span></span></span>

## What power figures were really used for

One of the best-known ritual objects from the Kongo cultural world is the power figure often called an *nkisi* (plural *minkisi*). Museums frequently describe these as "power figures", but specialists increasingly note that even this translation captures only part of their role. Earlier European collectors routinely called them "fetishes", a term now recognised as misleading because it reduced sophisticated religious and legal practices to superstition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Institution Male figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Institution Male figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span></span></span>

Rather than functioning as idols that people worshipped, many *minkisi* were understood as containers through which spiritual forces could be directed by trained ritual specialists. A specialist prepared medicines and symbolic materials inside cavities, often concealed behind mirrors or resin-filled compartments. These substances could be used to:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* protect individuals or communities;
* confirm treaties and sworn agreements;
* identify theft or wrongdoing;
* support healing;
* settle disputes;
* guard important places or boundaries.

</div>

The dramatic appearance of many surviving figures reflects these practical roles. Metal blades or nails were often added during the life of an object to record oaths, petitions or interventions rather than to signify violence alone. Each insertion could represent a new legal case, promise or appeal for justice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Institution Male figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Institution Male figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because many museum visitors still interpret the nails as evidence of "black magic". In their original setting, however, they often symbolised accountability, enforcement and communal order as much as supernatural power.

## How masks worked in performance

Congolese masks were rarely intended to be viewed as isolated artworks. The carved face was only one element within a much larger performance involving costume, music, dance, specialist knowledge and carefully controlled participation.

Among Yaka communities in what is now the Democratic Republic of the [Congo]({{ 'where-congos-strangest-collections-keep/' | relative_url }}), for example, certain masks formed part of male initiation ceremonies. Smithsonian research describes these masks as appearing in choreographed performances representing ancestors and culture heroes, promoting growth, healing and the transition of boys into recognised adulthood. Their designs also referred symbolically to fertility, seasonal cycles and relationships between male and female principles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africa.si.edu/collection/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africa.si.edu">[National Museum of African Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africa.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of African Art Cap mask &#124; National Museum of African Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of African Art Cap mask &#124; National Museum of African Art</span></span></span>

Seen inside a museum case, however, most of that experience disappears. Visitors observe:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a carved wooden face instead of a complete costume;
* stillness instead of movement;
* silence instead of music;
* an anonymous object instead of trained performers;
* sculpture instead of a social event.

</div>

The result is a profound change in meaning. What originally functioned as an event becomes displayed as an object, encouraging viewers to judge it primarily on appearance rather than purpose.

This transformation explains why many Congolese masks appear mysterious or unsettling in museums. Their visual forms were never intended to communicate independently of the performances that gave them life.

## How colonial labels distorted meaning

Few words have influenced public understanding more than "fetish". European traders, missionaries and colonial officials used the term broadly for African religious objects they neither fully understood nor respected. It implied irrational devotion to physical things and blurred together healing, religion, law, ancestor traditions and spiritual practice into a single category of supposed superstition.

Modern museums increasingly acknowledge that this vocabulary is historically loaded. The Smithsonian notes that there is no true English equivalent for *nkisi* and explicitly explains that terms such as "fetish" or even "power figure" fail to capture its full meaning. A *nkisi* could be a carved figure, but it could equally be a basket, pot or other container prepared and activated by a ritual specialist. The sculpture itself was only one possible physical form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Institution Male figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Institution Male figure &#124; Smithsonian Institution</span></span></span>

Colonial collecting also separated objects from the specialists who understood them. Once removed from shrines, villages and ceremonial settings, many entered museums with only fragmentary documentation. Catalogues often recorded who purchased an object while preserving little information about:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* who commissioned it;
* who used it;
* which community maintained it;
* how long it remained active;
* the circumstances under which it left its original setting.

</div>

These gaps continue to affect interpretation today, even in major museum collections.

## Why museum displays can still mislead

Museums now devote far more attention to cultural context than they did a century ago, but displaying ritual objects remains difficult.

Power figures often survive because their wooden bodies endured, while many of the organic materials that originally empowered them have deteriorated or were deliberately removed. Some figures therefore appear incomplete, making them resemble sculptures rather than ritual technologies. The Metropolitan Museum of Art explains that religious specialists originally combined carefully selected materials—including earths, seeds, feathers, minerals and animal substances—to direct spiritual forces towards healing, protection or other specific purposes. Without those components, viewers may mistake the remaining carving for the entire object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/311035" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Power Figure: Male (Nkisi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtPower Figure: Male (Nkisi) - Kongo peoples - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

Similarly, masks displayed on walls invite comparison with paintings or decorative carvings. Their original significance depended on movement, costume and controlled participation rather than static visual appreciation.

The challenge for museums is therefore not simply conservation but interpretation: helping visitors understand that many Congolese ritual objects were designed to do something rather than merely to be looked at.

## Why these misunderstandings matter today

The way Congolese ritual objects have been interpreted continues to influence debates over restitution, collecting and public display.

Many objects became famous in Europe and North America because collectors valued their dramatic appearance while paying less attention to the systems of knowledge that produced them. Contemporary museums increasingly seek to restore that missing context through collaboration with African scholars and communities, revised exhibition labels and more careful language about spiritual traditions.

For visitors interested in unusual museums and curious collections, this shift changes the experience entirely. The fascination no longer lies simply in apparently sinister statues covered with nails or striking carved masks. It lies in recognising how objects once central to justice, healing and initiation were transformed into museum curiosities through colonial collecting, and how today's institutions are working to explain those histories more accurately. Understanding that transformation reveals that the greatest mystery is often not what the objects originally meant, but how easily those meanings were lost once they entered museum collections.
