## Introduction

[Denmark]({{ 'what-makes-denmarks-strangest-museums-so/' | relative_url }})’s witch museums are unusual because they are not celebrations of magic or the occult. Instead, they are carefully researched places that examine how fear, religious belief and legal authority combined to destroy real lives. Visitors expecting displays that argue witches possessed supernatural powers will find something very different: exhibitions built around court records, eyewitness testimony, surviving artefacts and the documented experiences of people accused of impossible crimes.

## Were Denmark's Witches Victims of Belief?

Denmark's museums deliberately shift attention away from the idea of "real witches" and towards the mechanisms of persecution. Their exhibitions ask visitors to consider how ordinary neighbours came to accuse one another, why courts accepted extraordinary testimony, and why so many of the convicted were women living on the margins of accepted social expectations.

Rather than presenting a supernatural mystery, they present a social one. Visitors encounter records showing accusations growing from illness, failed harvests, personal disputes, rumours and religious anxieties. Within this worldview, misfortune required an explanation, and alleged witches became convenient targets.

This approach also reflects current historical scholarship. Researchers generally regard the Danish witch trials as products of early modern religious culture, local politics and judicial practice rather than evidence that authorities uncovered genuine supernatural crimes. The museums therefore treat magic as a historical belief that produced real consequences, not as a phenomenon that has been historically verified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hexmuseum.dk/en/our-story-engelsk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hexmuseum.dk">[HEX! ## Ribe, Maren Spliids and the HEX! Museum

### Maren Spliids as a documented individual

The museum's best-known figure is Maren Spliids, a prosperous tailor's wife executed in 1641 after one of Denmark's most famous witch trials. Her story has endured partly because unusually rich documentary evidence survives.

Rather than presenting her as a mysterious sorceress, the museum reconstructs the legal process that led to her execution. Visitors learn how testimony accumulated, how suspicion hardened into certainty, and how a respected resident could ultimately be condemned under laws that accepted witchcraft as a genuine criminal offence.

Importantly, the museum also reminds visitors that Maren Spliids was not unique. Other women, including the lesser-known Lamme Sidsel, appear in the exhibition to demonstrate that many victims have disappeared almost entirely from public memory despite suffering the same fate. Short films, reconstructed objects, interactive maps and surviving historical material place individual lives within the wider European pattern of persecution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hexmuseum.dk/en/the-permanent-exhibition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hexmuseum.dk">[HEX! ### Explaining fear rather than encouraging it

A striking feature of HEX! is that many displayed objects are shown not because they possessed magical power, but because people believed they did. Protective charms, supposed witchcraft implements and reconstructed ritual objects illustrate contemporary ideas rather than endorsing them.

Interactive displays ask questions such as why communities believed witches could cause illness or steal prosperity, encouraging visitors to understand the mentality of seventeenth-century Europe instead of simply mocking it. Maps comparing persecution across Europe further reinforce that Denmark formed part of a much broader historical phenomenon rather than an isolated episode.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hexmuseum.dk/en/the-permanent-exhibition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hexmuseum.dk">[HEX! ## The Køge Accusations and the *Possessed* Exhibition

While Ribe concentrates on famous individual cases, Køge Museum's exhibition *Possessed* examines Denmark's largest witch-trial episode through the experience of an entire community.

Between 1612 and 1615, sixteen women from Køge were accused of witchcraft. Only one survived. The exhibition reconstructs these events using municipal records, witness statements and the rare written account left by local resident Anna Bartkær, whose family experienced the accusations directly. These surviving documents allow visitors to follow the gradual escalation from suspicion to formal prosecution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museerne.dk/en/pages/koege-museum-besat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museerne.dk">[Museum Sydøstdanmark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museerne.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Sydøstdanmark Køge Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum SydøstdanmarkKøge Museum - Exhibitions - POSSESSED – Museum Sydøstdanmark...</span></span></span>

Unlike fictional depictions of witch hunts, the exhibition concentrates on administrative reality. Court protocols reveal expenses incurred for executions, witness examinations and legal proceedings, illustrating that persecution became embedded within everyday civic government rather than existing outside it.

The museum also displays authentic seventeenth-century objects associated with beliefs about witchcraft alongside carefully explained reconstructions. Their purpose is not to demonstrate that supernatural practices worked, but to reveal what contemporaries genuinely feared and how these fears influenced legal decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museerne.dk/en/pages/koege-museum-besat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museerne.dk">[Museum Sydøstdanmark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museerne.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Sydøstdanmark Køge Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum SydøstdanmarkKøge Museum - Exhibitions - POSSESSED – Museum Sydøstdanmark...</span></span></span>

## How Museums Separate Persecution History from Occult Myth

One reason these museums stand out within Denmark's broader landscape of unusual collections is their careful distinction between folklore and evidence.

They avoid suggesting that historical accusations validate modern claims about magic. Instead, they separate three different ideas:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Historical belief:** People genuinely believed witches existed and could cause harm.
* **Historical fact:** Courts prosecuted and executed real people on that basis.
* **Modern interpretation:** Historians examine these events through surviving records rather than accepting supernatural explanations.

</div>

This distinction is especially important because witchcraft has accumulated centuries of folklore, popular fiction and commercial imagery. The museums acknowledge those traditions while continually returning visitors to documented evidence.

HEX!, for example, explicitly describes one of its goals as replacing myth and hearsay with historical understanding. It also connects early modern witch persecution with broader themes including prejudice, fear, gender expectations and the dangers of identifying social outsiders as threats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hexmuseum.dk/en/our-story-engelsk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hexmuseum.dk">[HEX! ## Why These Museums Matter Within Denmark's Curiosity Culture

Many unusual museums display mysterious objects and invite visitors to wonder whether extraordinary claims might be true. Denmark's witch museums achieve almost the opposite effect.

Their collections are compelling precisely because the tragedy requires no supernatural explanation. The horror lies in ordinary institutions—courts, churches, neighbourhoods and families—that collectively accepted impossible accusations as legal reality.

This makes them distinctive within Denmark's landscape of curious museums. Instead of preserving evidence for magic, they preserve evidence of belief itself: how fear can become official policy, how rumours acquire legal authority, and how historical records restore individuality to people long reduced to the label of "witch."

For visitors interested in strange history, these museums therefore offer something more enduring than tales of spells or curses.
