## Introduction

[Bosnia and Herzegovina]({{ 'bosnia-and-herzegovina/' | relative_url }}) preserves two remarkable underground sites that were built for survival but tell profoundly different stories about who deserved protection in times of crisis. Near Konjic, the vast ARK D-0 bunker—better known as Tito's bunker—was constructed in secrecy during the Cold War to shelter Yugoslavia's political and military leadership from nuclear war. Beneath Sarajevo Airport, the Tunnel of Hope was dug by ordinary citizens during the 1992–1996 siege to keep an encircled city alive. Both are now visitor sites, yet they present strikingly different ideas of resilience: one planned for the survival of an elite state apparatus, the other created through collective effort to sustain an entire population. ## Inside Tito's nuclear command bunker

Hidden beneath Mount Zlatar near Konjic, ARK D-0 was one of socialist Yugoslavia's most closely guarded military secrets. Construction began in 1953 and continued for 26 years, creating a self-contained underground complex designed to withstand a nuclear attack while allowing President Josip Broz Tito and selected military and political officials to continue governing. The facility contains command rooms, communications centres, living quarters, medical facilities, technical systems and infrastructure intended to function independently from the outside world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/project-d-0-ark-1-2-3-4-5-artistic-collaboration-turns-nuclear-bunker-art-gallery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project D-0 ARK 1-2-3-4-5. An artistic collaboration turns Nuclear</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project D-0 ARK 1-2-3-4-5. The bunker's unusual appeal lies in the fact that visitors do not simply view artefacts behind glass. The bunker itself is the exhibit. Walking through its preserved corridors reveals the practical realities of Cold War planning: blast doors, sealed ventilation systems, conference rooms and private accommodation arranged according to military hierarchy rather than comfort. Official management emphasises preserving the site's original equipment and layout, with guided visits helping protect both its authenticity and its status as a protected national monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://titosbunker.ba/en/rules-and-faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: titosbunker.ba">[Titov bunker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">titosbunker.ba</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Titov bunker Rules and FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Titov bunkerRules and FAQs - Titov bunker...</span></span></span>

What distinguishes ARK D-0 from many Cold War bunkers elsewhere in Europe is its second life as a cultural space. Since 2011, the D-0 ARK Underground Biennale has installed contemporary artworks throughout the complex, encouraging visitors to think about secrecy, political power, fear of nuclear catastrophe and the legacy of the former Yugoslavia. Rather than celebrating military engineering alone, the exhibitions deliberately complicate the site's meaning by placing modern artistic responses inside rooms originally designed for command and survival. UNESCO has highlighted the project as an example of transforming a secret military installation into an internationally recognised cultural venue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/project-d-0-ark-1-2-3-4-5-artistic-collaboration-turns-nuclear-bunker-art-gallery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project D-0 ARK 1-2-3-4-5. ## How the Tunnel of Hope sustained Sarajevo

The Tunnel of Hope represents almost the opposite philosophy. During the Siege of Sarajevo, the city was largely cut off from the outside world. In 1993, workers and volunteers completed an approximately 800-metre tunnel beneath the airport runway, connecting the besieged city with territory outside the encirclement. Although narrow and physically demanding to use, it became Sarajevo's vital lifeline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sarajevonavigator.ba/en/text/216" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sarajevonavigator.ba">[sarajevonavigator.ba]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sarajevonavigator.ba</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sarajevonavigator.ba.</span></span></span>

Through the tunnel travelled food, fuel, medicine, humanitarian supplies, military equipment and communications infrastructure. Electricity and telephone cables were eventually installed, while small rail carts helped move heavy loads through the confined passage. Thousands of people also crossed it despite the difficult conditions. Without this hidden route, the city's ability to resist prolonged isolation would have been dramatically reduced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sarajevonavigator.ba/en/text/216" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sarajevonavigator.ba">[sarajevonavigator.ba]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sarajevonavigator.ba</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sarajevonavigator.ba.</span></span></span>

Today's Tunnel of Hope Museum preserves roughly 20 metres of the original passage together with the family house that concealed one entrance. Rather than recreating an idealised underground environment, the museum combines surviving tunnel sections with photographs, personal testimony and wartime objects to explain how daily survival depended upon extraordinary improvisation. The emphasis is less on engineering than on human endurance under siege.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sarajevonavigator.ba/en/text/216" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sarajevonavigator.ba">[sarajevonavigator.ba]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sarajevonavigator.ba</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sarajevonavigator.ba.</span></span></span>

Unlike the controlled secrecy that defined Tito's bunker, the tunnel's story is widely remembered as one of collective labour. It was dug largely by hand under wartime conditions, operated continuously despite constant danger, and served civilians as well as military defenders. Its physical modesty is precisely what gives it emotional power: visitors experience how little space separated life from catastrophe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vogue.com/article/sarajevo-bosnia-travel-guide" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vogue.com">[Vogue]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vogue.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although the city is often recognized for the 1984 Winter Olympics and the destructive civil war in the 1990s, today it thrives with trad...</span></span></span>

## Elite protection versus collective endurance

Seen together, these underground sites offer one of Bosnia and Herzegovina's most revealing historical contrasts.

Tito's bunker (ARK D-0)Tunnel of HopePlanned over decades during the Cold WarBuilt rapidly during active warfareDesigned for political and military leadershipCreated to sustain an entire besieged cityIntended for continuity of government after nuclear attackIntended to preserve everyday life during siegeHighly secret military installationHidden but dependent on civilian participationNow combines military heritage with contemporary artNow focuses on testimony, memory and survival

This contrast extends beyond architecture. Tito's bunker reflects confidence in central planning, technological preparedness and hierarchical control. Everything about its design assumed that preserving government leadership was essential to preserving the state. The Tunnel of Hope instead reflects emergency adaptation. It emerged because existing institutions could no longer guarantee normal life, leaving citizens themselves to construct the infrastructure needed for survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/project-d-0-ark-1-2-3-4-5-artistic-collaboration-turns-nuclear-bunker-art-gallery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project D-0 ARK 1-2-3-4-5. The emotional experience of visiting the two sites is correspondingly different. The bunker often inspires astonishment at the scale of Cold War investment and the resources devoted to protecting a privileged few. ## Why these underground worlds matter today


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Both sites have become important destinations within Bosnia and Herzegovina's distinctive heritage landscape because they preserve environments rather than collections of detached objects. Their original structures remain central to understanding their stories.

</div>

ARK D-0 demonstrates how a place built around secrecy can be reinterpreted through public access and contemporary art without erasing its military origins. The Tunnel of Hope shows how an improvised wartime structure has become a museum centred on memory, resilience and civilian experience. Neither site romanticises conflict.
