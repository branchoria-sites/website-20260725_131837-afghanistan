## Introduction

The best places for an offbeat visitor are the Fish Museum and Germplasm Centre in Mymensingh, Panchagarh Rocks Museum, Bangladesh Bank’s Taka Museum, the Ethnological Museum in Chattogram and the folk collections at Sonargaon and Bangla Academy. ## Where Bangladesh’s strangest collections are found

The country’s unusual collections tend to belong to public authorities, universities or cultural foundations. That affects both their tone and their accessibility. Visitors should expect educational labels, dioramas and specimen cases rather than theatrical haunted-house presentation. Some collections are open on regular schedules, while others operate more like research resources and may require prior contact.

This also explains why Bangladesh’s most interesting “curiosity cabinets” often sit inside institutions whose primary purpose is not tourism. A university preserves disappearing fish species; a central bank arranges coins as a history of sovereignty; an ethnological museum stages models of everyday and ceremonial life; a prison preserves chairs, cells and rooms associated with political leaders. Their strangeness comes from the concentration and recontextualisation of ordinary, sacred or traumatic objects.

## Fish preserved against disappearance

The Professor Mostafa Ali Reza Hossain Fish Museum and Germplasm Centre at Bangladesh Agricultural University in Mymensingh is probably Bangladesh’s closest equivalent to a specialist natural-history oddity museum. Established in 2009, it combines public education with scientific collection-building and fisheries conservation. The university still lists it as an active resource within its Faculty of Fisheries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fs.bau.edu.bd/view/pmarh-fish-museum-germplasm-centre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fs.bau.edu.bd">[fs.bau.edu.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fs.bau.edu.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pmarh fish museum germplasm centre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMARH Fish Museum &amp; Germplasm CentreProf. Mostafa Ali Reza Hossain Fish Museum and Germplasm Center (PMARH FMGC), established in 2009 is...</span></span></span>

Its principal attraction is not a single monster specimen but the cumulative effect of preserved aquatic bodies. Historical descriptions report hundreds of freshwater species represented in jars and displays, alongside fossils, threatened species, educational exhibits and material explaining the relationship between fish, rivers and human livelihoods. Many specimens were collected not merely for display but for identification and biodiversity research, which gives the museum greater scientific credibility than a commercial cabinet of aquatic curiosities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_Museum_%26_Biodiversity_Centre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fish Museum &amp; Biodiversity Centre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fish Museum &amp; Biodiversity Centre</span></span></span>

The unsettling element is also the museum’s main intellectual value. Rows of preserved fish make biodiversity loss physically visible. Bangladesh’s national biodiversity strategy cited research associated with the centre indicating rapid declines among riverine fish and the apparent disappearance of some species from sampled waters. The collection therefore functions partly as a record of animals that visitors may no longer encounter easily in living rivers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cbd.int/doc/world/bd/bd-nbsap-v2-en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cbd.int">[Convention on Biological Diversity]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cbd.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Convention on Biological Diversity CBD Strategy and Action Plan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Convention on Biological Diversity CBD Strategy and Action Plan</span></span></span>

Bangladesh Agricultural University states that visits are available with prior contact on Fridays and Saturdays, divided into morning and afternoon periods. Anyone travelling specifically to see it should arrange access directly rather than rely on old tourism listings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fs.bau.edu.bd/view/pmarh-fish-museum-germplasm-centre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fs.bau.edu.bd">[fs.bau.edu.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fs.bau.edu.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pmarh fish museum germplasm centre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMARH Fish Museum &amp; Germplasm CentreProf. Mostafa Ali Reza Hossain Fish Museum and Germplasm Center (PMARH FMGC), established in 2009 is...</span></span></span>

## A museum made almost entirely of stones

Panchagarh Rocks Museum, on the campus of Panchagarh Government Women’s College, turns one of the least glamorous museum materials—stone—into a distinctive regional collection. It is regularly described in Bangladeshi reporting as the country’s first or only dedicated rocks museum. The collection includes igneous and sedimentary rocks, mineral sands, silica, limestone, clay, gravel, fossilised material and large stone specimens gathered from the surrounding region and elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bssnews.net/tourism/344780" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bssnews.net">[BSS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bssnews.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s, each accompanied by an identification plaque detailing its origin and...</span></span></span>

What makes it more than a classroom geology display is its connection with northern Bangladesh’s landscape. Panchagarh lies near Himalayan river systems that transport an unusually varied mixture of stones, sands and sediments. Museum labels reportedly identify specimens and explain their origins, allowing visitors to read the region as a geological archive rather than simply a flat agricultural landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bssnews.net/tourism/344780" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bssnews.net">[BSS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bssnews.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s, each accompanied by an identification plaque detailing its origin and...</span></span></span>

Older accounts mention petrified wood and fossilised rocks alongside granite, basalt, quartzite and large individual pieces. Descriptions differ over the precise number and age of objects, so dramatic claims about “ancient” specimens should be treated cautiously unless supported by the museum’s own labels or geological documentation. The collection is credible as an educational assemblage, but it is not presented online through a detailed, peer-reviewed catalogue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dailyasianage.com/news/254796/rocks-museum-in-panchagarh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dailyasianage.com">[The Asian Age]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dailyasianage.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Asian Age Rocks Museum in Panchagarh &#124; The Asian Age Online, Bangladesh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Asian Age Rocks Museum in Panchagarh &#124; The Asian Age Online, Bangladesh</span></span></span>

For strange-museum travellers, its appeal lies in scale and specificity: a local college campus preserving the silent debris of rivers and deep geological time. It is a good example of how Bangladesh’s unusual collections often emerge from regional initiative rather than national tourism planning.

## Money as archaeology, propaganda and illusion

Bangladesh Bank’s Taka Museum in Mirpur, Dhaka, is the country’s specialist museum of currency and numismatics. It began as a currency museum at the central bank’s Motijheel headquarters in 2009 before developing into a broader public museum. Its galleries place modern Bangladeshi notes beside coins and monetary objects from earlier political periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bb.org.bd/en/index.php/museum/index" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bb.org.bd">[bb.org.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bb.org.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bb.org.bd.</span></span></span>

The oldest pieces give the museum the atmosphere of an archaeological treasure room. Bangladesh Bank identifies Kushan-period coins and Gupta gold and silver issues among its ancient holdings, as well as later coinage and collections of gold pieces. These objects trace how authority, trade, religion and imperial imagery were stamped onto small, portable discs long before the establishment of modern Bangladesh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bb.org.bd/museum/collections/ancient.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bb.org.bd">[bb.org.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bb.org.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bb.org.bd.</span></span></span>

Currency museums also expose the deliberate theatre of money. A note is valuable partly because institutions, design and public belief make it so. Changes in portraits, monuments, scripts and symbols reveal shifts in the state’s preferred version of national identity. Bangladesh introduced redesigned notes in 2025 that removed the long-familiar portrait of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman from several denominations, demonstrating that banknotes remain politically active objects rather than neutral pieces of paper.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/world/south-asia/bangladesh-currency-drops-bangabandhus-image-new-notes-without-sheikh-mujibur-rahmans-picture-are-out/articleshow/121559121.cms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: timesofindia.indiatimes.com">[The Times of India]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">timesofindia.indiatimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on indiatimes.com.</span></span></span>

The museum is comparatively visitor-oriented. Official information mentions documentary screenings, touchscreens, a children’s area and a shop selling commemorative coins, notes, specimen currency and publications. This makes it one of the easier unusual collections to approach without specialist knowledge. Current opening information should still be checked with Bangladesh Bank, as unofficial schedules online are inconsistent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bb.org.bd/en/index.php/museum/facilities" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bb.org.bd">[bb.org.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bb.org.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bb.org.bd.</span></span></span>

## Folk belief preserved without becoming a witchcraft museum

Bangladesh has no securely documented public museum devoted exclusively to witchcraft, ghosts or ritual magic. The closest evidence-led route into supernatural belief is through folk-art and ethnological collections. Here, sacred legends, protective figures, festival practices and spirit-filled stories survive in scrolls, masks, musical instruments, household objects and ceremonial equipment.

The Folk Art and Craft Museum at Sonargaon was founded in 1975 through the work of artist Zainul Abedin and the Bangladesh Folk Arts and Crafts Foundation. Its displays include woodcarving, metalwork, terracotta, folk ornaments, musical instruments, masks and objects associated with rural occupations and domestic life. Although it is a major heritage attraction rather than a “weird museum”, it contains objects whose meanings extend beyond decoration into devotion, storytelling and protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://beautifulbangladesh.gov.bd/district-destination/narayanganj/heritage/117" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beautifulbangladesh.gov.bd">[Beautiful Bangladesh]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beautifulbangladesh.gov.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on beautifulbangladesh.gov.bd.</span></span></span>

Among the most intriguing traditions associated with such collections are painted scrolls recounting the deeds of Gazi Pir, a legendary holy figure connected in folk narratives with dangerous animals, forests and supernatural power. Banglapedia identifies these scrolls as a significant rural painting tradition and notes that the Sonargaon museum holds examples. The stories blend religious devotion, entertainment and local understandings of perilous landscapes; they should be read as [folk belief]({{ 'folk-belief/' | relative_url }}) and visual storytelling, not as evidence that miraculous events literally occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php?title=Gazir_Pat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.banglapedia.org">[en.banglapedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.banglapedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gazir Pat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gazir Pat</span></span></span>

Bangla Academy’s Folk Heritage Museum in Dhaka is smaller and less routinely accessible. The academy says it displays roughly 500 objects made from materials including wood, clay, metal and plant pith, and that it opened for display in 2020. Access is described as being provided when needed for folklore researchers, specialists and interested visitors, which suggests an archive-like institution rather than a guaranteed walk-in attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://banglaacademy.gov.bd/site/page/671ea032-0ceb-4f92-8f58-7ef91fb9ccab/-Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: banglaacademy.gov.bd">[banglaacademy.gov.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">banglaacademy.gov.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">জাদুঘর উপবিভাগ &#124; পাতা &#124; বাংলা একাডেমি</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">জাদুঘর উপবিভাগ &#124; পাতা &#124; বাংলা একাডেমি</span></span></span>

Its importance lies in preserving forms easily lost when handmade rural objects fall out of everyday use. Folk paintings, embroidered textiles, ornaments, painted rickshaw panels and musical instruments can contain comic exaggeration, dreamlike animals, religious imagery and scenes drawn from popular cinema. UNESCO describes Dhaka’s rickshaw painting as a mobile folk-art tradition that gives the city a distinctive shared identity. Once removed from the vehicle and placed in a museum case, these panels become fragments of an urban visual mythology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/rickshaw-painting-colourful-heritage-bangladesh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rickshaw Painting: A Colourful Heritage of Bangladesh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rickshaw Painting: A Colourful Heritage of Bangladesh</span></span></span>

## The Ethnological Museum and the problem of staged culture

The Ethnological Museum in Chattogram is the country’s principal museum devoted to the material cultures of different ethnic communities. Official and reference sources describe it as Bangladesh’s only dedicated ethnological museum. It opened to the public in the 1970s and developed galleries presenting communities from the Chittagong Hill Tracts and other regions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ethnologicalmuseum.chittagong.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/699149ba516a96d4de417380" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ethnologicalmuseum.chittagong.gov.bd">[ethnologicalmuseum.chittagong.gov.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ethnologicalmuseum.chittagong.gov.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on chittagong.gov.bd.</span></span></span>

Its cases and tableaux include boats, weapons, weaving equipment, baskets, pipes, jewellery, clothing and models of domestic or ceremonial scenes. Banglapedia records displays such as a Mru boat, Chakma writing, Manipuri dress, Marma festival objects and a model depicting an animal-sacrifice ceremony. For visitors interested in ritual objects and unusual social customs, these are among the most distinctive displays in Bangladesh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Ethnological_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.banglapedia.org">[en.banglapedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.banglapedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ethnological Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Among important exhibits are a typical boat of the Murangs; a showcase containing the ornaments of the Marma-Murangs; a model of the Tipr...</span></span></span>

The museum nevertheless requires critical viewing. Ethnological museums often freeze living communities into apparently timeless “traditional” scenes. Objects may be authentic while the arrangement reflects an older curatorial language in which minority groups are observed and classified from outside. Labels using historical names or broad categories may not match the identities preferred by communities today.

Visitors should therefore treat the galleries as two things at once: a valuable repository of fragile material culture and a record of how the state has chosen to represent cultural difference. The most useful questions are not simply “What is this strange object?” but “Who collected it, who explained it, and is the practice still understood in the same way?”

## Prison cells as historical exhibits

Old Dhaka Central Jail offers a much darker kind of curiosity experience. The prison on Nazimuddin Road operated for more than two centuries before inmates were transferred to a newer facility. When sections opened temporarily to visitors in 2016, reports described moss-covered buildings, cells and memorial museums connected with Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and four national political leaders murdered inside the jail in 1975.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thedailystar.net/country/news/dhaka-jail-opened-public-1307809" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thedailystar.net">[thedailystar.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thedailystar.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Daily Star Dhaka jail opened for public</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Daily Star Dhaka jail opened for public</span></span></span>

The preserved elements included rooms, personal objects and a chair associated with Mujibur Rahman. Unlike a replica dungeon built for tourism, this is an authentic carceral landscape: the architecture itself carries the evidence of confinement. That authenticity makes it potentially powerful, but it also demands interpretation that distinguishes political commemoration from a complete history of all those imprisoned there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thedailystar.net/city/228-yr-old-dhaka-jail-opens-today-1308181" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thedailystar.net">[The Daily Star]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thedailystar.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Daily Star228-yr-old Dhaka jail opens today</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Daily Star228-yr-old Dhaka jail opens today</span></span></span>

Public access has been intermittent because the wider site is undergoing conservation and redevelopment. A 2019 government project formally began work to preserve historic structures and their surroundings. Reporting in 2026 said that sections were expected to reopen gradually, with a museum planned to display archives and items used by notable inmates. It should therefore be considered a developing heritage complex rather than a reliably open conventional museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://prison.gov.bd/pages/news/6922dabe933eb65569e065f0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prison.gov.bd">[prison.gov.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prison.gov.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on prison.gov.bd.</span></span></span>

The site matters within Bangladesh’s strange-history landscape because prisons are normally hidden institutions. Turning one into a museum exposes the physical machinery of state punishment, but it can also narrow attention towards celebrated prisoners. A rigorous future museum would need to address ordinary inmates, colonial punishment, living conditions and changes in criminal justice as well as national political heroes.

## A former seat of power becomes a contested museum

The most recent addition to Bangladesh’s museum landscape is the conversion of Ganabhaban, the former official residence of Sheikh Hasina, into a museum relating to the July 2024 uprising and alleged abuses under her government. Reporting from early 2026 described preserved protest graffiti, damaged furniture, victims’ belongings, commissioned artworks and replicas of secret detention cells.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.com/world/asia/article/tulip-siddiq-sheik-hasina-bangladesh-r9vrwm0hf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.com">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times Tulip Siddiq, the despotic Bangladeshi PM and a house of memory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tulip Siddiq, a former UK MP and niece of Hasina, is also implicated in the scandal. This is an unusual museum not because its objects are eccentric, but because they have been transformed into exhibits almost immediately after a change of political power. A former bedroom, desk or shelving unit can move within months from private government property to evidence, memorial and artistic material. That speed gives the collection emotional force while also raising questions about long-term curatorial independence.

The museum reportedly juxtaposes bloodstained clothing, a child’s bicycle, protest remains and recreated detention spaces with accusations against the former regime. Such objects can communicate violence more directly than statistics, but the presentation remains politically contested. ## What Bangladesh does not currently offer

Claims about Bangladeshi ghost museums, UFO museums, cryptid collections or public witchcraft archives should be treated with caution. No well-documented permanent institution of those types emerged from official museum pages, major cultural reference sources or credible national reporting reviewed for this page. Folklore involving spirits, miraculous holy figures and supernatural danger certainly exists, but it is usually preserved through literature, performance, religious practice and general folk collections rather than a specialist museum.

Similarly, medical colleges may maintain anatomy, pathology or traditional-medicine teaching museums, but these are normally educational facilities for students rather than public attractions. For example, the Government Unani and Ayurvedic Medical College in Dhaka lists anatomy and medicinal-substance museums among its facilities, yet there is little reliable public visitor information. Such collections should not be advertised as tourist sites without direct permission from the institution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Unani_and_Ayurvedic_Medical_College_%26_Hospital" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Government Unani and Ayurvedic Medical College &amp; Hospital</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Unani and Ayurvedic Medical College &amp; Hospital</span></span></span>

The absence of a commercial “museum of the bizarre” is itself revealing. Bangladesh’s unusual objects are generally preserved because they are endangered, educational, politically significant or connected with cultural identity. They are rarely separated from their social purpose merely to create spectacle.

## Planning an unusual museum route

A realistic curiosity-focused journey would begin in Dhaka with the Taka Museum and, subject to access, the Bangla Academy folk collection. Sonargaon can be visited as a separate excursion for the richest concentration of folk crafts, masks, scroll traditions and rural-life displays. Mymensingh offers the specialist fish collection, while Panchagarh’s rock museum requires a much longer journey north. Chattogram’s Ethnological Museum forms the strongest south-eastern counterpart, especially for material connected with the cultures of the hill regions.

Access should always be verified directly. University museums may require prior contact, archive-like collections may open only by arrangement, and politically sensitive heritage sites may close or change entry procedures at short notice. Even apparently settled opening hours can vary around public holidays, religious observances, maintenance work or institutional events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fs.bau.edu.bd/view/pmarh-fish-museum-germplasm-centre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fs.bau.edu.bd">[edu.bd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fs.bau.edu.bd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pmarh fish museum germplasm centre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMARH Fish Museum &amp; Germplasm CentreProf. Mostafa Ali Reza Hossain Fish Museum and Germplasm Center (PMARH FMGC), established in 2009 is...</span></span></span>

The most rewarding approach is to abandon the expectation of a conventional weird-museum trail. Bangladesh’s real curiosities are embedded in wider stories: disappearing river species, stones carried from distant geological formations, money redesigned after political change, folk saints who command wild animals, minority cultures reconstructed in dioramas, and prisons or palaces converted into public memory. These collections are stranger—and more consequential—when read not as isolated oddities but as evidence of what the country fears losing, chooses to preserve and continues to argue about.
