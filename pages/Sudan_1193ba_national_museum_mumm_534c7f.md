## Introduction

Before the outbreak of war in April 2023, the [Sudan]({{ 'what-remains-of-sudans-strangest/' | relative_url }}) National Museum in Khartoum was one of Africa's most remarkable archaeological museums. It combined some of the world's earliest preserved human remains with entire ancient temples reconstructed in its gardens, creating an experience unlike most national museums. Instead of displaying only portable artefacts, it allowed visitors to walk through relocated Nubian sanctuaries while also examining evidence of ancient burial customs spanning thousands of years.

Today, the museum is known as much for what has been lost as for what it once contained. Systematic looting, severe structural damage and the destruction of galleries have transformed one of Sudan's greatest cultural institutions into a symbol of the wider threat facing the country's heritage. Although many monumental stone structures still stand, the fate of thousands of smaller objects remains uncertain, and recovery efforts continue amid an ongoing humanitarian crisis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/culture-emergencies/heritage-emergency-fund/urgent-safeguarding-response-damaged-collections-sudan-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Urgent Safeguarding Response for the Damaged Collections at the Sudan National Museum - UNESCO Heritage Emergency Fund...</span></span></span>

## The earliest mummies and what they reveal

The museum's most striking displays were its collection of ancient human remains. These were not presented as sensational curiosities but as archaeological evidence showing how communities living along the Nile understood death, religion and social identity over several millennia.

Among the best-known exhibits were naturally and deliberately preserved bodies dating back to roughly the third millennium BC. These rank among the oldest mummified human remains found in the Nile Valley outside Egypt. Unlike the highly standardised embalming techniques associated with later Egyptian royal burials, Sudan's early burials demonstrate a variety of preservation methods and funerary traditions, reflecting the development of local cultures such as Kerma before the rise of the Kingdom of Kush.

For archaeologists, these mummies have provided evidence about:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* ancient diets through bone and dental analysis;
* diseases and injuries suffered during life;
* burial customs and changing religious beliefs;
* textile production and wrapping techniques;
* trade through imported grave goods and jewellery.

</div>

The museum also displayed coffins, funerary equipment, pottery and personal ornaments recovered from Kushite and Meroitic cemeteries. Together they illustrated that ancient Sudan possessed sophisticated civilisations with their own artistic traditions rather than simply reflecting developments in neighbouring Egypt. This distinction has become increasingly important as modern scholarship has reassessed the independent history of Nubia and Kush.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/82998ed2a655113e86b938e705656889" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The conflict between the Sudanese army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) led to widespread destruction and looting, with RS...</span></span></span>

Human remains also prompted ethical questions familiar to museums worldwide. Curators increasingly interpreted the bodies as individuals whose lives could be studied respectfully, rather than as spectacular exhibits. The collection therefore occupied an unusual position: scientifically invaluable, publicly accessible and emotionally powerful without relying on myths about "mummy curses" or supernatural folklore.

## Rescued Nubian temples in the museum garden

The museum garden was arguably even more unusual than the galleries themselves. Rather than displaying architectural fragments indoors, it contained complete temples reconstructed stone by stone after being rescued from flooding during the construction of Egypt's Aswan High Dam in the 1960s.

The international UNESCO campaign to save Nubian monuments relocated several threatened structures to higher ground. While famous sites such as Abu Simbel remained in Egypt, Sudan received a number of rescued temples that were rebuilt within the grounds of the National Museum.

Among the most significant were:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The Temple of Buhen**, originally constructed during the reign of Pharaoh Hatshepsut near the Egyptian frontier fortress of Buhen.
* **The Temple of Semna**, another fortress temple associated with Egypt's Middle Kingdom frontier.
* **The Temple of Aksha**, dating largely to the reign of Ramesses II.
* Additional reconstructed chapels and architectural elements from Nubian sites threatened by the rising waters of Lake Nasser.

</div>

Unlike conventional archaeological displays, visitors could walk through these monuments almost as outdoor ruins. Although relocated from their original landscapes, they preserved carvings, inscriptions and architectural layouts that would otherwise have disappeared beneath the reservoir.

The juxtaposition was distinctive. Inside the museum were preserved bodies from ancient cemeteries; outside stood sacred buildings rescued from a modern engineering project. Few museums combined human remains and full-scale ancient architecture so directly within a single site. The reconstructed temples also illustrated one of the twentieth century's largest international heritage rescue operations, demonstrating how archaeology sometimes involves moving entire monuments rather than excavating isolated artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/culture-emergencies/heritage-emergency-fund/urgent-safeguarding-response-damaged-collections-sudan-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Urgent Safeguarding Response for the Damaged Collections at the Sudan National Museum - UNESCO Heritage Emergency Fund...</span></span></span>

## Looting, damage and the uncertain fate of the collection

The conflict that began in April 2023 fundamentally altered the museum's future. As fighting engulfed Khartoum, the museum fell within territory controlled for long periods by the Rapid Support Forces (RSF). Heritage specialists soon reported looting, vandalism and damage to the building.

UNESCO warned during 2024 that reports suggested widespread theft of collections and possible illicit trafficking of archaeological objects. International museum organisations urged collectors and dealers to remain alert for Sudanese artefacts entering the art market illegally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/sudan-unesco-raises-alarm-reports-illicit-trafficking-cultural-heritage" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sudan: UNESCO raises the alarm on reports of illicit trafficking of...</span></span></span>

A preliminary assessment after access became possible in March 2025 confirmed the scale of the destruction. According to UNESCO:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* exhibition galleries had been extensively damaged;
* museum infrastructure had largely collapsed;
* systematic looting had occurred;
* storage facilities holding thousands of archaeological objects had been badly affected;
* the annex containing more than 10,000 objects connected with the UNESCO Nubian Salvage Campaign suffered severe damage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/culture-emergencies/heritage-emergency-fund/urgent-safeguarding-response-damaged-collections-sudan-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Urgent Safeguarding Response for the Damaged Collections at the Sudan National Museum - UNESCO Heritage Emergency Fund...</span></span></span>

</div>

Reports from journalists and heritage workers described smashed display cases, damaged conservation facilities and at least one exposed mummy after storage units had been forced open. Portable collections—including gold artefacts, jewellery and smaller antiquities—appear to have been especially vulnerable because they could be removed and trafficked more easily than monumental stone sculptures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/82998ed2a655113e86b938e705656889" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The conflict between the Sudanese army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) led to widespread destruction and looting, with RS...</span></span></span>

By contrast, many of the museum's largest outdoor monuments survived largely because they were too heavy to steal. The reconstructed temples, colossal statues and stone lions remained standing despite suffering varying degrees of damage from neglect, vandalism or nearby fighting. Reuters photographs taken after the Sudanese army regained control of parts of Khartoum showed damaged temples, broken pottery scattered across the grounds and bullet-marked statues that nevertheless remained recognisable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/sudan-preservationists-struggle-restore-countrys-shattered-cultural-treasures-2025-09-30/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[reuters.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The conflict, which killed tens of thousands and displaced millions, led to the army retaking Khartoum from paramilitary forces, but left...</span></span></span>

## Why the museum matters beyond its collections

The Sudan National Museum was never simply a storehouse of ancient objects. It offered one of the clearest public narratives of Sudan's long history, linking prehistoric communities, the Kingdoms of Kerma and Kush, Christian Nubia and later Islamic cultures within a single institution.

Its mummies demonstrated that sophisticated funerary traditions flourished in ancient Sudan alongside—and sometimes independently from—those of Egypt. Its reconstructed temples preserved monuments that would otherwise have disappeared beneath an artificial lake. Together they challenged simplified views of Sudan as merely an extension of Egyptian civilisation, instead presenting the country as the centre of its own ancient cultures.

The wartime destruction therefore represents more than damage to a museum building. It has disrupted archaeological research, public education and national memory. International organisations and Sudanese specialists are now working to document surviving collections, identify missing artefacts, strengthen inventories and prepare for long-term conservation.
