## Introduction

The USS *Pueblo* is one of [North Korea]({{ 'what-makes-north-koreas-museums-so/' | relative_url }})'s most unusual public exhibits because it is both a genuine military artefact and a carefully staged political symbol. Captured by North Korean forces in January 1968 during an intelligence-gathering mission, the American naval vessel has remained in North Korean custody ever since. It is the only commissioned US Navy ship still held by another country and has been transformed into a permanent museum exhibit in Pyongyang.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-014/h-014-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command H-014-1 The Seizure of USS Pueblo (AGER-2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandH-014-1 The Seizure of USS Pueblo (AGER-2)May 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2019</span></span></span>

For visitors, the ship is presented not simply as a preserved vessel but as physical proof of a military victory over the United States. Within North Korea's wider landscape of unusual collections, the *Pueblo* stands apart because it turns an authentic Cold War object into a curated "war trophy", where every room, display and explanation reinforces the state's preferred historical narrative.

## How Did the USS *Pueblo* Become a Trophy?

On 23 January 1968, USS *Pueblo* (AGER-2) was conducting signals intelligence operations off the Korean Peninsula when it was intercepted by North Korean naval forces. The United States maintained that the ship was operating in international waters, while North Korea claimed it had violated territorial waters. After a brief engagement, the lightly armed vessel was boarded and seized. One American sailor was killed during the attack, and the remaining 82 crew members were taken prisoner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-014/h-014-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command H-014-1 The Seizure of USS Pueblo (AGER-2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandH-014-1 The Seizure of USS Pueblo (AGER-2)May 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2019</span></span></span>

The crew spent almost eleven months in captivity before being released in December 1968 following lengthy negotiations. Although the sailors returned home, the ship itself never did. Instead, North Korea retained it as captured property and eventually incorporated it into a permanent museum display, where it remains decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/fifty-years-ago-north-korea-captured-american-ship-and-nearly-started-nuclear-war-180967919/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

Unlike many military museums that preserve equipment from numerous conflicts, the *Pueblo* occupies a unique place because the exhibit revolves around a single captured object whose symbolic value has grown over time. Its continued display allows the original incident to be retold as an enduring national triumph rather than a historical episode.

### The capture and detention of the crew

North Korean guides traditionally describe the capture as evidence that their armed forces defeated an aggressive American spy mission. American and most international accounts instead describe the vessel as an intelligence-gathering ship seized during a Cold War confrontation, with the crew subjected to harsh imprisonment, interrogation and abuse before their release. These competing narratives remain central to how the ship is interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-014/h-014-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[navy.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command H-014-1 The Seizure of USS Pueblo (AGER-2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandH-014-1 The Seizure of USS Pueblo (AGER-2)May 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2019</span></span></span>

Because the exhibit is inseparable from these conflicting histories, visitors encounter not a neutral presentation but an explicitly political interpretation of the event.

## What Visitors Are Shown Aboard the Ship

Over the years the *Pueblo* has been relocated within Pyongyang and is now associated with the city's principal war museum complex. Foreign tourists permitted to visit have generally been escorted through selected parts of the ship by official guides.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/faces-from-afar-a-frightening-and-fascinating-journey-through-north-korea-180948509/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian MagazineFaces From Afar: A Frightening and Fascinating Journey Through North KoreaDecember 18, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 18, 2012</span></span></span>

The tour typically focuses less on the technical history of the vessel than on its symbolic role. Guides frame the ship as tangible proof that even a militarily stronger opponent can be defeated through determination and ideological strength.

The vessel itself therefore functions as the primary exhibit. Unlike many museum ships restored to illustrate everyday naval life, the *Pueblo* is preserved chiefly as captured evidence rather than as an example of naval engineering.

## How Military Museums Turn Machines into Evidence

The *Pueblo* illustrates a broader feature of North Korean museum culture: military hardware is displayed not simply as historical technology but as physical confirmation of official historical claims.

In many military museums around the world, captured weapons appear alongside material from both sides of a conflict to explain campaigns, tactics and technological development. In North Korea, the emphasis is different. Captured equipment often serves as visible proof that the state defeated powerful foreign enemies, regardless of the broader military balance or diplomatic context. The artefact itself becomes a witness supporting a predetermined narrative.

This approach is particularly striking because the ship is unquestionably authentic. There is no dispute that the vessel was captured or that it remains in North Korea. Debate instead centres on the interpretation attached to it: why it was operating near the Korean Peninsula, whether it entered territorial waters, the circumstances surrounding the seizure and the broader political consequences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-014/h-014-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[navy.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command H-014-1 The Seizure of USS Pueblo (AGER-2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandH-014-1 The Seizure of USS Pueblo (AGER-2)May 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2019</span></span></span>

As a result, the *Pueblo* demonstrates how museums can transform real objects into persuasive historical arguments through selective interpretation rather than through fabrication.

## Why the USS *Pueblo* Matters in North Korea's Curiosity Landscape

Among North Korea's unusual public collections, the *Pueblo* occupies a distinctive position because it combines several categories at once. It is simultaneously a preserved Cold War artefact, a functioning museum ship, a diplomatic relic and a state-sponsored war trophy.

For travellers interested in unusual collections rather than conventional military history, its significance lies less in the ship itself than in what its presentation reveals. Every aspect of the exhibit is designed to show how an authentic object can be used to reinforce national identity and political legitimacy.

Unlike curiosity museums built around eccentric collectors or strange objects, the *Pueblo* demonstrates a different kind of collecting culture: preserving the possessions of an adversary as permanent evidence of victory. In North Korea's tightly controlled museum system, few exhibits embody that idea more completely than the captured American intelligence ship that has remained on display for more than half a century.
