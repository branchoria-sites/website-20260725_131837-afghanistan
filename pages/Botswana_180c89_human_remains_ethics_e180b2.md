## Introduction

[Botswana]({{ 'where-botswana-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s debate over human remains is not centred on sensational displays or famous mummy galleries. Instead, it revolves around a small number of powerful cases that have shaped thinking about dignity, scientific research and the legacy of colonial collecting. The country's best-known examples are the naturally preserved Tuli mummy and the repatriation of the man widely known as "El Negro", whose body was displayed in a Spanish museum for decades before being returned to Botswana for burial in 2000. Together, these cases have helped redefine how museums, archaeologists and governments balance public education with respect for the dead, placing provenance, consent and cultural sensitivity ahead of curiosity alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ubrisa.ub.bw/jspui/handle/10311/507" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ubrisa.ub.bw">[UBrisa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ubrisa.ub.bw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Botswana Research, Innovation and Scholarship Archive: One body playing many parts-Ie Betjouana, el Negro, and il Bos...</span></span></span>

## The Tuli mummy and the limits of scientific display

One of Botswana's most unusual archaeological discoveries is the so-called Tuli mummy, a naturally preserved human body found in the country's eastern Tuli Block. Unlike the deliberately embalmed mummies of Egypt, this individual appears to have been preserved by local environmental conditions rather than by human intervention. Such naturally mummified remains are exceptionally rare in southern Africa, making the discovery scientifically valuable for understanding ancient health, burial practices and environmental history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botswana_National_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Botswana National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Botswana National Museum</span></span></span>

What makes the Tuli mummy significant today is not simply its rarity but the way it illustrates changing museum ethics. Earlier generations of museums often treated unusual human remains as spectacular exhibits. Contemporary practice increasingly asks different questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What research can legitimately be carried out?
* Is permanent public display justified?
* Should remains be exhibited at all, or primarily conserved for research?
* How should descendant communities be involved in decisions?

</div>

Botswana's museum sector has generally moved towards careful conservation and contextual interpretation rather than presenting archaeological bodies as curiosities. Human remains are treated primarily as archaeological and cultural evidence, not entertainment. This reflects broader international museum practice, where displays of the dead increasingly require clear educational purpose and careful ethical review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botswana_National_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Botswana National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Botswana National Museum</span></span></span>

## El Negro and the legacy of colonial display

The most influential case in Botswana's museum history concerns the individual long known internationally as "El Negro".

The man probably died in southern Africa around the early nineteenth century. According to research led by historians at the University of Botswana, his body was removed from its grave by French taxidermists during the 1830s, preserved using taxidermy techniques and eventually displayed in Europe as an ethnographic specimen. By the early twentieth century he had become the principal attraction of the Darder Museum in Banyoles, Spain, where visitors viewed him for decades as an exotic curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ubrisa.ub.bw/jspui/handle/10311/507" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ubrisa.ub.bw">[UBrisa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ubrisa.ub.bw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Botswana Research, Innovation and Scholarship Archive: One body playing many parts-Ie Betjouana, el Negro, and il Bos...</span></span></span>

The display attracted relatively little international attention until protests gathered momentum around the 1992 Barcelona Olympic Games. Critics argued that exhibiting the preserved body of an African man perpetuated scientific racism and colonial ideas that reduced human beings to museum objects. Campaigns by African governments, scholars and anti-racism organisations eventually led to the removal of the display and negotiations over repatriation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln0.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[Thuto]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro Index Page</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Negro Index Page...</span></span></span>

Research undertaken in Botswana challenged earlier assumptions about the man's identity. Rather than belonging to a vaguely defined "Bushman" population, historians concluded that he was probably a young Tswana-speaking man associated with the Batlhaping people living near the Orange and Vaal rivers in the early nineteenth century. This work demonstrated how colonial collecting practices had distorted both identity and provenance for generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ubrisa.ub.bw/jspui/handle/10311/507" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ubrisa.ub.bw">[UBrisa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ubrisa.ub.bw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Botswana Research, Innovation and Scholarship Archive: One body playing many parts-Ie Betjouana, el Negro, and il Bos...</span></span></span>

## Why the repatriation remained controversial

Although Spain agreed to return the remains in 2000, the process itself became another ethical lesson.

By the time the remains reached Botswana, much of the taxidermied material had been removed in Spain. Instead of receiving the preserved body that had stood in the museum for decades, Botswana received mainly skeletal remains packed in a small container. Many observers regarded this as another act of disrespect because decisions about what constituted the "museum object" had been made without meaningful African participation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln0.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[Thuto]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro Index Page</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Negro Index Page...</span></span></span>

The remains were buried in Gaborone, transforming a former museum exhibit into a recognised human burial. Rather than becoming a permanent attraction in Botswana, the case became an example of restitution and ethical responsibility. The emphasis shifted from ownership to dignity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln0.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[Thuto]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro Index Page</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Negro Index Page...</span></span></span>

The controversy also highlighted several wider questions that museums around the world continue to face:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Whether scientific value can justify displaying human remains.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/human-remains" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* How to establish the true identity and origin of historical individuals.
* Who has authority to decide the future of contested remains.
* Whether restitution should include associated artefacts and historical documentation, not simply the body itself.

</div>

## Research, repatriation and respectful treatment

Botswana's experience reflects a broader international movement away from treating human remains as collectible specimens and towards recognising them as people whose bodies deserve respectful handling.

For archaeologists, human remains remain an important source of evidence about diet, disease, migration and ancient lifeways. Scientific investigation can produce valuable knowledge that cannot easily be obtained elsewhere. At the same time, museums increasingly accept that research must be balanced against cultural values, religious beliefs and the wishes of communities connected to the deceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://d.lib.msu.edu/pula/342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: d.lib.msu.edu">[MSU Digital Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">d.lib.msu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MSU Digital LibraryMissing persons, stolen bodies and issues of patrimony: the El Negro story...</span></span></span>

In practical terms, this has encouraged several principles:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* establish clear provenance before acquisition or display;
* involve relevant communities whenever identities or cultural affiliations can be established;
* distinguish educational interpretation from spectacle;
* consider repatriation where remains were acquired through colonial exploitation or grave robbery;
* recognise that some remains may be better preserved in research collections or reburied rather than permanently exhibited.

</div>

These principles are now widely reflected in international museum ethics and have shaped how Botswana presents its own archaeological heritage.

## What these cases mean for Botswana's curiosity culture

Within Botswana's landscape of unusual heritage, human remains occupy a distinctive place. They are not celebrated as macabre attractions but used to explore difficult questions about colonialism, archaeology, identity and museum responsibility.

The Tuli mummy demonstrates how rare archaeological discoveries can advance scientific understanding while still demanding restraint in public presentation. The story of El Negro shows how an object once treated as an exhibit can become a symbol of restitution, historical correction and human dignity.

Together, they illustrate that Botswana's most compelling museum stories are often not about strange objects themselves, but about changing ideas of who has the right to collect, display and ultimately care for the dead.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
