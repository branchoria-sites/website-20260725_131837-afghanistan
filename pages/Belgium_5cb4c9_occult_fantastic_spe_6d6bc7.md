## Introduction

[Belgium]({{ 'why-belgium-keeps-its-strangest-objects/' | relative_url }}) has relatively few large public collections devoted exclusively to the occult, yet it has become adept at turning witches, monsters and unsettling imagery into memorable visitor experiences. In Bruges, a commercial Museum of Witchcraft presents the history of magic through theatrical sets, holograms and reconstructed scenes. In Brussels, the Fantastic Art Museum approaches the uncanny through painting, sculpture and surreal imagination rather than claims about supernatural reality. Together they demonstrate a distinctive Belgian pattern: the uncanny is often presented as a cultural performance, where education, entertainment and artistic fantasy deliberately overlap.

## Bruges and the public image of witchcraft

The Museum of Witchcraft in Bruges is designed first and foremost as an immersive visitor attraction. Rather than concentrating on Belgian witch trials alone, it ranges widely across ancient magic, European witch persecutions and contemporary Wicca, combining artefacts with dramatic lighting, life-sized figures and multimedia displays. Among the objects highlighted by the museum are a seventeenth-century witches' scales and a ducking stool associated with historical accusations of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitbruges.be/en/things-to-do/culture-and-heritage/museum-witchcraft" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitbruges.be">[Visit Bruges]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitbruges.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Bruges Museum of Witchcraft &#124; Visit Bruges</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Bruges Museum of Witchcraft &#124; Visit Bruges</span></span></span>

This presentation is highly accessible because it tells a story visually. Visitors move through scenes intended to evoke fear, suspicion and fascination, experiencing witchcraft as a changing cultural image rather than reading a chronological historical exhibition. The theatrical techniques are part of the attraction rather than an attempt to recreate historical reality.

That approach also creates an interpretive challenge. The museum's own narrative simplifies several centuries of European belief by presenting a broad progression from respected ancient figures to persecuted witches. Historians generally describe a more complicated process involving changing religious ideas, legal systems, local conflicts, fears of harmful magic and social tensions rather than one continuous evolution. The immersive displays therefore work best as interpretations of how modern society imagines witchcraft, not as primary historical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitbruges.be/en/things-to-do/culture-and-heritage/museum-witchcraft" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitbruges.be">[Visit Bruges]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitbruges.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Bruges Museum of Witchcraft &#124; Visit Bruges</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Bruges Museum of Witchcraft &#124; Visit Bruges</span></span></span>

The museum's location reinforces this visitor experience. It shares premises with a commercial torture museum and offers combination tickets, placing witchcraft within a broader tourist market built around dark history and macabre curiosity rather than within Bruges' publicly managed museum network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitbruges.be/en/things-to-do/culture-and-heritage/museum-witchcraft" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitbruges.be">[Visit Bruges]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitbruges.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Bruges Museum of Witchcraft &#124; Visit Bruges</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Bruges Museum of Witchcraft &#124; Visit Bruges</span></span></span>

## Fantastic art, monsters and visual play

Brussels presents a different route into the uncanny. The Fantastic Art Museum does not ask visitors to believe in monsters or supernatural events. Instead, it celebrates the artistic tradition of the fantastic: surreal creatures, grotesque figures, dream imagery and visual paradoxes that have long been associated with Belgian art.

The museum deliberately resembles a cabinet of curiosities rather than a conventional white-walled gallery. Paintings, sculptures and installations are arranged to create surprise and imaginative play, encouraging visitors to wander through an environment where the strange is normal. Official descriptions explicitly connect the collection with artists and traditions associated with Hieronymus Bosch, Pieter Bruegel, René Magritte, Paul Delvaux, Félicien Rops and Hergé, placing contemporary fantastic art within a much longer Belgian visual culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.brusselsmuseums.be/en/museums/maf-fantastic-art-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brusselsmuseums.be">[Brussels Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brusselsmuseums.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brussels Museums MAF</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brussels MuseumsMAF - Fantastic Art Museum &#124; Brussels Museums...</span></span></span>

Unlike a folklore museum, the Fantastic Art Museum is less concerned with preserving historical beliefs than with exploring imagination itself. Monsters become artistic devices rather than creatures from local legend. Surrealism, absurdity and grotesque humour replace claims about historical witchcraft or paranormal events.

The museum's limited weekend opening hours and relatively small scale also distinguish it from Belgium's major state museums. It functions more as a specialised cultural destination than as a national institution, attracting visitors who deliberately seek unusual artistic experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.brusselsmuseums.be/en/museums/maf-fantastic-art-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brusselsmuseums.be">[Brussels Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brusselsmuseums.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brussels Museums MAF</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brussels MuseumsMAF - Fantastic Art Museum &#124; Brussels Museums...</span></span></span>

## How Belgium manufactures the uncanny

Seen together, these attractions illustrate three different mechanisms for creating uncanny experiences.

**Historical staging.** The Bruges museum reconstructs moments of accusation, persecution and ritual using theatrical techniques that encourage emotional engagement before critical reflection.

**Artistic transformation.** The Brussels museum removes the question of supernatural truth altogether. Instead, it demonstrates how artists repeatedly transform fear, fantasy and monsters into visual creativity.

**Immersive design.** Neither attraction relies solely on display cases. Lighting, sound, scenography and carefully arranged spaces encourage visitors to feel as though they have entered another world rather than simply viewing collections.

These methods reflect wider trends in museum interpretation. Increasingly, visitor attractions seek emotional engagement through immersive presentation, but the most successful experiences also help audiences distinguish between atmosphere and evidence rather than allowing one to replace the other.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02086" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neurophysiological effects of museum modalities on emotional engagement with real artworksFebruary 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 2, 2026</span></span></span>

## How to separate atmosphere from historical evidence

Visitors interested in Belgium's stranger cultural attractions benefit from reading these sites on two levels at once.

First, ask what kind of institution you are visiting. A commercial attraction designed around immersive storytelling has different aims from a university collection, an archive or a municipal museum. Entertainment does not automatically reduce historical value, but it changes how exhibits should be interpreted.

Second, distinguish original objects from reconstructed settings. Authentic artefacts may sit within highly theatrical displays. The object can be genuine even when the surrounding environment is intentionally dramatic.

Third, pay attention to the language used. References to "belief", "legend", "folklore" or "tradition" describe cultural history, while statements implying historical certainty deserve closer scrutiny if they simplify complex subjects such as European witch persecutions.

Finally, remember that Belgium's strongest contribution to occult tourism is not the preservation of extensive witchcraft collections comparable to specialist museums elsewhere in Europe. Its distinctive achievement lies in showing how witches, monsters and unsettling imagery continue to be reimagined through tourism, artistic expression and carefully designed visitor experiences. The country's uncanny attractions are therefore most revealing when understood as performances of cultural memory rather than repositories of supernatural evidence.
