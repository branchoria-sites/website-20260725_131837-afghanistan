## Introduction

For visitors seeking Switzerland’s stranger side, the highlights include the witchcraft collections of Aargau and Glarus, the biomechanical nightmares of the H.R. [Giger Museum]({{ 'giger-museum/' | relative_url }}) in Gruyères, the University of Zurich’s remarkable [medical wax models]({{ 'wax-moulages/' | relative_url }}), and the cryptozoology holdings connected with Lausanne’s natural history collections. Some are public attractions, some are specialist research collections, and some preserve beliefs or legends rather than proven phenomena. ## Witchcraft, trials and Swiss folk beliefs

### The Hexenmuseum Schweiz: where folklore meets history

One of Switzerland’s most direct encounters with the country’s supernatural past is the Hexenmuseum Schweiz in Gränichen, canton Aargau. This private museum explores witch trials, folk religion, protective traditions, medicinal plants, magical beliefs and legendary creatures. Rather than presenting witchcraft as literal supernatural fact, the collection examines the objects, stories and practices that shaped European ideas about witches and hidden forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myswitzerland.com/en-ch/experiences/hexenmuseum-schweiz/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myswitzerland.com">[Switzerland Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myswitzerland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Switzerland Tourism Hexenmuseum Schweiz &#124; Switzerland Tourism</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Switzerland Tourism Hexenmuseum Schweiz &#124; Switzerland Tourism</span></span></span>

The museum is unusual because it sits between folklore museum, historical archive and immersive curiosity attraction. Visitors encounter material connected with popular beliefs: charms, herbs, supernatural beings and traditional explanations of illness or misfortune. Its importance lies less in proving magical claims than in showing how communities once interpreted danger, healing and uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myswitzerland.com/en-ch/experiences/witch-museum-switzerland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myswitzerland.com">[Switzerland Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myswitzerland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Switzerland Tourism Hexenmuseum Schweiz &#124; Switzerland Tourism</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Switzerland Tourism Hexenmuseum Schweiz &#124; Switzerland Tourism</span></span></span>

### Anna Göldi and the memory of witch persecution

Switzerland also preserves one of Europe’s most famous witchcraft cases through the Anna Göldi Museum in Glarus. Göldi was executed in 1782 and is often described as the last person executed for witchcraft in Europe. Modern interpretations focus on the injustice of her trial and the wider history of persecution rather than accepting the accusations against her. Her case was officially rehabilitated by the canton of Glarus in 2008.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/culture/from-the-cute-to-the-horrific-switzerlands-weirdest-museums/77796301" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swissinfo.ch">[SWI swissinfo.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swissinfo.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SWI swissinfo.ch From the cute to the horrific: Switzerland’s weirdest museums</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWI swissinfo.chFrom the cute to the horrific: Switzerland’s weirdest museums - SWI swissinfo.chMay 22, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 22, 2024</span></span></span>

The museum’s significance is that it transforms a story once framed as supernatural crime into a discussion about law, prejudice, social fear and human rights. It belongs as much to the history of justice as to the history of witch legends.

## Alien art and manufactured nightmares

### The H.R. Giger Museum: Switzerland’s biomechanical dark side

The H.R. Giger Museum in Gruyères is one of Switzerland’s most internationally recognised unusual collections. It preserves the work of Swiss artist Hans Ruedi Giger, whose biomechanical style combined human anatomy, machines and surreal horror. The museum contains paintings, sculptures, furniture and film designs, including work connected with the creature design for the 1979 film *Alien*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myswitzerland.com/en-gb/experiences/hr-giger-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myswitzerland.com">[Switzerland Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myswitzerland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Switzerland Tourism Musée H.R. Giger &#124; Switzerland Tourism</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Switzerland Tourism Musée H.R. Giger &#124; Switzerland Tourism</span></span></span>

Unlike folklore collections based on older beliefs, Giger’s world is a consciously created mythology. His disturbing forms are artistic inventions rather than claims about real extraterrestrial life. The fascination comes from how convincingly he blended familiar biological shapes with mechanical structures, creating imagery that feels both futuristic and ancient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myswitzerland.com/en-gb/experiences/hr-giger-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myswitzerland.com">[Switzerland Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myswitzerland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Switzerland Tourism Musée H.R. The museum is especially unusual because of its setting: Giger’s works are displayed inside Château St-Germain in the medieval town of Gruyères, creating a deliberate contrast between traditional Swiss heritage and unsettling science-fiction imagery. The adjacent Giger Bar extends the same artistic language into a public space designed around his skeletal, alien-inspired aesthetic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myswitzerland.com/en-gb/experiences/hr-giger-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myswitzerland.com">[Switzerland Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myswitzerland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Switzerland Tourism Musée H.R. ## Medical curiosities preserved as science

### The Museum of Wax Moulages in Zurich

One of Switzerland’s strangest collections is also one of its most scientific. The Museum of Wax Moulages at the University of Zurich preserves highly realistic wax models showing skin diseases and other medical conditions. Created originally as teaching tools before modern photography and digital imaging became dominant, these three-dimensional models allowed doctors and students to study conditions in detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uzh.ch/en/explore/museums/moulagenmuseum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uzh.ch">[UZH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uzh.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum of Wax Moulages &#124; University of Zurich &#124; UZH</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum of Wax Moulages &#124; University of Zurich &#124; UZH...</span></span></span>

The collection is unusual because the objects look like traditional curiosity-cabinet specimens, yet their purpose was practical education. They are not theatrical “freak show” displays; they are historical medical records. The Zurich collection is considered among the best preserved and most lifelike wax moulage collections in the world, with around 600 examples displayed from a much larger holding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uzh.ch/en/explore/museums/moulagenmuseum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uzh.ch">[UZH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uzh.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum of Wax Moulages &#124; University of Zurich &#124; UZH</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum of Wax Moulages &#124; University of Zurich &#124; UZH...</span></span></span>

For modern visitors, the museum offers a reminder that medical history itself can appear strange. Diseases that are now rare, better understood or treated differently remain visible as carefully crafted physical records of earlier medical knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zuercher-museen.ch/en/museums/museum-of-wax-moulages" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zuercher-museen.ch">[Zürcher Museen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zuercher-museen.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Zürcher Museen Museum of Wax Moulages &#124; Zürcher Museen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zürcher Museen Museum of Wax Moulages &#124; Zürcher Museen</span></span></span>

## Monsters, mysteries and unexplained creatures

### Switzerland’s cryptozoology collection

Switzerland has a more academic connection to monsters through the cryptozoology collection at the Naturéum in Lausanne. Cryptozoology is the study of animals whose existence has not been scientifically proven, often based on reports, images, tracks or stories. Famous examples include creatures such as the yeti, Bigfoot and the Loch Ness monster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://natureum.ch/en/discover/museum-libraries/cryptozoology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: natureum.ch">[natureum.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">natureum.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptozoology &#124; Naturéum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptozoology &#124; Naturéum</span></span></span>

The Lausanne collection is linked to Bernard Heuvelmans, a Belgian zoologist often regarded as a founder of modern cryptozoology. He donated documentation, books, reports, drawings and related material to the zoological collections. The material is valuable mainly as a record of human attempts to investigate mysterious animals, not as proof that legendary creatures exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://natureum.ch/en/discover/museum-libraries/cryptozoology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: natureum.ch">[natureum.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">natureum.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptozoology &#124; Naturéum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptozoology &#124; Naturéum</span></span></span>

This distinction makes the collection especially interesting. It preserves the history of a question rather than a confirmed answer: why people report strange creatures, how evidence is judged, and where the boundary lies between folklore, investigation and science.

## Why Switzerland’s strange collections matter


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Switzerland’s unusual museums reveal a different side of a country often associated with order, technology and reliability. They show a long-running interest in collecting difficult subjects: fears of witchcraft, imagined worlds beyond Earth, diseases of the human body and creatures that exist mainly in stories.

</div>

The strongest collections do not simply present the strange as entertainment. The Hexenmuseum and Anna Göldi Museum examine belief and persecution; the Moulagenmuseum preserves medical knowledge; the Naturéum’s cryptozoology holdings document attempts to understand mysteries; and the H.R. Giger Museum demonstrates how an artist can create a new mythology from anxiety about bodies, machines and the unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.myswitzerland.com/en-ch/experiences/hexenmuseum-schweiz/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myswitzerland.com">[myswitzerland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myswitzerland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Switzerland Tourism Hexenmuseum Schweiz &#124; Switzerland Tourism</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Switzerland Tourism Hexenmuseum Schweiz &#124; Switzerland Tourism</span></span></span>

Taken together, Switzerland’s curiosity culture is less about proving monsters or magic exist and more about preserving the human urge to investigate, explain and imagine. Its strangest collections are ultimately museums of uncertainty: places where science, memory, folklore and creativity meet.
