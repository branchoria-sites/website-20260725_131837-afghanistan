## Introduction

[France]({{ 'what-makes-frances-strangest-museums-so/' | relative_url }})’s most unsettling medical museums were never created to shock visitors. They were built because generations of doctors needed a way to study the human body before photography, medical imaging and digital teaching transformed medical education. Preserved bodies, flayed anatomical preparations, wax models of diseased skin and carefully catalogued pathological specimens allowed students to compare real cases, recognise rare conditions and understand anatomy with a level of realism that drawings alone could not provide.

Today these collections can appear disturbing, yet their original purpose changes how they are understood. Rather than celebrating the macabre, France's surviving medical museums preserve the history of diagnosis, anatomy and clinical teaching. ## The preserved bodies of the Musée Fragonard

The Musée Fragonard at the National Veterinary School in Maisons-Alfort is perhaps France's most famous example of a teaching collection whose exhibits still have the power to surprise modern visitors. Although many people associate it with horror imagery, it originated as part of one of Europe's oldest veterinary schools, founded in the eighteenth century to educate surgeons and veterinarians through direct observation of anatomy.

Its most celebrated objects are the *écorchés*—real bodies whose skin was removed so muscles, tendons and blood vessels could be displayed in lifelike poses. These preparations are associated with Honoré Fragonard, the school's first professor of anatomy. His techniques remain only partly understood, but they combined careful dissection with preservation methods that allowed the tissues to survive for centuries. The museum regards the mounted figure of a man riding a dissected horse as the only surviving large composition that can confidently be attributed to Fragonard himself, an important distinction because many other anatomical preparations have been linked to him without firm evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr/le-musee-des-moulages-de-lhopital-de-saint-louis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr">[Hôpital Saint-Louis]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hôpital Saint-Louis Le musée des moulages &#124; Hôpital Saint-Louis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hôpital Saint-LouisLe musée des moulages &#124; Hôpital Saint-LouisMay 21, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 21, 2026</span></span></span>

What makes these displays unusual is not simply that they contain preserved human remains. They demonstrate an eighteenth-century belief that anatomy could be learned through three-dimensional observation rather than diagrams alone. Every exposed tendon and muscle became part of an educational model intended to reveal how living bodies functioned.

The museum also broadens this approach beyond humans. Comparative anatomy sits alongside veterinary pathology, congenital abnormalities, skeletons and preserved animal organs, encouraging students to compare structures across species. Seen in this context, the famous human figures become one element within a much larger scientific collection rather than isolated curiosities.

## Wax diseases and clinical teaching at Saint-Louis

If the Musée Fragonard demonstrates anatomy, the Musée des Moulages at Hôpital Saint-Louis explains how nineteenth-century physicians taught diagnosis before colour photography.

Instead of preserving whole bodies, doctors commissioned extraordinarily realistic wax casts taken directly from patients suffering from skin diseases. These moulages reproduced the exact colour, texture and shape of lesions in three dimensions, giving students an opportunity to study conditions that might otherwise disappear as patients recovered or died.

The collection began in 1867 when dermatologist Charles Lailler asked the modeller Jules Baretta—originally a maker of artificial fruit—to produce accurate wax reproductions of skin disorders. The resulting models proved so useful that Baretta abandoned his previous trade and spent decades creating thousands of medical moulages. Today the museum preserves nearly 5,000 examples, making it one of the world's largest surviving dermatological wax collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr/le-musee-des-moulages-de-lhopital-de-saint-louis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr">[Hôpital Saint-Louis]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hôpital Saint-Louis Le musée des moulages &#124; Hôpital Saint-Louis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hôpital Saint-LouisLe musée des moulages &#124; Hôpital Saint-LouisMay 21, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 21, 2026</span></span></span>

These wax models solved several practical problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **They preserved rare cases** long after patients had left hospital.
* **They showed true colour**, something black-and-white photography could not capture.
* **They standardised teaching**, allowing every student to examine the same condition closely.
* **They supported international medicine**, since visiting physicians could compare diseases without relying solely on written descriptions.

</div>

The museum building itself reflected this educational mission. Opened in 1889 alongside the first International Congress of Dermatology, it combined consultation rooms, a teaching library, conference facilities and the wax collection into a single institute devoted to dermatological education. The arrangement demonstrated that the moulages were not museum pieces in the modern sense but working teaching tools integrated into everyday clinical practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr/histoire-du-musee-des-moulages" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr">[Hôpital Saint-Louis]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hôpital Saint-Louis Histoire du Musée des Moulages &#124; Hôpital Saint-Louis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hôpital Saint-Louis Histoire du Musée des Moulages &#124; Hôpital Saint-Louis</span></span></span>

## Why preserve bodies instead of relying on books?

Modern readers often assume anatomical collections existed because medicine lacked ethical standards. The historical reality is more practical.

Before X-rays, MRI scans and high-resolution medical photography, physicians had limited ways to record complex diseases. Printed illustrations depended heavily on an artist's interpretation, while many pathological conditions changed rapidly over time.

Teaching collections addressed these limitations in different ways:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Anatomical preparations revealed internal structures impossible to understand from flat illustrations.
* Preserved organs allowed repeated study by successive generations of students.
* Wax moulages captured the appearance of diseases with remarkable fidelity.
* Large collections enabled comparison between common and extremely rare conditions.

</div>

In effect, these museums functioned as three-dimensional medical textbooks assembled over decades or even centuries.

## How context changes shock into medical history

Visitors frequently describe these collections as disturbing on first encounter, particularly the flayed human figures at the Musée Fragonard or the vivid dermatological waxes at Saint-Louis. Yet the historical context fundamentally alters their meaning.

Unlike commercial exhibitions built around spectacle, French medical collections were organised according to scientific principles. Objects were classified by anatomy or pathology, accompanied by teaching materials and intended to answer clinical questions rather than provoke emotional reactions. Even the arrangement of the Saint-Louis wax models alphabetically by disease reflected educational priorities, making the collection easier for students to navigate regardless of their level of medical training.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr/histoire-du-musee-des-moulages" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr">[Hôpital Saint-Louis]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hopital-saintlouis.aphp.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hôpital Saint-Louis Histoire du Musée des Moulages &#124; Hôpital Saint-Louis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hôpital Saint-Louis Histoire du Musée des Moulages &#124; Hôpital Saint-Louis</span></span></span>

The experience also illustrates changing museum ethics. Nineteenth-century physicians generally referred to preserved remains as specimens or preparations, emphasising their scientific value. Contemporary curators increasingly seek to restore the humanity of the individuals behind these collections, acknowledging that many historical specimens were stripped of personal identity. Recent debates in France have focused on documentation, provenance, respectful interpretation and, where appropriate, restitution or rehumanisation of human remains held in scientific collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/science/article/2026/04/08/in-catacombs-museums-and-labs-the-delicate-heritage-of-human-remains_6752206_10.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It opens with the Paris Catacombs’ recent restoration, emphasizing a shift from spectacle to a respectful memory space, where the presenc...</span></span></span>

Rather than diminishing their historical importance, these ethical discussions have added another layer of interpretation. Visitors are encouraged to consider not only what the collections taught doctors in the past, but also how medicine itself has changed in its understanding of consent, dignity and the display of the human body.

## Why these collections matter within France's curiosity culture

France's medical museums occupy a distinctive place among the country's unusual collections because they were never intended as cabinets of horror. Their strangeness comes from preserving scientific practices that once represented the cutting edge of medical education.

The Musée Fragonard shows how anatomy became visible through preserved bodies, while the Musée des Moulages demonstrates how disease itself could be transformed into durable teaching material. Together they reveal a period when the body was treated as a physical archive of medical knowledge, carefully collected so future doctors could learn from it.

For visitors interested in France's stranger museums, these institutions stand apart from folklore attractions, curiosity cabinets or theatrical displays. Their unsettling appearance is inseparable from a serious educational purpose, reminding us that many of history's most extraordinary museum objects were created not to entertain, but to teach.

    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
