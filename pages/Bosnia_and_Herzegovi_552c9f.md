## Introduction

Some are state or academically curated museums, some remain connected to religious communities or military property, and others are visitor-facing attractions built around living belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/project-d-0-ark-1-2-3-4-5-artistic-collaboration-turns-nuclear-bunker-art-gallery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project D-0 ARK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An artistic collaboration turns...23 Jun 2021 — The publication is a ten-year retrospective of the “Project D – 0 Ark”, known today as t...</span></span></span>

## The nuclear bunker that became an art museum

The clearest answer to “What is Bosnia and Herzegovina’s strangest museum?” is ARK D-0 near Konjic, commonly called Tito’s bunker. Constructed by the Yugoslav military between 1953 and 1979, the vast underground command shelter was intended to protect President Josip Broz Tito and senior military and political personnel during a nuclear attack. Its roughly 6,500 square metres contain interconnected residential, communications, command and technical areas, creating the impression of a sealed subterranean settlement rather than a conventional exhibition building.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biennialfoundation.org/biennials/d-0-ark-biennial-bosnia-and-herzegovina/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biennialfoundation.org">[biennialfoundation.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biennialfoundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Biennial FoundationD-0 ARK Biennial (Bosnia and Herzegovina)The Project Biennial of Contemporary Art, D-0 ARK Underground was organized i...</span></span></span>

What makes ARK D-0 unusual is that its machinery, corridors, conference rooms and private quarters are not merely backdrops. The bunker itself is the principal artefact. Visitors move through an environment designed around secrecy, hierarchy and anticipated apocalypse, with much of its original military character still legible. Public access is normally organised through guided visits, reflecting both the complexity of the site and its continuing status as controlled heritage rather than an abandoned ruin open for casual exploration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armijska_Ratna_Komanda_D-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Armijska Ratna Komanda D-0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Armijska Ratna Komanda D-0</span></span></span>

The bunker also operates as a hybrid contemporary-art and military museum. Beginning in 2011, the D-0 ARK Underground project brought artists and curators into the complex, installing works that respond to Cold War power, surveillance, confinement and collective memory. UNESCO has described the project as an international artistic collaboration that turned the nuclear shelter into an art gallery, while the project itself presents the bunker as a combined military museum and contemporary exhibition space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/project-d-0-ark-1-2-3-4-5-artistic-collaboration-turns-nuclear-bunker-art-gallery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project D-0 ARK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An artistic collaboration turns...23 Jun 2021 — The publication is a ten-year retrospective of the “Project D – 0 Ark”, known today as t...</span></span></span>

That artistic layer changes the visit. A purely technical museum might celebrate engineering ingenuity; an untouched bunker might invite Cold War nostalgia or simple astonishment. The installations instead encourage visitors to question why such enormous resources were devoted to protecting a political elite, what sort of future the bunker imagined, and how a secret state facility should be interpreted after the state that built it has disappeared. The result is credible as preserved military heritage, but deliberately unsettled as historical storytelling: the art prevents the rooms from becoming neutral scenery.

## Sarajevo’s underground museum of survival

Sarajevo’s Tunnel of Hope is another museum in which the location carries more force than any detachable object. During the siege of Sarajevo, a tunnel was dug beneath the airport to connect the encircled city with territory outside the siege lines. A preserved section, associated buildings, photographs, equipment and interpretive displays now form the Tunnel D-B Memorial Complex, administered by the Sarajevo Memorial Centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tunelspasa.ba/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tunelspasa.ba">[mcsarajevo.ba]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tunelspasa.ba</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tunelspasa.ba.</span></span></span>

The museum is unusual because an improvised piece of wartime infrastructure has become both evidence and exhibition. Visitors are not simply shown maps of the siege; they enter part of the narrow passage through which people, food, medicine, weapons, fuel and information moved. Its low ceiling and confined dimensions make the physical reality of the route immediately understandable, although the accessible portion is only a fragment of the original tunnel.

This is neither an eccentric private collection nor a theatrical haunted attraction. It is a public memorial site interpreting a recent conflict whose causes, terminology and political legacy remain sensitive. Its strongest exhibits are practical objects: tools, containers, communications equipment and photographs showing how an extraordinary underground system functioned. The curatorial challenge is to preserve the ingenuity of civilian survival without turning the siege into an adventure story. The site matters within Bosnia and Herzegovina’s curiosity landscape because it reveals how an apparently mundane structure can become one of a city’s most charged historical objects.

ARK D-0 and the Tunnel of Hope make a revealing pair. The Konjic bunker was secret, expensive and designed from above to shelter state leadership from a hypothetical catastrophe. The Sarajevo tunnel was improvised under siege to sustain a city during an actual catastrophe. One embodies planned elite survival; the other, collective endurance. Seen together, they form an unusually powerful underground history of twentieth-century Bosnia and Herzegovina.

## When toys and small belongings become evidence

The [War Childhood]({{ 'war-childhood/' | relative_url }}) Museum in Sarajevo takes a very different approach. Rather than reconstructing military operations, it collects personal belongings and recorded testimony from people whose childhoods were affected by war. Its Bosnian collection includes more than 5,000 objects, among them toys, books, letters, photographs, improvised clothing and other possessions whose significance comes from the story attached to them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warchildhood.org/global-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warchildhood.org">[War Childhood]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warchildhood.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on warchildhood.org.</span></span></span>

A single object may appear ordinary until its owner explains how it was used during shelling, displacement, bereavement or prolonged scarcity. This pairing of material evidence with first-person memory gives the museum the intimate atmosphere of a collective memory box. Accounts are generally presented without overwhelming visitors with military chronology, shifting attention from commanders and territorial changes towards the routines, inventions and fears of children.

The museum is professionally curated rather than merely sentimental. Objects and audio-visual testimony are preserved for exhibition, education and research, and the collection has expanded beyond Bosnia and Herzegovina to include childhood experiences from other conflicts. That broader scope allows comparison without erasing the institution’s origins in the Bosnian war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warchildhood.org/global-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warchildhood.org">[warchildhood.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warchildhood.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on warchildhood.org.</span></span></span>

Its strangeness is ethical rather than sensational. A toy, diary or scrap of cloth becomes museum-worthy not because it is rare or beautiful, but because it carries an experience that official records may fail to preserve. For visitors interested in unusual collections, this is an important corrective to the traditional cabinet of curiosities: the value of the object rests not in exoticism but in testimony.

## The Sarajevo Haggadah and the power of a single object

The National Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina is a large scholarly institution covering archaeology, ethnology and natural history, but its most famous object has the aura of a treasure kept apart from the ordinary flow of the galleries. The [Sarajevo Haggadah]({{ 'haggadah/' | relative_url }}) is an illuminated Jewish manuscript made on parchment, containing 142 leaves and a remarkable cycle of biblical miniatures. It was probably produced in medieval Spain and later travelled through Jewish exile and migration before reaching Sarajevo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zemaljskimuzej.ba/en/item/sarajevo-haggadah/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zemaljskimuzej.ba">[Zemaljski muzej BiH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zemaljskimuzej.ba</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on zemaljskimuzej.ba.</span></span></span>

Its appeal is partly visual, but its modern history has made it a symbol of survival. The manuscript endured the expulsion of Jews from Iberia, later persecution and the upheavals of twentieth-century Bosnia. It has consequently accumulated stories of rescue, concealment and intercommunal protection. The museum itself presents the book as evidence of a society capable of preserving the heritage of religious communities other than the majority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zemaljskimuzej.ba/en/new-edition-of-the-sarajevo-haggadah-reprint-and-study-available/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zemaljskimuzej.ba">[Zemaljski muzej BiH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zemaljskimuzej.ba</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Zemaljski muzej Bi HNew Edition of the Sarajevo Haggadah Reprint and Study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zemaljski muzej Bi HNew Edition of the Sarajevo Haggadah Reprint and Study</span></span></span>

Visitors should not assume that the original is continuously displayed like an ordinary book. Because of its fragility and value, access is restricted to scheduled viewing periods in a protected room; the museum publishes specific days and times for seeing it. Current arrangements should be checked directly before travelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zemaljskimuzej.ba/en/admission/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zemaljskimuzej.ba">[Zemaljski muzej BiH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zemaljskimuzej.ba</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on zemaljskimuzej.ba.</span></span></span>

The Haggadah is unusual not because its provenance is paranormal or unexplained, but because one small manuscript has become a container for several histories at once: medieval Jewish art, Sephardic displacement, Sarajevo’s multicultural identity and the repeated danger of cultural destruction. It also demonstrates how the country’s most compelling curiosity objects often gain meaning through survival rather than mystery.

## Franciscan monasteries as living cabinets of memory

Some of Bosnia and Herzegovina’s richest collections remain inside functioning Franciscan monasteries. These institutions combine church, archive, library and museum, often preserving objects accumulated over centuries rather than assembled according to a modern collecting plan. Their displays can therefore feel closer to historic treasuries or cabinets of memory than to streamlined national museums.

The Franciscan monastery at Fojnica is the leading example. Its holdings include early printed books, manuscripts, Ottoman documents, coins, weapons, paintings, church objects and regional costume. Sources describe a library of thousands of volumes, including incunabula—books printed in Europe before 1501—and works written in historic Bosnian scripts. The archive also preserves thousands of Ottoman-era documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.resilience-ri.eu/blog/treasures-from-eastern-europe-franciscan-monastery-fojnica/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resilience-ri.eu">[resilience-ri.eu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resilience-ri.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">treasures from eastern europe franciscan monastery fojnica</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">treasures from eastern europe franciscan monastery fojnica</span></span></span>

The best-known object is a charter traditionally associated with Sultan Mehmed II’s conquest of Bosnia in 1463, granting protection and religious freedom to the Franciscans. Within the monastery, it functions as far more than an administrative document: it is treated as a foundational witness to the community’s survival under Ottoman rule. The collection also contains the Fojnica Armorial, a heraldic manuscript whose dating and historical reliability require care. Later copies and competing interpretations mean it should not be treated as a straightforward medieval register of noble families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://go-bosnia.com/en/entity/franciscan-monastery-fojnica/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: go-bosnia.com">[go-bosnia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">go-bosnia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Bosnia – Bosna na dlanu!Franciscan Monastery in Fojnica</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Go Bosnia – Bosna na dlanu!Franciscan Monastery in Fojnica</span></span></span>

That mixture of devotional importance, political symbolism and scholarly uncertainty is precisely what makes Fojnica valuable to curiosity-minded visitors. The monastery is not presenting a hoax collection, yet some objects have acquired reputations larger and cleaner than their documented provenance allows. A responsible visit distinguishes the physical manuscript from later claims made about it.

Other Franciscan museums extend this pattern. The museum at Humac, established in the nineteenth century, preserves archaeological material gathered around the monastery and is often described as the country’s oldest museum. Its identity is inseparable from local collecting by friars, showing how religious houses served as early archaeological repositories before modern state institutions were fully developed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museums.eu/museum/details/300" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.eu">[Museums.EU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museums.eu.</span></span></span>

These monastery museums are public-facing but remain living religious environments. Opening arrangements may be more limited or less standardised than at major city museums, and interpretation may reflect both academic research and the spiritual identity of the custodians. Visitors should approach them neither as neutral archaeological stores nor as mere collections of sacred relics, but as institutions where scholarship, communal memory and devotion overlap.

## Icons, reliquaries and sacred craftsmanship

Sarajevo’s Museum of the Old Orthodox Church offers another concentrated religious collection. Its holdings include icons, liturgical vestments and gold and silver church objects, with works from both local traditions and wider Orthodox artistic networks, including icons associated with Crete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museums.eu/museum/details/290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.eu">[Museums.EU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museums.eu.</span></span></span>

To a visitor accustomed to secular galleries, such a treasury may resemble a cabinet of precious and mysterious objects. Yet the exhibits were not originally made as curiosities. Icons are devotional images, and vessels, garments and metalwork were created for worship. Their unusual power in a museum setting comes from the tension between religious function and art-historical display.

The collection also helps explain why Bosnia and Herzegovina’s curiosity culture cannot be separated neatly into ethnic or religious compartments. Manuscripts, icons, Catholic archives, Islamic documents and Jewish books survived through overlapping systems of custody, trade and protection. Their meanings today are shaped by both the communities that venerate them and the museums that catalogue them.

## Medjugorje: belief, souvenirs and an unresolved apparition

Medjugorje, in western Herzegovina, is not primarily a museum. It is a pilgrimage centre built around claims that six young people began seeing the Virgin Mary in 1981. The reported apparitions transformed a relatively small settlement into an international religious destination of churches, prayer sites, devotional businesses, hotels and shops selling rosaries, statues, medals, bottles and printed messages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medjugorje" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

For a project concerned with strange public collections, Medjugorje matters because the entire town operates as a living economy of sacred objects and testimony. Devotional items are purchased, blessed, carried home and incorporated into private collections. Written messages attributed to the apparition circulate as spiritual texts. Stones, photographs and objects associated with pilgrimage journeys may acquire personal relic-like status even when they have no formal standing as church relics.

The credibility question requires precision. In September 2024, the Vatican authorised devotion and pilgrimages connected with Medjugorje by issuing a favourable pastoral judgement. It did not authenticate the alleged apparitions as supernatural. The decision recognised positive spiritual effects associated with the site while advising caution towards purported messages and claims. Catholics may participate in the devotion, but they are not required to accept the visions as genuine appearances of Mary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/pope-francis-allows-devotion-medjugorje-where-virgin-mary-said-appear-2024-09-19/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[reuters.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">While the pope has not confirmed the authenticity of the apparitions, he highlights the positive spiritual outcomes for Catholics from th...</span></span></span>

This distinction is often blurred by pilgrimage marketing. Believers may speak of the apparitions as fact; sceptics may treat the whole landscape as commercialised folklore; the Catholic authorities occupy a more careful position, permitting devotion without confirming the supernatural explanation. For visitors interested in unusual religion, Medjugorje is therefore less a museum of an established miracle than a vast, still-developing archive of belief, testimony, objects and commerce.

## Medieval tombstones and speculative meanings

Bosnia and Herzegovina is also known for its medieval carved tombstones, many decorated with figures, animals, weapons, geometric patterns and celestial motifs. Large groups survive in open-air burial grounds, making the landscape itself a dispersed archaeological collection. The stones are firmly established historical monuments, but their imagery has encouraged interpretations ranging from cautious art history to claims about secret religions, ancient astronomical knowledge or coded symbolism.

One particularly speculative proposal suggests that motifs on some tombstones might preserve a European memory of the supernova observed in 1054. The researchers involved present the carvings as possible evidence rather than proof, attempting to compare celestial designs with reconstructed historical skies. The hypothesis is intriguing but should not be presented as a settled reading of the stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

This is a useful example of how archaeological anomalies are created. The objects themselves are authentic; the uncertainty lies in interpretation. Repeated symbols do not automatically form a decipherable code, and later viewers may impose astronomical or esoteric systems that the makers never intended. For curiosity travellers, the rewarding approach is to enjoy the strange imagery while separating documented archaeological context from modern speculation.

## Smaller curiosities beyond the main museums

Not every unusual Bosnian collection occupies a formal gallery. Trebinje preserves a rare Austro-Hungarian pigeon post office, now associated with the Museum of Herzegovina. Built for military communications, it once housed messenger pigeons linking the area with wider administrative networks. The birds have gone, but the restored structure survives as a monument to an obsolete communications system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/beyond-the-beyond/2019/08/dead-media-beat-pigeon-post-office-building-standing-bosnia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dead Media Beat: a pigeon post-office building standing in Bosnia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dead Media Beat: a pigeon post-office building standing in Bosnia</span></span></span>

Its appeal is modest but genuine: it is an architectural remnant of “dead media”, preserving the physical infrastructure of a technology that has almost vanished from public memory. Unlike Tito’s bunker, it was not designed for nuclear command; unlike the Sarajevo tunnel, it did not arise from emergency improvisation. It represents the quieter oddity of a system that was once practical and is now difficult to imagine as routine.

Commercial attractions also exist, including illusion-based and immersive visitor experiences in Sarajevo. These can be entertaining, but they should be distinguished from collections with historic provenance. An optical-illusion attraction generally presents designed perceptual effects rather than preserving Bosnian scientific instruments, folk magic or historical anomalies. It belongs to tourist entertainment more than to the country’s museum heritage.

## What Bosnia and Herzegovina does not currently offer

Reliable public evidence for a permanent Bosnian museum devoted specifically to witchcraft, occult practice, cryptids, UFOs, spiritualism, medical deformities or criminal curiosities is sparse. Individual ethnographic collections may contain amulets, ritual objects, folk beliefs or protective practices, but they are usually embedded within broader displays of domestic life, religion and regional custom rather than framed as museums of the supernatural.

That absence should not be filled with invented attractions or inflated claims. Bosnia and Herzegovina has abundant folklore, sacred landscapes and stories of supernatural beings, but folklore traditions are not automatically represented by dedicated museums. Visitors searching for a “Bosnian monster museum” or “Sarajevo witchcraft museum” should be wary of recycled travel listings, temporary exhibitions and attractions whose names have been mistranslated or exaggerated.

The stronger route into the country’s uncanny heritage is through ethnology, religious collections and place-based storytelling. Protective objects, legends, funerary customs and beliefs about illness or misfortune are more likely to appear within regional museums, monastery treasuries and historic houses than in a single specialist institution. This fragmented model is less immediately marketable, but it more accurately reflects how unusual beliefs have been preserved.

Bosnia and Herzegovina’s unusual sites reward visitors who ask what kind of authority each institution possesses. The following distinctions are especially useful:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Preserved historic environment:** ARK D-0 and the Tunnel of Hope are powerful because the structure itself survives. Interpretation may change, but the physical setting provides unusually direct evidence.
* **Testimony-based collection:** The War Childhood Museum depends on the relationship between donated objects and personal accounts. The story is part of the artefact’s meaning.
* **Academic museum:** The National Museum uses professional conservation and archaeological or art-historical research, although individual objects such as the Sarajevo Haggadah also carry powerful public symbolism.
* **Religious treasury:** Franciscan and Orthodox collections preserve authentic historical material, but displays may combine scholarly description with devotional or communal interpretation.
* **Living pilgrimage culture:** Medjugorje is public, commercial and religious at once. Its importance is undeniable, while the supernatural explanation remains unconfirmed.
* **Speculative interpretation:** Claims about coded tombstone imagery or other archaeological mysteries should be presented as hypotheses unless supported by a broad scholarly consensus.

</div>

These categories can overlap. Tito’s bunker is simultaneously military heritage, an art venue and a tourist attraction. Fojnica is a monastery, archive, museum and symbol of communal continuity. Medjugorje is both a place of prayer and an international souvenir economy. The overlap is not a flaw; it is one of the defining features of the country’s curiosity landscape.

## Why these collections matter

Bosnia and Herzegovina’s strangest museums are not united by monsters, magic or theatrical horror. They are united by preservation under pressure. A manuscript survives expulsion and war. A monastery archive safeguards documents across changing empires. A nuclear shelter outlives the political system that commissioned it. A child’s toy becomes evidence of life under siege. A pilgrimage town develops around claims that remain neither formally authenticated nor simply dismissed.

This gives the country a distinctive place in European curiosity culture. Its unusual collections are often serious, emotionally demanding and politically layered. They ask visitors to examine who was expected to survive, whose memories enter museums, which sacred claims receive institutional approval, and how ordinary objects become symbols after catastrophe.

For the offbeat traveller, the most memorable itinerary is therefore not a conventional list of “weird museums”. It is a route through different forms of belief and evidence: from the engineered paranoia of ARK D-0, through Sarajevo’s intimate records of siege and childhood, to the guarded Haggadah, monastery treasuries and the unresolved devotional landscape of Medjugorje. Together they show a country where curiosity is inseparable from secrecy, survival and the contested ownership of the past.
