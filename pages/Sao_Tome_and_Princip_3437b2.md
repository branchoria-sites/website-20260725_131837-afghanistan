## Introduction

The country’s curiosity culture therefore lies less in spectacular “weird museums” than in unexpected objects and settings: human remains reportedly kept beneath a planter’s portrait, graphic images of a colonial massacre, religious statuary displaced after independence, abandoned plantation hospitals, obsolete processing equipment and turtle displays linked to night-time nesting beaches. Some of these places are formal public museums; others are conservation centres, hotel collections or preserved industrial landscapes whose interpretation can vary greatly.

## The fortress museum where the country confronts its past

The National Museum of São Tomé and Príncipe occupies São Sebastião Fort on the waterfront of São Tomé city. Portuguese authorities built the fort in the sixteenth century to protect the harbour, and it later acquired a lighthouse. The museum opened shortly after independence, in 1976, bringing together religious art, administrative records, plantation objects, photographs and material gathered from state buildings and former agricultural estates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forte_de_S%C3%A3o_Sebasti%C3%A3o_%28S%C3%A3o_Tom%C3%A9%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forte de São Sebastião (São Tomé</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forte de São Sebastião (São Tomé</span></span></span>

The building is a major part of the experience. Cannons, thick defensive walls and Atlantic-facing bastions create an unmistakably colonial setting, but the interpretation inside does not simply celebrate imperial architecture. Displays address slavery, plantation labour and the political transition from Portuguese rule. Former colonial statues, including figures associated with the European discovery of the islands, were removed from prominent public spaces after independence and placed near or within the museum setting. Their relocation effectively changed them from monuments of authority into historical objects open to reinterpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forte_de_S%C3%A3o_Sebasti%C3%A3o_%28S%C3%A3o_Tom%C3%A9%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forte de São Sebastião (São Tomé</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forte de São Sebastião (São Tomé</span></span></span>

Religious material forms another substantial strand. Catholic sculptures, paintings and church objects reflect the institutional power of Christianity under Portuguese rule, but they can also be read as evidence of the islands’ complex cultural mixing. These are not presented as an occult collection, and visitors should not expect a museum of African magic or witchcraft. Their unusual quality comes instead from the concentration of devotional objects within a military fort and from the way sacred art, plantation history and national memory occupy the same relatively small institution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A3o_Sebasti%C3%A3o_Museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">São Sebastião Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">São Sebastião Museum</span></span></span>

### Photographs of the Batepá massacre

The museum’s most difficult material concerns the Batepá massacre of February 1953, when Portuguese colonial authorities and associated forces killed large numbers of São Toméans during a campaign of arrests, beatings, torture and forced labour. The precise number of victims remains disputed, but the event became central to the country’s anti-colonial memory. Historical research records that photographs of mutilated bodies were subsequently displayed in a room of the national museum. Recent visitor accounts also warn that some imagery can be extremely graphic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museudoaljube.pt/en/2023/02/03/batepa-massacre/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museudoaljube.pt">[museudoaljube.pt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museudoaljube.pt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">batepa massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">batepa massacre</span></span></span>

This is not a conventional crime exhibition designed for thrills. The photographs operate as political evidence and memorial material, challenging earlier colonial claims of racial harmony. They make the museum unusually direct for a small national institution, but their display also raises familiar questions about dignity, warning signs and the treatment of images of dead victims. Families travelling with children should ask staff or a guide about the route through the galleries before entering sensitive rooms.

### The planter’s remains

One of the strangest frequently reported objects in São Sebastião Fort is a box said to contain the remains of a wealthy cocoa planter, positioned beneath or near his portrait. The detail appears in local tourism descriptions, but it is poorly documented in accessible institutional catalogues. It should therefore be treated as a reported museum curiosity rather than an object whose identity, provenance and display history are securely established in published scholarship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitsaotome.com/atelier-galleries/forte-de-sao-sebastiao.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsaotome.com">[visitsaotome.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsaotome.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forte de São Sebastião</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forte de São Sebastião</span></span></span>

Even with that caution, the display is revealing. São Tomé became profoundly shaped by large cocoa and coffee estates whose owners accumulated immense wealth while relying on coerced or harshly controlled labour systems. A planter’s bodily remains, if correctly identified, would turn an abstract history of plantation power into an uncomfortable physical presence. It is precisely the kind of object that needs strong labelling: who the person was, why the remains are held there, how they were authenticated and why continued display is considered appropriate.

## Plantations as open-air collections

The country’s former plantations, generally called estates, are among its most distinctive heritage environments. Around the beginning of the twentieth century, São Tomé and Príncipe contained roughly 150 such complexes. They were not merely fields and manor houses but self-contained settlements with workers’ accommodation, warehouses, rail systems, workshops, chapels and hospitals. After independence, many declined or were partially abandoned, leaving machinery, medical buildings and domestic architecture to decay in the tropical landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bradtguides.com/a-world-of-rocas-historic-plantations-on-sao-tome-principe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bradtguides.com">[Bradt Guides]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bradtguides.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bradt Guides A world of roças: historic plantations on São Tomé &amp; Príncipe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bradt Guides A world of roças: historic plantations on São Tomé &amp; Príncipe</span></span></span>

These sites are not all museums, and it would be misleading to describe every ruined estate as a curated attraction. Their value lies in the survival of entire industrial and social landscapes. At places such as Água Izé and Agostinho Neto, visitors may encounter immense processing buildings, rails, drying areas and the ruins of plantation hospitals. The atmosphere can feel uncanny because the estates preserve the scale of a vanished economic system while remaining inhabited, cultivated or reclaimed by vegetation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kanaga-at.com/en/trip-info/sao-tome-and-principe/the-ancient-coffee-and-cocoa-plantations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kanaga-at.com">[Kanaga Africa Tours]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kanaga-at.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kanaga-at.com.</span></span></span>

Interpretation is uneven. Some tours focus heavily on architecture, chocolate tasting or photogenic decay while giving limited attention to forced labour and inequality. A responsible visit should connect machinery and manor houses with the people who planted, harvested, transported and processed the crops. The plantations matter within São Tomé and Príncipe’s strange-history landscape because they resemble abandoned company towns: ambitious experiments in controlling labour, production, housing, health and movement within one enclosed system.

### Monte Café and the machinery of coffee

Monte Café contains a small coffee museum or visitor display associated with one of the country’s best-known historic estates. Accounts describe old processing equipment, explanations of coffee and cocoa production, and tastings. The attraction is more industrial than eccentric, but its collection of obsolete machinery shows how raw beans were transformed within a plantation economy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.com/ShowUserReviews-g294442-d20365586-r919498288-Roca_Monte_Cafe-Sao_Tome_Sao_Tome_Island.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.com">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Huge, Old Coffee Plantation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Huge, Old Coffee Plantation</span></span></span>

Visitors should distinguish the historical estate from a polished European-style industrial museum. Information, opening arrangements and the availability of guided visits may change. The strongest experience comes from seeing machines, cultivation and surviving plantation buildings together rather than treating the visit solely as a tasting stop.

## Turtle museums and community conservation

Some of the country’s most unusual modern collections are devoted to marine turtles. On Príncipe, the Kaxí Tetuga Museum was created with conservation organisations working to protect the marine species found around the island, including the five turtle species recorded in Príncipe’s waters. It functions as an interpretation centre rather than a cabinet of rare trophies: displays introduce turtle biology, threats, nesting and conservation work. Turismo São Tomé e Príncipe<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.turismostp.com/listagem/museu-biodiversidade-marinha" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: turismostp.com">[turismostp.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">turismostp.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Foi criado por um conjunto de ONGs que trabalham para suportar a conservação das espécies marinhas nomeadamente as 5 espécies de tartarug...</span></span></span>

Its setting gives the museum particular force. Visits can be connected to guided observation of nesting turtles or hatchlings, subject to season and conservation rules. Tourism income has also been presented as a way of supporting local communities and demonstrating that living turtles have greater long-term value than killed animals. This makes the collection part museum, part environmental campaign and part community tourism project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.programatato.org/en/turtle-watching" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: programatato.org">[Programa Tatô]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">programatato.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on programatato.org.</span></span></span>

The unusual object here is ultimately the living animal beyond the display case. Specimens, models, shells or educational graphics are used to prepare visitors for encounters on carefully managed beaches. That relationship between exhibition and field observation distinguishes Kaxí Tetuga from a conventional natural-history gallery.

## Fishing knowledge preserved in a waterfront hut

At Morro Peixe on northern São Tomé, the Museum of the Sea and Artisanal Fishing occupies a modest waterfront structure associated with the local fishing community. It developed from an earlier turtle-conservation centre and presents marine biodiversity alongside the tools, knowledge and practices of small-scale fishing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lonelyplanet.com/points-of-interest/museo-do-mar-e-da-pesca-artesanal/1557200" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lonelyplanet.com">[lonelyplanet.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lonelyplanet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lonelyplanet.com.</span></span></span>

Its significance comes from who holds the knowledge. Rather than presenting fishing solely through formal scientific classification, the museum draws on community experience of boats, species, coastal conditions and working life. This gives everyday equipment the status of a collection: nets, lines, models, photographs and locally made objects become records of a maritime culture that is usually practised rather than archived.

The museum is also an environmental education space. Conservation organisations in São Tomé and Príncipe work with fishing communities because marine protection cannot be separated from livelihoods. Current visitor information indicates that advance contact may be necessary, so travellers should not assume regular urban-museum opening hours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.pt/Attraction_Review-g13341631-d9454299-Reviews-Museu_do_Mar_e_da_Pesca_Artesanal-Morro_Peixe_Sao_Tome_Island.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.pt">[tripadvisor.pt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.pt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museu do Mar e da Pesca Artesanal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museu do Mar e da Pesca Artesanal</span></span></span>

## Príncipe’s private natural-history collection

Roça Belo Monte on Príncipe combines a restored plantation property, hotel and private museum. The museum concentrates on the island’s biodiversity and cultural history, placing endemic wildlife within the broader story of the estate and the surrounding protected landscape. Príncipe’s entire emerged land area and associated islets form a UNESCO biosphere reserve, giving the collection a scientific and conservation context that extends well beyond hotel decoration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://principe.gov.st/en/autonomous-region-of-principe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: principe.gov.st">[principe.gov.st]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">principe.gov.st</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on principe.gov.st.</span></span></span>

This is a tourist-led private collection rather than a national institution. Its presentation is likely to be more polished than many small public museums, but access may be connected to hotel arrangements or guided activities. The plantation setting also demands careful reading. Restored elegance and wildlife conservation can coexist with a history of colonial extraction; one should not be allowed to erase the other.

Nearby estate landscapes retain drying sheds, bells and old agricultural machinery. These surviving structures make Príncipe’s natural-history tourism unusually layered: endemic birds and turtles are interpreted within buildings created for a plantation economy that transformed the same environment.

## Living traditions without display cases

Not all of the country’s most curious heritage can be contained in a museum. Tchiloli, a theatrical tradition performed on São Tomé, adapts a European story involving Charlemagne, betrayal, death and a legal dispute into an extended local performance combining music, movement, elaborate costume and African theatrical practice. UNESCO added it to the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2025, following its inclusion in the national inventory in 2024.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ich.unesco.org/en/video/76583" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ich.unesco.org">[Intangible Cultural Heritage UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ich.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

For a visitor interested in masks, ritual dress or strange theatrical collections, Tchiloli may sound like material for a folklore museum. Yet its real significance lies in continued performance rather than static costume display. The clothes, texts, roles and musical practices belong to participating groups and communities. Removing them into cases would preserve objects but risk losing the lengthy social drama that gives them meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/display/book/9789004710511/BP000008.pdf?srsltid=AfmBOopye0XIRtn29uoLaek8Po_P0RDPxWsIj3MnAu6RiApZIcVuu3ql" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on brill.com.</span></span></span>

This distinction helps explain São Tomé and Príncipe’s wider museum landscape. Much of its distinctive heritage survives through performance, community memory, working agriculture, fishing knowledge and conservation activity. Formal collecting exists, but it is only one part of how the islands preserve unusual histories.

## What visitors should expect

São Tomé and Príncipe rewards travellers who approach its collections as small, locally shaped institutions rather than fully standardised attractions. Displays may have limited English interpretation, guides can be crucial, and opening times or access arrangements may change. Community museums and private collections may require advance contact, while plantation sites range from curated estates to inhabited ruins with no formal exhibition structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.co.uk/Attraction_Review-g294442-d6620761-Reviews-Museu_Nacional_De_Sao_Tome_Principe-Sao_Tome_Sao_Tome_Island.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.co.uk">[tripadvisor.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tripadvisor.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The most credible and worthwhile curiosities are those anchored in documented history or active conservation: the National Museum’s colonial and independence collections, the photographic memory of Batepá, surviving plantation machinery, community fishing displays and the turtle interpretation centres. More dramatic claims, such as the identity of bodily remains or poorly labelled relics, should be treated cautiously unless the museum provides provenance.

What makes the country distinctive is not an abundance of novelty museums. It is the closeness between objects and the landscapes that produced them. A cannon still faces the harbour it once defended; plantation machines remain near the fields they served; turtle displays lead to nesting beaches; and a medieval European drama survives as an African island tradition. São Tomé and Príncipe’s strangest collections are compelling because they have not been fully separated from living memory, political argument or place.
