## Introduction

Afro-Cuban sacred collections occupy a distinctive place within Cuba's unusual heritage. They are not simply displays of old religious objects but museums and exhibitions that stand alongside living spiritual traditions still practised across the island. Visitors encounter carved figures, ceremonial vessels, ritual drums, clothing and altars that explain Afro-Cuban religions, yet many of these same objects remain active symbols in contemporary ceremonies rather than relics of a vanished past. This creates an unusual balance between public interpretation and ongoing devotion, making Cuba's sacred collections unlike conventional ethnographic museums. Rather than presenting belief as something safely confined to history, they reveal how religion, community identity and cultural memory continue to shape everyday Cuban life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.slaveryandremembrance.org/partners/partner/index.cfm?id=P0079" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slaveryandremembrance.org">[slaveryandremembrance.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slaveryandremembrance.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Slavery and Remembrance Municipal Museum of Guanabacoa &#124; Slavery and Remembrance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Slavery and Remembrance Municipal Museum of Guanabacoa &#124; Slavery and Remembrance</span></span></span>

## When sacred objects become museum collections

Many museums display religions that no longer have active communities behind them. Cuba's Afro-Cuban collections are different because they represent traditions that remain woven into neighbourhood life. Regla de Ocha (often internationally known as Santería), Ifá, Palo and other Afro-Cuban religious traditions continue to be practised by thousands of Cubans from diverse social backgrounds, often alongside Catholicism rather than in opposition to it. Ceremonies usually take place in private homes rather than monumental temples, making museums one of the few places where visitors can gain structured public access to the material culture surrounding these beliefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnh.org/exhibitions/cuba/afro-cuban-religion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnh.org">[American Museum of Natural History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnh.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Museum of Natural History Exhibition: Afro-Cuban Religion &#124; AMNH</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Museum of Natural History Exhibition: Afro-Cuban Religion &#124; AMNH</span></span></span>

This creates a recurring challenge for curators. Ritual objects can be interpreted as historical artefacts, works of art or ethnographic specimens, but they may simultaneously retain sacred meaning for practitioners. Museums therefore occupy a space between education and respect, where the purpose is less to display "exotic" religion than to explain traditions that remain part of modern Cuban society.

## The Museum of the Orishas and its monumental figures

One of Havana's most unusual visitor attractions is the Museum of the Orishas, whose galleries are dominated by large, vividly coloured sculptures representing the orishas, the powerful spiritual beings central to the Yoruba-derived religious tradition in Cuba. Rather than presenting small archaeological finds in display cases, the museum immerses visitors among monumental figures accompanied by explanatory panels describing their symbolic attributes, colours and associations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.co.uk/Attraction_Review-g147271-d5983893-Reviews-Museo_de_los_Orishas-Havana_Ciudad_de_la_Habana_Province_Cuba.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.co.uk">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MUSEO DE LOS ORISHAS (2026) All You SHOULD Know Before You Go (w/ Reviews)...</span></span></span>

For many international visitors, the museum provides a first introduction to a belief system that can otherwise seem difficult to approach. The sculptures are intentionally theatrical, yet they represent living religious personalities rather than fictional mythological characters. Visitors are generally encouraged to understand the symbolism rather than treat the figures as curiosities.

The museum illustrates an important distinction. Although the objects are displayed publicly, they are not functioning shrines. Their purpose is educational and cultural, helping explain traditions whose ceremonies normally occur in private domestic settings. This separation allows non-practitioners to learn about Afro-Cuban religion without intruding upon active rituals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnh.org/exhibitions/cuba/afro-cuban-religion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnh.org">[American Museum of Natural History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnh.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Museum of Natural History Exhibition: Afro-Cuban Religion &#124; AMNH</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Museum of Natural History Exhibition: Afro-Cuban Religion &#124; AMNH</span></span></span>

## Guanabacoa: where community history shapes the collection

If one place demonstrates the close relationship between museum collections and living religion, it is Guanabacoa on the eastern side of Havana. Long recognised as one of Cuba's major centres of Afro-Cuban religious life, the municipality combines historical importance with continuing spiritual practice.

The Municipal Museum of Guanabacoa was established in 1964 and became Cuba's pioneering museum dedicated to religious ethnology. Its collections preserve ritual objects connected with major Afro-Cuban traditions, including ceremonial costumes, musical instruments, offerings and representations associated with different religious systems of African origin. Rather than isolating individual spectacular artefacts, the museum explains how these traditions developed through slavery, forced conversion, cultural adaptation and the blending of African and Catholic practices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.slaveryandremembrance.org/partners/partner/index.cfm?id=P0079" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slaveryandremembrance.org">[Slavery and Remembrance]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slaveryandremembrance.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Slavery and Remembrance Municipal Museum of Guanabacoa &#124; Slavery and Remembrance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Slavery and Remembrance Municipal Museum of Guanabacoa &#124; Slavery and Remembrance</span></span></span>

The museum deliberately recreates aspects of domestic ritual environments. Exhibition spaces include examples of household altars, ceremonial furniture and initiation settings that illustrate how worship occurs within everyday homes rather than separate temples. By reconstructing complete ritual contexts instead of displaying disconnected objects, the museum helps visitors understand how sacred collections function within living communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museoafrocuba.wordpress.com/salas-de-exposicion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museoafrocuba.wordpress.com">[Museo Municipal de Guanabacoa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museoafrocuba.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo Municipal de GuanabacoaSalas de exposición – Museo Municipal de GuanabacoaDecember 15, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2017</span></span></span>

## Museums, ceremonies and the limits of exoticism

Afro-Cuban collections present an interpretive challenge that many museums around the world have struggled with. Religious objects can easily be marketed as mysterious, magical or occult curiosities for tourists. Cuban museums generally attempt a different approach by placing ritual objects within historical, cultural and community contexts rather than presenting them as supernatural attractions.

This does not mean every visitor arrives without preconceptions. International tourism has long associated Afro-Cuban religion with fortune-telling, magic or sensational stories. Museum interpretation increasingly seeks to correct these stereotypes by emphasising that these are organised religious traditions with established ritual practices, ethical systems and lineages rather than collections of magical props.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.slaveryandremembrance.org/partners/partner/index.cfm?id=P0079" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slaveryandremembrance.org">[Slavery and Remembrance]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slaveryandremembrance.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Slavery and Remembrance Municipal Museum of Guanabacoa &#124; Slavery and Remembrance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Slavery and Remembrance Municipal Museum of Guanabacoa &#124; Slavery and Remembrance</span></span></span>

The distinction matters because many practitioners continue to visit museums while also participating in ceremonies elsewhere. Objects displayed behind glass may resemble those still used in active rituals, but museum examples have been transferred into a heritage setting where their educational role differs from their devotional purpose.

## Why these collections matter within Cuba's curiosity landscape

Among Cuba's unusual collections, Afro-Cuban sacred museums stand apart because they preserve something more complex than rare artefacts. They preserve relationships between objects, memory, identity and belief.

The country's preserved pharmacy museums, prisons and historical collections document past ways of life. Afro-Cuban sacred collections document traditions that have never entirely become history. Their exhibits show how objects can move between household ritual, community identity and public heritage while remaining culturally significant.

That makes these museums especially valuable for understanding Cuba itself. They reveal how African cultural traditions survived enslavement, adapted through centuries of social change and continue to influence music, art, language and everyday religious life.
