## Introduction

That absence does not mean South Sudan lacks curiosity culture. It means that the most compelling “collection” is fragmented between an experimental travelling museum, stored objects and archives in Juba, community memories, politically charged sacred relics and large overseas ethnographic holdings. For visitors and armchair travellers, South Sudan is therefore best understood not as a destination full of eccentric museums, but as a country where the ownership, meaning and visibility of unusual objects remain unusually unsettled.

## Is there a national museum to visit?

There is no firmly established, conventional national museum comparable to those in many neighbouring states. Museums serving the former unified Sudan were concentrated in Khartoum, while prolonged warfare and recurring emergencies obstructed museum building and heritage conservation in the south. South Sudanese heritage specialists have consequently debated not merely what a national museum should contain, but whether a central institution in Juba would feel relevant to people living far from the capital.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://southsudanmuseumnetwork.files.wordpress.com/2018/12/ssmn-juba-workshop-report-2018.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southsudanmuseumnetwork.files.wordpress.com">[southsudanmuseumnetwork.files.wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southsudanmuseumnetwork.files.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ssmn juba workshop report 2018</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ssmn juba workshop report 2018</span></span></span>

A national museum initiative did begin in 2014, but in an unusual form. UNESCO and South Sudanese cultural authorities developed a travelling, community-based exhibition intended to gather stories rather than simply remove impressive objects from villages. Its material was to include life histories, photographs, audio and video recordings, and contributed objects. The exhibition was planned to travel through several states so that communities could discuss how they wished to be represented in any future museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/communities-south-sudan-build-their-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">communities south sudan build their national museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Communities of South Sudan build their National Museum19 Jun 2014 — The future National Museum will be a place where the people of...</span></span></span>

This made the project closer to a moving consultation room, oral-history archive and provisional museum than to a building full of labelled cases. That distinction matters. Reliable, current visitor information for a permanent national museum remains scarce. Travellers should not assume that references to the “South Sudan National Museum” describe an ordinary attraction with fixed galleries, advertised opening hours and a stable public collection. The best-documented version remains the participatory travelling initiative and the wider, unfinished ambition to create a national institution.

## The hidden collection in Juba

A group of material objects held in Juba has been described as a potential foundation for a future national museum. Reported holdings include spears, headrests, grave posts, paintings and musical instruments. Their importance lies not in conventional rarity alone, but in their ability to represent domestic life, artistic traditions, warfare, spiritual authority and commemoration across South Sudan’s many communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://riftvalley.net/news/sudan-and-south-sudan/south-sudan-diaspora-objects-and-future-tradition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: riftvalley.net">[riftvalley.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">riftvalley.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nts is intended to form the core of South Sudan&#x27;s national museum...</span></span></span>

These objects are not known as a dependable walk-in attraction. They should be thought of as a stored national collection in waiting: culturally important, institutionally significant and potentially fascinating, but not equivalent to a fully interpreted public museum. Documentation also warns against assuming that an object has one simple ethnic or functional identity. A spear might be a weapon, prestige object, performance prop, inheritance or ritual instrument depending on its history and setting.

The collection’s grave posts are especially relevant to strange-museum enthusiasts. Monumental wooden figures made by Bongo communities in western South Sudan were erected around the graves of notable people. Some examples took simplified human form, while other carved posts recorded animals killed by the deceased, turning a burial place into a sculptural account of reputation and achievement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/309909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Bongo artist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Bongo artist</span></span></span>

Surviving grave figures are now scattered. The British Museum holds a nearly life-sized wooden female figure dated broadly to between 1880 and 1920, while the Metropolitan Museum of Art and Pitt Rivers Museum document related funerary sculpture and photography. These objects may look uncanny when isolated in Western galleries, but they were not originally made as generic “tribal curiosities”. They belonged to specific memorial landscapes and relationships with the dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/309909" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Bongo artist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Bongo artist</span></span></span>

## The prophet’s rod: sacred relic or political trophy?

The most striking individual curiosity associated with modern South Sudan is the ceremonial rod of the Nuer prophet Ngundeng Bong. Usually called the prophet’s rod in English-language accounts, it combines the qualities of sacred relic, anti-colonial trophy, political symbol and disputed heritage object. It was taken during the colonial era, later found in private hands in Britain and returned to southern Sudan in 2009 by historian Douglas Johnson.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://riftvalley.net/news/sudan-and-south-sudan/fate-ngungdengs-dang/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: riftvalley.net">[riftvalley.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">riftvalley.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on riftvalley.net.</span></span></span>

Its return was not staged as an ordinary museum acquisition. It was received in Juba by Riek Machar, then vice-president, amid public celebration and ritual. Accounts describe an ox being slaughtered and the rod being displayed before political and community leaders. Rather than entering a transparent public collection, it was reportedly kept by Machar and disappeared from ordinary public view. Its precise current whereabouts have remained unclear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://riftvalley.net/news/sudan-and-south-sudan/fate-ngungdengs-dang/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: riftvalley.net">[riftvalley.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">riftvalley.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on riftvalley.net.</span></span></span>

Beliefs surrounding the rod are inseparable from Ngundeng’s reputation as a prophet credited by followers with supernatural powers and predictions. Modern political narratives have linked the relic to claims that a destined leader would fulfil Ngundeng’s prophecies. Such interpretations are matters of belief and political mobilisation, not verifiable evidence that the object possesses magical powers. Yet the belief itself is historically consequential: a sacred object can shape legitimacy, allegiance and fear whether or not its supernatural reputation is accepted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://martinplaut.com/2014/03/06/south-sudan-riek-machar-and-the-prophets-rod/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinplaut.com">[Martin Plaut]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinplaut.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Martin Plaut South Sudan: Riek Machar and the prophet&#x27;s rod</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Martin Plaut South Sudan: Riek Machar and the prophet&#x27;s rod</span></span></span>

For a museum, the rod would be extraordinarily difficult to display responsibly. Presenting it merely as an ethnographic artefact would flatten its spiritual meaning. Marketing it as a magical object would sensationalise living belief. Treating it only as political propaganda would ignore its older religious history. It is a vivid example of why South Sudan’s unusual heritage cannot always be placed safely behind glass.

## The national archive as a cabinet of government memory

The [National Archives]({{ 'national-archives/' | relative_url }}) of South Sudan is not an oddity museum, but it may be the country’s most important surviving collection. Its records span colonial administration, Sudanese independence, regional government and successive civil wars. They include district reports, official correspondence, handover notes, inspection records, administrative diaries and other documents that are sometimes the only detailed evidence of local government activity in particular areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://riftvalley.net/projects/sudan-and-south-sudan/national-archive-south-sudan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: riftvalley.net">[riftvalley.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">riftvalley.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">national archive south sudan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">national archive south sudan</span></span></span>

The archive has itself had an unusually precarious history. Records were dispersed, exposed to heat, flooding, insects and neglect, and in some cases destroyed during conflict. Emergency conservation after 2005 involved gathering papers from scattered storage locations, sorting them and placing them in archival boxes. Later projects trained local staff and digitised vulnerable material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/south-sudan-archives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">south sudan archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">south sudan archives</span></span></span>

Almost half of the documentary holdings have reportedly been digitised through the South Sudan National Archives programme. Online catalogues and the Sudan Memory platform provide a partial route into material that would otherwise be difficult for the public to see. The result is a form of digital curiosity cabinet: not a collection of monsters or occult devices, but a mass of fragile paperwork through which vanished districts, officials, disputes and everyday colonial assumptions become visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sudanmemory.org/cms/102/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sudanmemory.org">[sudanmemory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sudanmemory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sudanmemory.org.</span></span></span>

Access for ordinary visitors should not be taken for granted. National archives are working research institutions, and opening arrangements, permissions and physical conditions can change. Anyone hoping to consult the collection in Juba needs direct confirmation from the relevant ministry or archive staff rather than relying on tourist listings.

## South Sudan’s museums are largely overseas

The country’s largest accessible museum resource is outside its borders. The Pitt Rivers Museum in Oxford holds more than 1,300 artefacts and approximately 5,000 historic photographs from the former southern Sudan. Its dedicated online catalogue allows users to explore objects, collectors, locations and photographic records in far greater detail than is presently possible through a public museum in South Sudan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://southernsudan.prm.ox.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southernsudan.prm.ox.ac.uk">[southernsudan.prm.ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southernsudan.prm.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ox.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Other substantial holdings exist in institutions including the British Museum, Cambridge’s Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology, Durham University’s Sudan Archive, the Weltmuseum in Vienna and additional collections across Europe, Russia and beyond. A research network was established specifically because these holdings form a large but underused source for South Sudanese history, artistic practice and international connections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://southsudanmuseumnetwork.wordpress.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southsudanmuseumnetwork.wordpress.com">[wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southsudanmuseumnetwork.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

The strangest feature is not any single object but the geography of the collection. ## How credible are the old labels?

Historic ethnographic catalogues are valuable but not neutral. Many South Sudanese objects entered European collections during periods of slave trading, military conquest, missionary expansion and colonial administration. Some were bought or exchanged; others were taken under coercive circumstances or recorded so poorly that their acquisition history remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://riftvalley.net/news/sudan-and-south-sudan/south-sudan-diaspora-objects-and-future-tradition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: riftvalley.net">[riftvalley.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">riftvalley.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nts is intended to form the core of South Sudan&#x27;s national museum...</span></span></span>

Old museum descriptions frequently prioritised the collector rather than the maker. Individual African artists were often left unnamed, while objects were assigned broad ethnic labels that made cultures appear static and neatly bounded. Cambridge’s Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology notes how unusual it is for the maker of a nineteenth- or early twentieth-century African object to be recorded at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://maa.cam.ac.uk/sudan-and-south-sudan-objects-power-and-contested-power" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: maa.cam.ac.uk">[Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">maa.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cam.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Photographs also require caution. An image may preserve details of a sculpture, shrine or ceremony that no longer survives, but its caption may reflect the assumptions of an anthropologist or colonial official. A catalogue statement such as “ritual object” or “witch doctor’s equipment” should therefore not be repeated uncritically. Good contemporary interpretation asks who supplied the description, whether community knowledge supports it and how the object’s meaning may have changed.

This is particularly important for readers drawn to occult or supernatural material. South Sudanese prophetic objects, divination practices and memorial sculptures are not equivalents of European horror props. Their significance belongs to living religious, political and social histories. The intellectually interesting question is not “Did people really believe this strange thing?” but who regarded it as powerful, how that power was expressed and what happened when the object was collected or removed.

## What can a curious visitor realistically see?

South Sudan’s unusual collections are currently more accessible as a research trail than as a conventional tourist itinerary. A visitor should expect fragmented access and verify every institution directly.

**In Juba**, the National Archives is the most clearly documented heritage institution, although it is principally an archive rather than a museum. The stored object collection intended for a future national museum is culturally important but should not be assumed to be publicly displayed. References to the travelling national museum describe a community project, not necessarily a continuously operating exhibition with a permanent address.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sudanmemory.org/cms/102/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sudanmemory.org">[sudanmemory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sudanmemory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sudanmemory.org.</span></span></span>

**Online**, the Pitt Rivers Museum’s Southern Sudan catalogue provides the richest object-by-object experience. The South Sudan Museum Network also directs users towards collections in several countries, while the National Archives catalogue and Sudan Memory platform offer documentary material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://southsudanmuseumnetwork.wordpress.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southsudanmuseumnetwork.wordpress.com">[wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southsudanmuseumnetwork.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

**Outside South Sudan**, museums in Oxford, London, Cambridge, Durham and Vienna provide opportunities to see original objects, though not all South Sudanese holdings are on permanent display. Online database entries are often more dependable than general gallery descriptions because displays rotate and objects may remain in storage.

Current security and transport conditions must take priority over heritage tourism. Local access can be affected by political tension, institutional resources and sudden changes in public administration. Specialist travel advice and direct institutional confirmation are essential before attempting any visit.

## Why the absence matters

South Sudan’s strange-history landscape is defined by a paradox. The country possesses objects with exceptional stories—prophetic relics, monumental grave sculpture, colonial trophies, historic photographs and rescued archival papers—but lacks a stable public system for bringing them together. The resulting gaps are not empty spaces. They reveal how war, colonial collecting, political rivalry and limited infrastructure determine what becomes visible as “heritage”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789464260120.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sidestone.com">[sidestone.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sidestone.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sidestone.com.</span></span></span>

A future national museum could become far more than a storehouse. South Sudanese heritage discussions have imagined museums as places for education, inter-community dialogue, artistic experimentation and peacebuilding. At the same time, participants have warned that an encyclopaedic display dividing the population into fixed ethnic groups could reproduce colonial categories or deepen arguments over representation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://southsudanmuseumnetwork.files.wordpress.com/2018/12/ssmn-juba-workshop-report-2018.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southsudanmuseumnetwork.files.wordpress.com">[southsudanmuseumnetwork.files.wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southsudanmuseumnetwork.files.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ssmn juba workshop report 2018</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ssmn juba workshop report 2018</span></span></span>

The most promising model may therefore be distributed rather than monumental: regional displays, travelling exhibitions, digital access, community-controlled documentation and selective loans from overseas collections. Such an approach would fit South Sudan’s geography while allowing objects to be interpreted by the people whose histories they carry.

For enthusiasts of unusual museums, South Sudan offers no easy gallery of curiosities. It offers something rarer: an unfinished national collection whose most evocative objects remain scattered, contested, hidden, sacred or politically dangerous. The curiosity lies as much in their journeys and absences as in the things themselves.
