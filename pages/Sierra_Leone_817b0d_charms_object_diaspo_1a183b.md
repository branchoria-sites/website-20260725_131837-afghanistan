## Introduction

Many of [Sierra Leone]({{ 'where-sierra-leone-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s protective charms and ritual objects are no longer in the places where they were originally made, worn or understood. Instead, they are scattered across museums, universities and private collections in Britain and elsewhere, where they were acquired during the colonial period by administrators, missionaries, soldiers, traders and early anthropologists. This "object diaspora" is one of the country's most important heritage stories because it changes who has access to these objects, how they are interpreted, and who has authority to explain them.

For visitors interested in Sierra Leone's unusual collections, this is less a story about mysterious magical artefacts than about the movement of culturally significant objects across continents. Many collections now available through overseas museum databases preserve objects that would otherwise have been lost. At the same time, they also preserve colonial assumptions about religion, healing and everyday belief that modern researchers increasingly question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sierraleoneheritage.org/search/search_db" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierraleoneheritage.org">[Sierra Leone Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierraleoneheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sierraleoneheritage.org.</span></span></span>

## Protective objects were part of everyday life, not simply "magic"

Colonial catalogues frequently described Sierra Leonean artefacts as "charms", "fetishes", "medicine" or "superstitious objects". Those labels often compressed a wide variety of practices into a single category. In reality, protective objects served many different purposes.

Some were carried on the body to seek protection during travel, hunting or conflict. Others were associated with healing, family welfare or membership of particular communities. Some contained carefully selected natural materials, while others consisted of carved objects, leather packets or containers whose significance depended upon knowledge that was never intended for outsiders.

This matters because many museum records preserve only the collector's description. A catalogue entry reading simply "charm" may reveal almost nothing about how an object was actually used or understood within its original community. Modern scholarship increasingly treats these labels as historical evidence about colonial collecting practices rather than as complete descriptions of Sierra Leonean belief systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sierraleoneheritage.org/search/search_db" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierraleoneheritage.org">[Sierra Leone Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierraleoneheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sierraleoneheritage.org.</span></span></span>

## How colonial collectors classified unfamiliar things

During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, colonial officials frequently collected objects while travelling through Sierra Leone. Their priorities were often administrative or ethnographic rather than collaborative. Artefacts were removed because they appeared unusual, visually striking or representative of what collectors believed were "native customs".

Museum databases still preserve this history. The British Museum, for example, lists numerous Sierra Leonean objects under categories such as "charm", "medicine (protection)", "charm-holder" and related terms, many collected from Mende-speaking regions or other communities during the colonial era. These records often identify the place of acquisition and the collector but provide relatively little information about the object's cultural context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/x84289" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

The language used in older catalogues also reflects colonial assumptions. Words such as "fetish" once appeared routinely in museum documentation across West Africa but are now widely recognised as misleading because they imposed European ideas onto diverse African religious and social practices. Contemporary curators increasingly revise catalogue records to use more culturally specific descriptions where evidence allows.

## Why so many Sierra Leonean charms are overseas

Unlike monumental architecture, portable ritual objects were relatively easy to transport. Small protective packets, carved figures, medicine containers and related objects travelled with colonial officials returning to Britain, entered missionary collections or were donated to museums.

As a result, researchers today often discover more digitised examples of Sierra Leonean protective objects in overseas institutions than in public displays within Sierra Leone itself. Collections are spread across multiple institutions rather than concentrated in one location, making the heritage difficult to view as a coherent whole.

The object diaspora also reflects historical inequalities in collecting. Communities that created these objects rarely controlled how they were documented, interpreted or exhibited after they left Sierra Leone. Instead, much of the surviving written information comes from the collectors themselves, whose understanding was often incomplete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sierraleoneheritage.org/search/search_db" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierraleoneheritage.org">[Sierra Leone Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierraleoneheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sierraleoneheritage.org.</span></span></span>

## Digital access has changed the conversation

One important development is the rapid growth of online museum catalogues. Large institutions increasingly publish photographs, accession records and provenance information that can be viewed from anywhere.

For Sierra Leone, this has significant practical value. The Sierra Leone Heritage digital collections and similar online resources allow researchers, students and members of the public to compare objects held in different museums without travelling internationally. Digital catalogues also make it easier to identify relationships between objects that were separated decades ago through different collecting expeditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sierraleoneheritage.org/search/search_db" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierraleoneheritage.org">[Sierra Leone Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierraleoneheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sierraleoneheritage.org.</span></span></span>

Digital access, however, is not the same as cultural return. A high-resolution photograph cannot replace an object's ceremonial, historical or community significance. Online records also inherit many of the limitations of older catalogues, including uncertain dates, outdated terminology and incomplete provenance.

## Provenance, ownership and cultural return

The movement of Sierra Leonean ritual objects into overseas collections now raises broader questions shared across African museum heritage.

The central issues include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **How were objects acquired?** Some were purchased, others exchanged, donated or collected under colonial power relationships that are difficult to evaluate today.
* **How complete are the records?** Provenance documentation varies greatly, particularly for nineteenth-century collections.
* **Who should interpret the objects?** Increasingly, museums seek collaboration with scholars and communities connected to the collections rather than relying solely on historic catalogue descriptions.
* **Should objects be returned?** Repatriation debates differ from case to case. Some discussions focus on permanent return, others on long-term loans, shared custodianship, improved documentation or collaborative exhibitions.

</div>

Unlike internationally famous disputes over monumental bronzes or royal treasures, Sierra Leone's protective charms have generally attracted less public attention. Nevertheless, they raise many of the same ethical questions about consent, cultural authority and the legacy of colonial collecting.

## Why the object diaspora matters to Sierra Leone's curiosity culture

For anyone exploring Sierra Leone's unusual heritage, the fascination lies not in treating protective objects as mysterious relics of "magic", but in understanding how their meanings changed once they entered museums.

A charm that originally functioned within a living network of belief became an ethnographic specimen. A protective object worn in everyday life became an accession number in a catalogue. In some cases, the object's practical or spiritual purpose disappeared from the written record altogether, leaving only the collector's interpretation.

This transformation is itself part of Sierra Leone's strange museum history. The country's most intriguing collections are often divided between local museums and far-flung international institutions, creating a dispersed archive that reflects colonial history as much as the objects themselves. Understanding that journey helps visitors read museum labels more critically, recognising that the history of collecting can be as revealing as the artefacts on display.

    });

      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {

    });

    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }

      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;

    if (trigger && menu) {

        event.stopPropagation();

        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });

        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
