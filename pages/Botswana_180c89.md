## Where Botswana’s unusual collections are found

Botswana’s National Museum system has a broad remit covering cultural and natural heritage rather than a single narrow museum subject. Its work includes collecting, researching, conserving and exhibiting archaeological material, ethnographic objects, art and natural-history specimens. Government reporting has described the national holdings as exceeding 500,000 items across archaeology, art, ethnography, monuments and cultural science, with records preserved in formats ranging from physical objects and photographs to audio, film and print.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dailynews.gov.bw/common_up/dailynews/dailynews_pdf/04-03-2024_08-04-45_1709532285_dailynews_pdf.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dailynews.gov.bw">[Daily News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dailynews.gov.bw</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Daily News Tsogwane launches Leobo La Dingaka</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily NewsTsogwane launches Leobo La DingakaMarch 3, 2024 — 5 Mar 2024 — the national museum had acquired over 500 000 of heritage collec...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 3, 2024</span></span></span>

## Tsodilo Hills: rock art, archaeology and sacred landscape

Tsodilo Hills is Botswana’s clearest example of a vast open-air collection whose meaning cannot be reduced to the objects visible on a museum shelf. Rising sharply from the Kalahari in north-western Botswana, the hills contain around 400 rock-art sites and more than 4,000 individual paintings. UNESCO inscribed Tsodilo as a World Heritage Site in 2001, emphasising not only the concentration of images but also the site’s long association with settlement, belief, identity and spiritual continuity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/2289/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre UNESCO World Heritage Centre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Criterion (i): For many thousands of years the rocky outcrops of Tsodilo in the harsh landscape of the Kalahari Desert have been visited...</span></span></span>

Visitors encounter painted animals, human figures and geometric designs spread across rock shelters and exposed surfaces. The site is sometimes called the “Louvre of the Desert”, but that nickname can be misleading if it suggests a conventional art gallery. The paintings belong to a lived landscape rather than a neutral exhibition space. Paths, hills, caves and images carry meanings shaped by local histories and by the beliefs of communities associated with the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1021/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Tsodilo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Located in north-west Botswana near the Namibian Border in Okavango Sub-District, the Tsodilo Hills are a small area of massive quartzite...</span></span></span>

A small site museum near the entrance provides archaeological context through finds such as pottery, stone tools, spearheads and beads. These displays help explain that Tsodilo was not simply a place where pictures were made: it was repeatedly inhabited and visited over an immense period. The physical museum is modest; the real collection is dispersed across the hills.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yourbotswana.com/botswana-museums/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yourbotswana.com">[YourBotswana]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yourbotswana.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on yourbotswana.com.</span></span></span>

Tsodilo also demonstrates why interpretation matters. Rock art is vulnerable to erosion, mineral staining, water movement, sunlight and human contact. It should not be approached as an invitation to decode every figure as a monster, deity or astronomical message. Archaeology can establish material histories, but individual images may remain uncertain, and spiritual interpretations should not be separated from the communities for whom the place still carries meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/tsodilo-hills" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Tsodilo Hills, Botswana</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Tsodilo Hills, Botswana</span></span></span>

## Human remains, mummification and difficult museum ethics

Botswana’s archaeological collections include human remains accumulated through excavation, rescue archaeology and accidental discovery. A published review of the Botswana National Museum’s holdings described a collection that had grown over several decades, while also drawing attention to questions of documentation, storage, research practice and ethical management. These are scientific collections, not public cabinets of horror, and their significance lies partly in the responsibilities they impose on institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327579367_AN_INCIDENTAL_COLLECTION_OF_ARCHAEOLOGICAL_HUMAN_REMAINS_IN_BOTSWANA_HISTORY_AND_CURRENT_STATE" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The most striking individual case is the Tuli mummy, discovered in north-eastern Botswana in 2008. Patrol staff near the Tuli Block initially thought they had found the remains of an animal, but the find proved to be naturally mummified human remains. Researchers described it as Botswana’s first known and, at the time of publication, only mummified human body. The National Museum was informed and the remains became the subject of scientific investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/268393101_The_Tuli_mummy_A_preliminary_report_from_Northeastern_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The Tuli mummy is unusual because mummification is not commonly associated with Botswana’s climate or popular archaeological image. Yet it should not be treated as a local equivalent of a commercial mummy attraction. Its value is archaeological: the body offers evidence about preservation, health, ancestry, burial circumstances and the history of the region. Whether such remains should be displayed, studied, reburied or retained is an ethical question involving descendant communities, researchers, heritage law and cultural expectations.

Botswana’s legal and administrative treatment of graves and human remains has itself been criticised by researchers as fragmented across different statutes and authorities. This makes the collection important not only because of what scientists may learn from the dead, but because it exposes a broader question familiar to museums worldwide: who has the right to keep, examine and interpret a human body?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327579626_A_Review_of_Statutes_and_Administrative_Jurisdictions_on_Graves_Burial_Grounds_and_Human_Remains_in_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## “El Negro” and the afterlife of a colonial exhibit

One of Botswana’s most disturbing museum-related stories concerns the man long displayed in Spain under the dehumanising name “El Negro”. His body had been taken from southern Africa in the nineteenth century, preserved by taxidermists and exhibited for generations as an ethnographic specimen. Following an international campaign, his remains were transferred to Botswana in 2000 and buried at Tsholofelo Park in Gaborone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln06.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thuto.org.</span></span></span>

The burial was intended as an act of restoration and dignity, but the story did not end there. Questions arose about how much of the original body had actually been returned, whether associated material had been withheld and whether Botswana was the correct country of burial. Later historical research argued that the man may have come from what is now South Africa rather than Botswana.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373108196_El_Negro_Return_Presentation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) El Negro Return Presentation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) El Negro Return Presentation</span></span></span>

Tsholofelo Park is therefore not an oddity museum in the ordinary sense. It is a memorial site linked to the history of museums as institutions that once collected and displayed colonised people as racial “types”. The case matters because it turns the museum gaze back upon itself. The unsettling object is not the man’s body but the system that transformed a person into a spectacle, then struggled to repair the harm.

For visitors interested in unusual collections, this is one of Botswana’s most important stories precisely because it resists voyeurism. It connects the country to wider debates about repatriation, colonial collecting, incomplete provenance and the limits of symbolic restitution.

## Motopi Pan: a piece of asteroid history

Botswana also holds a remarkable scientific curiosity from beyond Earth. On 2 June 2018, the small asteroid 2018 LA entered the atmosphere over Botswana. It was only the second asteroid known at the time to have been detected in space before striking land. Observations allowed researchers to narrow the search area, and teams later recovered fragments named [Motopi Pan]({{ 'motopi-pan/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05997" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

Scientific analysis linked the meteorites to material associated with Vesta, one of the largest bodies in the asteroid belt. Researchers recovered more than 20 pieces during the main study, while later fieldwork found an additional fragment weighing about 92 grams. The meteorites are classified as a mixed type containing several related rock components formed through impact processes on their parent body.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05997" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arxiv.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The fragments were assigned to Botswana institutions for curation, including the Botswana National Museum and the Botswana Geoscience Institute. This is an unusual collection with unusually strong provenance: scientists observed the object before impact, reconstructed its flight, searched the predicted region and then analysed the recovered stones. Unlike a mystery meteorite with a doubtful sales history, Motopi Pan has a documented chain from asteroid to atmosphere to collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wits.ac.za/news/latest-news/research-news/2018/2018-06/fragment-of-impacting-asteroid-recovered-in-botswana.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wits.ac.za">[Wits University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wits.ac.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fragment of impacting asteroid recovered in botswana</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fragment of impacting asteroid recovered in botswana</span></span></span>

Public display arrangements may vary, so travellers should not assume the meteorites are always exhibited. Their importance nevertheless places Botswana within a very small group of countries possessing meteorites recovered from a pre-detected asteroid impact. In the country’s curiosity landscape, Motopi Pan is the closest thing to a genuine object from another world.

## Kobokwe’s Cave: witchcraft legend and religious transformation

Near Molepolole, Kobokwe’s Cave is associated with stories of witchcraft, execution and spiritual power. Local accounts commonly describe the cave and nearby cliffs as places where accused witches or sorcerers were killed. Other traditions connect the area with chiefly authority, ancestors and ritual consultation. These claims belong largely to folklore and oral history rather than to a securely documented judicial record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.facebook.com/BotswanaGovernment/posts/kobokwes-cave-where-a-witch-a-legendary-scottish-explorer-and-a-tribal-chief-uni/1988369377912377/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: facebook.com">[facebook.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">facebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on facebook.com.</span></span></span>

The cave is also linked to the nineteenth-century history of Christianity among the Bakwena. Its reputation as a dangerous or supernaturally charged place became part of stories about confrontation between older spiritual fears and Christian conversion. That layered history has helped turn it into an informal heritage destination rather than a curated witchcraft museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/kebokwes-cave" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Kebokwe&#x27;s Cave in Molepolole</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Kebokwe&#x27;s Cave in Molepolole</span></span></span>

Visitors should therefore separate three different kinds of claim:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Folklore:** stories of witches, spirits and supernatural danger.
* **Religious memory:** narratives in which the cave represents spiritual testing or conversion.
* **Physical history:** the cave, rock formations and surrounding landscape as an actual place used and reinterpreted over time.

</div>

The site is most rewarding when approached as a landscape of changing belief. Treating every legend as literal history strips away the more interesting question: why particular caves become repositories for fear, authority, conversion and memory.

## Kuru and the community museum model

At D’Kar in western Botswana, the Kuru cultural institutions offer a different form of unusual collection. The museum and associated art project preserve and present San history, craft, visual art and community knowledge. Rather than presenting San life as a vanished curiosity, the project has supported living artists and provided exhibition and sales space for contemporary work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://indiaoutbound.info/experiences/dkar-small-is-beautiful/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indiaoutbound.info">[indiaoutbound.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indiaoutbound.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">INDIA OUTBOUNDD&#x27;Kar in Botswana: Small is Beautiful</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">INDIA OUTBOUNDD&#x27;Kar in Botswana: Small is Beautiful</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. Older ethnographic museums often isolated indigenous objects from the people who made them, arranging tools, clothing and ritual items as evidence of supposedly static cultures. The Kuru model links cultural preservation with employment, artistic production and community development. Its collection may include objects that outsiders regard as exotic, but its deeper purpose is not to manufacture strangeness for tourists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumnext.com/article/combating-poverty-through-art-the-kalahari-indigenous-peoples/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumnext.com">[MuseumNext]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumnext.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">combating poverty through art the kalahari indigenous peoples</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">combating poverty through art the kalahari indigenous peoples</span></span></span>

The art project is especially relevant to Botswana’s curiosity culture because many works draw upon animals, memory, landscape, dreams and inherited stories without pretending to be archaeological survivals. Visitors encounter contemporary interpretation alongside heritage. This makes Kuru less a cabinet of old curiosities than a place where cultural memory is continually remade.

## What visitors can realistically expect

Botswana’s unusual heritage is geographically dispersed and institutionally mixed. Some material is displayed in formal museums; some remains in research collections; some is embedded in sacred or commemorative landscapes. A practical itinerary is therefore more likely to combine several types of place than to move between specialist oddity museums.

The most useful expectations are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Tsodilo Hills** offers the strongest visitor experience, combining a small museum, guided routes, archaeology, rock art and living spiritual significance.
* **The Botswana National Museum in Gaborone** is the principal institutional gateway to national archaeology, ethnography, art and natural history, but exhibitions and access can change.
* **Tsholofelo Park** is a memorial landscape connected to the repatriation controversy surrounding “El Negro”, not a conventional museum exhibit.
* **Kobokwe’s Cave** is an informal folklore and religious site whose supernatural stories should be treated as tradition rather than verified fact.
* **Kuru at D’Kar** connects community heritage with contemporary San art and economic activity.
* **Motopi Pan meteorites and archaeological human remains** are primarily curated scientific holdings; public display should be confirmed rather than assumed.

## Why Botswana’s curiosity culture matters

Botswana’s strange-collection landscape is compelling because it repeatedly unsettles the boundary between exhibit and place. At Tsodilo, the “collection” covers an inhabited sacred landscape. At Kobokwe’s Cave, folklore is preserved through retelling rather than display cases. The Tuli mummy and other human remains raise questions about scientific custody. Motopi Pan records a journey from the asteroid belt to a national collection. The burial of “El Negro” exposes the violence hidden inside older museum practices.

This is not a country where the unusual is chiefly commercialised through wax monsters, haunted props or fabricated mysteries. Its most distinctive curiosities sit at the intersection of archaeology, belief, colonial history, indigenous representation and science. That makes Botswana a rewarding destination for readers and travellers prepared to look beyond the conventional idea of a weird museum—and to ask not only what is being preserved, but who has the authority to explain it.
