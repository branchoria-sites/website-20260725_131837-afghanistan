## Introduction

Beneath Beirut's National Museum lies one of [Lebanon]({{ 'where-does-lebanon-keep-its-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s most unusual public archaeological spaces: an underground gallery devoted to death, burial and remembrance rather than kings, battles or monumental architecture. Reopened in 2016 after decades of closure, the basement brings together naturally preserved medieval mummies from the Qadisha Valley, Phoenician stone sarcophagi, reconstructed Roman tombs and thousands of years of funerary objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2016/10/14/national-museum-of-beirut-opens-basement-for-first-time-since-civil-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theartnewspaper.com">[Art Newspaper]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theartnewspaper.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Art NewspaperNational Museum of Beirut opens basement for first time since civil war - The Art Newspaper - International art news and eve...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lebanon_5caa7f_beirut_mummies_buria_e5b68e-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Mummies illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Lebanon_5caa7f_beirut_mummies_buria_e5b68e-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Lebanon_5caa7f_beirut_mummies_buria_e5b68e-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

What makes the galleries distinctive is not simply the presence of human remains. The displays explain how different communities across Lebanon prepared for death, honoured the dead and expressed religious belief. Equally striking is the museum itself. During the Lebanese Civil War it stood on Beirut's Green Line, was occupied by fighters, shelled repeatedly and almost destroyed. The survival of the collection has therefore become part of the story told beneath the museum, where archaeology and modern history meet.

## What Lies Beneath Beirut's National Museum?

The underground galleries cover funerary traditions from prehistory through the Ottoman period. Rather than presenting a chronological history of Lebanon, they focus on what burial practices reveal about changing beliefs, social status, family life and artistic traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2016/10/14/national-museum-of-beirut-opens-basement-for-first-time-since-civil-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theartnewspaper.com">[Art Newspaper]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theartnewspaper.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Art NewspaperNational Museum of Beirut opens basement for first time since civil war - The Art Newspaper - International art news and eve...</span></span></span>

Visitors move through a sequence of tomb reconstructions, burial goods, inscriptions and sarcophagi before reaching the museum's most discussed exhibits: naturally preserved medieval mummies discovered in northern Lebanon. The atmosphere is deliberately subdued, with restrained lighting and archaeological interpretation replacing sensational presentation. Human remains are displayed as historical evidence rather than curiosities, emphasising the lives of ordinary people rather than famous rulers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenationalnews.com/arts-culture/surviving-the-test-of-time-national-museum-of-beirut-opens-its-treasure-trove-1.158271" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenationalnews.com">[The National]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenationalnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The NationalSurviving the test of time, National Museum of Beirut opens its treasure trove &#124; The NationalOctober 23, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 23, 2016</span></span></span>

## The Qadisha Valley mummies

The museum's medieval mummies come from a cave near the village of Asi al-Hadath in the Qadisha Valley. Archaeologists and cave researchers uncovered the remains during excavations in 1990 after local traditions pointed to a hidden refuge used during the late thirteenth century. The individuals are generally understood to have belonged to a Maronite Christian community that sheltered in the cave during Mamluk military campaigns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_mummies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maronite mummies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maronite mummies</span></span></span>

Unlike the carefully embalmed bodies of ancient Egypt, these individuals became mummified naturally. The cave's stable environmental conditions slowed decomposition, preserving not only bodies but also clothing, textiles, footwear, tools and domestic objects. This makes the discovery exceptionally valuable because it records everyday medieval life rather than elite burial customs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_mummies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maronite mummies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maronite mummies</span></span></span>

Researchers have been able to study diet, clothing manufacture, health and family life through the associated artefacts. One of the best-known individuals, often identified as a young woman named Yasmine, was found with remarkably preserved clothing and personal possessions, offering an unusually intimate glimpse of life in medieval rural Lebanon. Although eight mummies were recovered from the cave, only a selection is exhibited, with conservation work carried out before display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_mummies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maronite mummies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maronite mummies</span></span></span>

The importance of the find lies less in dramatic preservation than in context. Because bodies and belongings remained together, archaeologists gained evidence that written sources rarely preserve about ordinary villagers living during a turbulent period of Lebanese history.

## Sarcophagi, tombs and funerary objects

The medieval mummies form only one part of a much broader story about burial traditions across Lebanon.

The underground galleries contain one of the world's largest collections of Phoenician anthropoid sarcophagi: marble coffins carved with stylised human faces dating largely between the sixth and fourth centuries BC. Their blend of Near Eastern traditions with Egyptian artistic influence illustrates how Phoenician communities adapted ideas encountered through Mediterranean trade rather than simply copying foreign customs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2016/10/14/national-museum-of-beirut-opens-basement-for-first-time-since-civil-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theartnewspaper.com">[Art Newspaper]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theartnewspaper.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Art NewspaperNational Museum of Beirut opens basement for first time since civil war - The Art Newspaper - International art news and eve...</span></span></span>

Another highlight is the reconstructed Roman Tomb of Tyre. Excavated and painstakingly transferred into the museum, the painted burial chamber preserves colourful wall paintings, funerary decoration and the arrangement of burial niches. Italian conservators undertook major restoration work before the gallery reopened, allowing visitors to enter a faithful reconstruction without exposing the original archaeological site to constant visitor pressure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discoverlebanon.com/en/panoramic_views/beirut/national_museum.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverlebanon.com">[Discover Lebanon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverlebanon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverlebanon.com.</span></span></span>

Throughout the galleries, smaller finds help explain how burial rituals evolved over thousands of years. These include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Jewellery placed with the deceased.
* Ceramics and food offerings intended for the afterlife.
* Glass vessels, lamps and personal ornaments.
* Stone funerary stelae and carved inscriptions.
* Coffins, skeletal remains and grave architecture from multiple historical periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2016/10/14/national-museum-of-beirut-opens-basement-for-first-time-since-civil-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theartnewspaper.com">[Art Newspaper]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theartnewspaper.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Art NewspaperNational Museum of Beirut opens basement for first time since civil war - The Art Newspaper - International art news and eve...</span></span></span>

</div>

Taken together, these objects show that burial customs in ancient Lebanon were remarkably diverse. Religious beliefs, foreign influences and local traditions changed repeatedly, yet the practice of equipping the dead with meaningful possessions remained surprisingly consistent.

## War damage and the survival of the collection

The basement also carries a second history, one that belongs to twentieth-century Beirut rather than the ancient world.

When the Lebanese Civil War began in 1975, the National Museum found itself directly on the Green Line separating opposing forces. The building became a military position, suffered repeated shelling and was occupied by armed groups. Curators, led by Maurice Chehab and colleagues from the Directorate General of Antiquities, undertook extraordinary emergency measures to prevent the permanent loss of Lebanon's archaeological heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Beirut" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of Beirut</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of Beirut</span></span></span>

Small artefacts were hidden in basement storerooms and sealed behind masonry walls. Monumental sculptures, mosaics and sarcophagi that could not be moved were enclosed inside thick shells of concrete and timber. Although humidity, flooding and years of neglect damaged some material, these drastic measures saved much of the collection from looting and destruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Beirut" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of Beirut</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of Beirut</span></span></span>

The basement itself remained inaccessible for decades. Restoration eventually began with significant Italian financial and conservation support, culminating in the reopening of the underground galleries in October 2016.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2016/10/14/national-museum-of-beirut-opens-basement-for-first-time-since-civil-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theartnewspaper.com">[Art Newspaper]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theartnewspaper.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Art NewspaperNational Museum of Beirut opens basement for first time since civil war - The Art Newspaper - International art news and eve...</span></span></span>

The museum's resilience has continued to be tested. ## Why the underground galleries matter

Many museums display mummies or ancient tombs, but Beirut's underground galleries stand apart because they combine archaeological interpretation with the museum's own survival story.

The medieval mummies are important not because they are mysterious, but because they preserve the lives of ordinary villagers. The Phoenician sarcophagi and Roman tomb explain how successive civilisations understood death, identity and remembrance. At the same time, the museum building itself became an archaeological survivor of Lebanon's modern conflicts, carrying visible traces of war while protecting objects that span several millennia.

Within Lebanon's landscape of unusual collections, the basement is therefore not a cabinet of horrors or a spectacle of the dead. It is a carefully curated encounter with mortality, memory and cultural endurance, showing how both ancient communities and modern museum professionals sought to preserve what mattered most for future generations.

    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
