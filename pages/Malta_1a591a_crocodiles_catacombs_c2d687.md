## Introduction

[Malta]({{ 'where-malta-keeps-its-strangest-stories/' | relative_url }})’s strangest religious displays are not found in a single cabinet of curiosities but in a series of places where archaeology, natural history and Catholic devotion overlap. Within a short distance of one another, visitors can see a mummified Nile crocodile hanging inside a church museum, descend into vast early Christian catacombs once filled with human burials, and encounter reliquaries that preserve fragments of saints' remains as objects of prayer rather than spectacle. Together these sites reveal how Malta's religious culture has long absorbed objects from very different worlds, treating exotic animals, ancient cemeteries and sacred human remains as parts of a shared devotional landscape rather than separate museum categories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stagathamalta.com/museum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stagathamalta.com">[stagathamalta.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stagathamalta.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Agatha, Malta...</span></span></span>

## Why a crocodile appears beside Christian archaeology

Perhaps the most unexpected object in Malta's religious collections is the mummified crocodile displayed at St Agatha's Museum in Rabat. Visitors entering the museum pass archaeological finds, fossils, minerals and religious art before encountering the preserved reptile, an object that seems entirely out of place until viewed in the wider history of Mediterranean devotion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stagathamalta.com/museum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stagathamalta.com">[stagathamalta.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stagathamalta.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Agatha, Malta...</span></span></span>

### The mummified crocodile at St Agatha's Museum

The museum itself is attached to St Agatha's Crypt and Catacombs, a site associated with the early Christian cult of Saint Agatha. Its collections deliberately mix prehistoric fossils, Roman artefacts recovered from the catacombs, religious paintings, devotional objects and the famous crocodile. The museum identifies it simply as a mummified crocodile from the Nile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stagathamalta.com/museum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stagathamalta.com">[stagathamalta.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stagathamalta.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Agatha, Malta...</span></span></span>

Although no definitive documentation explains exactly when or why the crocodile entered the collection, its presence fits a wider early modern Catholic tradition. Exotic animals brought from Egypt or the eastern Mediterranean were sometimes displayed in churches as gifts from travellers, reminders of biblical lands, or symbols of divine power over dangerous creatures. Crocodiles also appeared in churches elsewhere around the Mediterranean, particularly in Italy, where they were occasionally suspended from ceilings as votive offerings or interpreted as representations of evil overcome by faith. Malta's example survives because the museum preserves the eclectic collecting habits of earlier centuries rather than presenting a narrowly archaeological display.

The result is strikingly different from a natural history museum. The crocodile is not interpreted primarily as zoological evidence but as part of a broader collection linking pilgrimage, antiquarian curiosity and Christian heritage.

## Why Malta's catacombs feel different from many others

The underground cemeteries around Rabat are among the largest surviving groups of early Christian catacombs in the Mediterranean. Their importance lies less in dramatic displays of skeletons than in what the architecture reveals about how communities buried and remembered their dead between the Roman and early Byzantine periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/1113" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Maltese Catacomb Complexes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMaltese Catacomb Complexes - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Unlike later European ossuaries where bones were deliberately arranged for display, Malta's catacombs were primarily communal burial complexes carved into soft limestone. Most visible human remains were removed during earlier centuries of excavation, reuse or archaeological investigation. What visitors encounter today are the tombs themselves: rock-cut chambers, burial niches, decorated graves and ceremonial spaces that communicate the social life surrounding death rather than presenting skeletons as exhibits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritagemalta.mt/explore/st-pauls-catacombs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagemalta.mt">[Heritage Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagemalta.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heritage Malta St Paul&#x27;s Catacombs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heritage MaltaSt Paul&#x27;s Catacombs - Heritage Malta...</span></span></span>

Several features make these catacombs distinctive:

* Large interconnected underground networks developed from earlier Punic and Roman burial traditions.
* Family tombs sit alongside evidence for broader communal cemeteries.

Rather than presenting a single religious narrative, the catacombs illustrate Malta as a crossroads where different communities shared burial space while maintaining distinct traditions. ### St Agatha's and St Paul's catacombs

Although often mentioned together, the two principal visitor sites offer different experiences.

St Paul's Catacombs form the largest accessible complex, with extensive passages, burial chambers and ceremonial areas that illustrate centuries of changing funerary practice. Heritage Malta emphasises the site's long development from Punic through Roman and Byzantine periods and notes that its association with St Paul is traditional rather than archaeologically demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritagemalta.mt/explore/st-pauls-catacombs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagemalta.mt">[Heritage Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagemalta.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heritage Malta St Paul&#x27;s Catacombs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heritage MaltaSt Paul&#x27;s Catacombs - Heritage Malta...</span></span></span>

St Agatha's Catacombs, by contrast, are closely connected with the neighbouring church and museum. Here the archaeological remains are interpreted alongside devotional traditions surrounding Saint Agatha, making it easier to see how religious memory and archaeological evidence became intertwined over many centuries.

## Sacred human remains are objects of devotion, not macabre attractions

To modern visitors, reliquaries containing fragments of human remains can appear unsettling. Within Malta's Catholic tradition, however, they were never intended as displays of mortality comparable to bone chapels or anatomical museums.

Relics are preserved because saints were believed to remain spiritually connected to the faithful after death. Bones, teeth or tiny bodily fragments were enclosed within elaborate reliquaries crafted from precious metals, crystal or carved wood, allowing believers to honour the saint without directly viewing the remains themselves.

In Malta, this tradition reflects centuries under the Knights of St John and the islands' enduring Catholic identity. Churches accumulated relics through gifts, exchanges and connections with Rome and the wider Mediterranean. The emphasis was devotional rather than sensational: the artistic container often became as important as the relic it protected.

This distinction matters because visitors accustomed to European ossuaries may expect dramatic displays of skeletons. Malta generally presents sacred remains in a very different way. The reliquary is treated as a liturgical object used in worship, processions and feast days rather than as a curiosity or work of macabre art.

## A distinctive blend of archaeology, faith and curiosity

What makes Malta unusual is not any single object but the way very different categories coexist within the same religious landscape.

A visitor can move from Roman burial chambers to a museum displaying an Egyptian crocodile, then continue to churches housing centuries-old reliquaries, all within a compact historic district. None of these collections were originally assembled to create a "weird museum". Instead, they reflect successive layers of collecting shaped by pilgrimage, scholarship, colonial connections, antiquarian interests and living Catholic devotion.

For that reason, Malta's sacred oddities reward careful interpretation. The crocodile is best understood as an inherited curiosity embedded in a religious collection. The catacombs are archaeological cemeteries whose atmosphere comes from their original function rather than exposed bones. The reliquaries preserve human remains, but they do so within a devotional framework where reverence consistently outweighs spectacle.

Seen together, these places reveal a version of Malta's curiosity culture that is both unusual and deeply rooted in history: a landscape where natural wonders, ancient burial spaces and sacred relics have long been preserved side by side without losing their original religious meaning.

    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
