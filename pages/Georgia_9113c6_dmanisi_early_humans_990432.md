## Introduction

Dmanisi is one of the most important archaeological sites in the world because it preserves some of the earliest known evidence of human ancestors living outside Africa. Buried beneath the ruins of a medieval town in southern [Georgia]({{ 'where-georgia-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }}), the site has produced remarkably complete fossil skulls, stone tools and thousands of animal remains dating to around 1.8 million years ago. These discoveries transformed ideas about when, how and by whom the first migrations out of Africa took place. Rather than being an archaeological curiosity in the sensational sense, Dmanisi is a place where careful scientific excavation has rewritten a major chapter of human history. For visitors interested in unusual collections, it offers something rarer than myth: genuine evidence that has changed the story of humanity itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Dmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreOctober 24, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2007</span></span></span>

## Why Dmanisi Changed the Human Story

Before the discoveries at Dmanisi, many researchers believed that early humans only expanded beyond Africa after developing larger brains, more sophisticated stone tools and more advanced bodies adapted for long-distance travel. The Georgian evidence challenged that model.

Excavations beginning in the 1980s uncovered primitive stone tools alongside fossil animals from the Early Pleistocene. The first hominin jawbone followed in 1991, and subsequent field seasons revealed exceptionally well-preserved skulls and skeletal remains belonging to several individuals. Together they demonstrated that small-brained members of the genus *Homo* had already dispersed into Eurasia roughly 1.8 million years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Dmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreOctober 24, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2007</span></span></span>

The site is especially valuable because everything was found in secure geological layers. The human fossils, animal bones and stone tools all belong to the same ancient landscape, allowing researchers to reconstruct not only who these early people were but also the environment in which they lived. Fossil evidence includes extinct rhinoceroses, sabre-toothed cats, giant ostriches, deer and other animals that help reconstruct the climate and ecology of the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Dmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreOctober 24, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2007</span></span></span>

Unlike many famous prehistoric sites represented by isolated teeth or fragmentary bones, Dmanisi preserves an unusually complete community of early humans. This richness has made it one of the world's benchmark sites for studying the first expansion of humans beyond Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Dmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreOctober 24, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2007</span></span></span>

## What Archaeologists Found at Dmanisi

The importance of Dmanisi comes from the combination of discoveries rather than any single spectacular fossil.

The excavations have produced:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Five unusually complete skulls from early members of the genus *Homo*.
* Multiple lower jaws and numerous postcranial bones.
* Thousands of stone tools made using relatively simple Oldowan technology.
* Thousands of fossil animal remains representing dozens of extinct species.
* Evidence that the site preserves repeated occupations rather than a single moment in time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Dmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreOctober 24, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2007</span></span></span>

The human fossils are generally dated to about 1.77–1.85 million years old, placing them among the earliest securely dated hominin remains outside Africa. Their excellent preservation has allowed researchers to compare skull shape, body proportions, walking ability and even aspects of health and ageing in far greater detail than is possible at many other sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/d2282" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human OriginsD2282 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program...</span></span></span>

The surrounding medieval ruins add another unusual dimension. Visitors walk through the remains of a medieval settlement while standing above deposits that preserve evidence from nearly two million years earlier, making Dmanisi one of the rare archaeological landscapes where vastly different periods occupy the same location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://georgia.travel/dmanisi-home-to-the-first-europeans" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: georgia.travel">[Georgia Travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">georgia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Travel Dmanisi Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dmanisi Archaeological Site - Home of the First Europeans &#124; Georgia Travel...</span></span></span>

## Why the Five Skulls Remain Scientifically Important

The five Dmanisi skulls are among the most discussed fossil discoveries in modern palaeoanthropology.

What makes them exceptional is not simply their age but their variation. Although all came from essentially the same population and period, the skulls differ noticeably in brain size, facial shape and jaw structure. Previously, fossils showing differences of this magnitude from different African sites had often been assigned to separate species.

When Skull 5 was described in 2013, researchers argued that the range of variation within the Dmanisi population might suggest that several named early human species could instead represent normal variation within a single evolving lineage. That interpretation proved controversial and remains debated, but it forced scientists to reconsider long-standing assumptions about how many early human species actually existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/fossils/d2282" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[Human Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human OriginsD2282 &#124; The Smithsonian Institution&#x27;s Human Origins Program...</span></span></span>

Not every specialist agrees with a single-species interpretation. Some researchers continue to recognise distinctions between early African forms, while more recent analyses of Dmanisi fossils—including studies of fossil teeth—have suggested that the picture may be more complex than either side originally proposed. New evidence continues to refine, rather than settle, the debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/human-evolution/homo-erectus-wasnt-the-first-human-species-to-leave-africa-1-8-million-years-ago-fossils-suggest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">By analyzing 24 fossilized teeth from the Dmanisi site in Georgia—dating back approximately 1.8 million years—researchers discovered dent...</span></span></span>

The site has also revealed evidence of resilience and social behaviour. One famous individual survived for years despite losing nearly all of its teeth, implying that companions may have helped provide food. While this does not prove modern-style caregiving, it offers rare insight into cooperation among some of the earliest humans known outside Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Dmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreOctober 24, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2007</span></span></span>

## What the Fossils Reveal About Early Human Migration

Dmanisi suggests that the first expansion beyond Africa did not necessarily require large brains or sophisticated technology.

The stone tools are relatively simple, yet the people who made them successfully occupied environments well beyond tropical Africa. Postcranial remains show a mixture of primitive and more modern characteristics. They retained small body size and relatively small brains but possessed body proportions and lower limbs well suited to efficient walking over long distances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature06134" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Postcranial evidence from early Homo from Dmanisi, Georgia &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Postcranial evidence from early Homo from Dmanisi, Georgia &#124; Nature...</span></span></span>

This combination changed scientific thinking about migration. Instead of waiting for a dramatic evolutionary breakthrough, early humans may have dispersed gradually using flexible behaviour and modest technological abilities.

The discoveries have therefore become central to discussions of one of archaeology's biggest questions: how a small African population eventually spread across Eurasia and, much later, the rest of the world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5225" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Dmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDmanisi Hominid Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreOctober 24, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2007</span></span></span>
