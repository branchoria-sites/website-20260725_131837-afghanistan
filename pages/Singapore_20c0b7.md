## Introduction

These institutions also differ sharply in purpose. Some are scholarly repositories; others are religious spaces, commercial attractions, memorial museums or private passion projects. Understanding that difference matters. A preserved animal specimen carries scientific documentation, while a sacred relic derives much of its meaning from faith and ritual. Haw Par Villa’s demons are neither archaeological evidence nor simple fantasy: they are theatrical interpretations of moral and religious traditions. Singapore’s strange museums are most interesting when visitors look beyond the startling object and ask who collected it, what claim is being made for it and why it has been placed on public view.

## Haw Par Villa: Singapore’s great cabinet of moral horrors

Haw Par Villa is the clearest expression of Singapore’s eccentric heritage. Originally known as Tiger Balm Gardens, it was created by businessman Aw Boon Haw, whose family fortune came from Tiger Balm. The grounds were developed as a public landscape of statues and dioramas illustrating Chinese stories, historical figures, ethical lessons and popular religious ideas. Heritage records identify the Courts of Hell and scenes from *Journey to the West* among the park’s best-known additions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/places/surveyed-sites/haw-par-villa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: roots.gov.sg">[Roots]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">roots.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">haw par villa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Haw Par VillaNotable statuaries that were added to the garden included the “Courts of Hell” and “Journey to the West”. Legendary heroes stand beside comic figures, deities, moral exemplars and gruesome scenes of punishment. The park’s visual language is direct and theatrical: virtue is rewarded, wrongdoing has bodily consequences, and folklore becomes something that can be walked through.

## Specimens, machines and medical objects

Away from Haw Par Villa, Singapore’s curiosity collections become less theatrical and more specialised. Their strangeness often comes from scale, preservation or obsolete technology.

The Lee Kong Chian Natural History Museum at the National University of Singapore is the country’s leading scientific cabinet of wonders. Its public galleries contain dinosaurs and Southeast Asian biodiversity displays, but the institution is much larger than its visitor-facing exhibits suggest. Its Zoological Reference Collection holds more than one million specimens representing at least 10,000 species, including rare and extinct animals. These are not novelty objects: they are research records used to identify species, study environmental change and document habitats that may no longer exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lkcnhm.nus.edu.sg/guardian-zoological-reference-collection-mrs-yang-changman/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lkcnhm.nus.edu.sg">[NUS LKCNHM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lkcnhm.nus.edu.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NUS LKCNHMThe Guardian of the Zoological Reference Collection – Mrs...July 18, 2018 — 18 Jul 2018 — ZRC is the largest and most comprehe...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 18, 2018</span></span></span>

The museum’s institutional history is itself a story of endangered collections. Its roots stretch back through Singapore’s earlier library and museum institutions. During the twentieth century, zoological material was separated from the National Museum, packed, relocated and preserved through periods when its long-term future was uncertain. The collection eventually found a permanent purpose-built home, which opened in 2015.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lkcnhm.nus.edu.sg/book-review-of-whales-and-dinosaurs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lkcnhm.nus.edu.sg">[NUS LKCNHM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lkcnhm.nus.edu.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NUS LKCNHMBook Review: Of Whales and Dinosaurs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NUS LKCNHMBook Review: Of Whales and Dinosaurs</span></span></span>

For visitors interested in oddities, the important point is that a natural-history museum is not merely a room of dramatic skeletons. Behind the displays lies an organised archive of bodies: shells, insects, fish, birds, mammals and other organisms labelled with information about where and when they were collected. The documentation turns an uncanny preserved creature into scientific evidence. That level of curation gives the museum far greater credibility than travelling “mystery animal” shows or unsupported cryptid displays.

The Singapore Musical Box Museum offers a much smaller but unusually focused experience. Its collection contains more than 40 antique musical boxes and mechanical music devices, including large machines that bear little resemblance to the small jewellery-box mechanisms familiar today. Guided demonstrations explain cylinders, discs, combs and other components, allowing visitors to hear machines that would otherwise sit silently behind glass. The museum also explores Singapore’s role in the regional trade and development of mechanical music during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://singaporemusicalboxmuseum.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: singaporemusicalboxmuseum.org">[Singapore Musical Box Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">singaporemusicalboxmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on singaporemusicalboxmuseum.org.</span></span></span>

This is a collector-led museum, but it presents itself as a repository for preservation, education and research rather than simply a room of expensive antiques. Its most distinctive claimed object is the “Singanam”, associated with early British–Singaporean musical-box manufacture. Visits and arrangements can be more structured than at a large national museum, so advance booking and confirmation of the current address are sensible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://singaporemusicalboxmuseum.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: singaporemusicalboxmuseum.org">[Singapore Musical Box Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">singaporemusicalboxmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on singaporemusicalboxmuseum.org.</span></span></span>

MINT Museum of Toys turns mass-produced objects into cultural evidence. Its collection numbers around 50,000 toys and related collectables from many countries, although only a portion can be displayed at once. Vintage robots, tin vehicles, character toys, dolls, advertising figures and space-age fantasies reveal what different societies sold to children and adults.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://emint.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: emint.com">[emint.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">emint.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on emint.com.</span></span></span>

The museum is commercial and nostalgia-led, but the best exhibits go beyond childhood reminiscence. Toys preserve changing attitudes to technology, empire, race, gender, war and popular entertainment. They can also show how global characters were copied, locally adapted or turned into merchandise. A cheaply made tin robot may therefore reveal as much about twentieth-century hopes and prejudices as a more conventionally important artefact.

Singapore General Hospital’s museum occupies another category: the institutional medical collection. Housed in the historic Bowyer Block, it preserves records, early medical equipment, surgical instruments and material concerning the development of nursing, specialist treatment and medical education. Entry is free, and the museum operates within an active hospital campus rather than as a conventional tourist attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sgh.com.sg/about-sgh/who-we-are/sgh-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgh.com.sg">[sgh.com.sg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgh.com.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sgh museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sgh museum</span></span></span>

Visitors seeking graphic pathology jars or a theatrical “museum of medical horrors” may find its tone restrained. Its unusual value lies in seeing once-modern technologies become historical objects. Early instruments and treatment equipment record how medicine was practised, taught and professionalised in Singapore. The setting also discourages sensationalism: these objects are interpreted as part of healthcare history, not as freak-show curiosities.

## Sacred relics and private worlds

Some of Singapore’s most unusual collections cannot be understood through museum standards alone because they remain part of living communities.

The Buddha Tooth Relic Temple and Museum in Chinatown was built to enshrine a tooth said by the temple to be a relic of the historical Buddha. The temple’s account states that it was discovered in Myanmar in 1980 during work on a collapsed religious structure and was later brought to Singapore. It is displayed within a large gold stupa and functions as an object of devotion, pilgrimage and religious identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=389e7067-97fb-4e1d-b7e9-688efd442319" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nlb.gov.sg">[National Library Board]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nlb.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library Board Buddha Tooth Relic Temple and Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library Board Buddha Tooth Relic Temple and Museum</span></span></span>

The relic’s physical authenticity has been publicly questioned. Dental specialists have argued that its size and form are incompatible with a human tooth and may indicate an animal origin. More broadly, Buddhist relics are not always subjected to scientific testing, because religious authorities and devotees may understand their significance through lineage, ritual and spiritual effects rather than laboratory verification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/1f2f8dca3b4431a77c3e19e653d94896" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News Teeth, finger bones and blessings: Buddhist relics inspire belief</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The temple&#x27;s founder, Master YongHua, insists the relics have miraculous properties, having witnessed them multiply and heal ailments. A responsible visit therefore avoids two easy mistakes. The first is to repeat the relic’s identity as scientifically proven fact. The second is to dismiss the entire temple as a hoax. Whatever the tooth’s biological origin, the shrine is an authentic devotional environment: people pray there, offerings are made, and the object shapes religious experience. The museum question and the faith question overlap, but they are not identical.

The Intan, in Joo Chiat, represents another boundary between museum and lived space. It is the private home and collection of Alvin Yapp, filled with Peranakan furniture, ceramics, beadwork, clothing, jewellery and domestic objects. Visits are strictly by appointment and are usually guided, making the experience closer to entering a collector’s carefully constructed world than walking through a public gallery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://the-intan.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: the-intan.com">[The Intan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">the-intan.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on the-intan.com.</span></span></span>

The Intan is not strange because its objects are macabre. It is unusual because of its density, intimacy and dependence on a single collector-curator. Objects are interpreted through personal storytelling, hospitality and performance rather than through impersonal museum labels. This can create a vivid connection with material culture, although visitors should remember that every private collection reflects its owner’s tastes, access and narrative choices.

The Singapore Musical Box Museum and The Intan demonstrate how strongly private collecting contributes to the country’s small-museum landscape. Such places can preserve objects ignored by larger institutions, but their opening arrangements, displays and long-term future may depend heavily on individual owners. They reward advance planning and should not be treated as casual walk-in attractions.

## Animals that are alive, not preserved

The Live Turtle and Tortoise Museum stretches the meaning of “museum” further. Its principal collection consists of living turtles and tortoises rather than dead specimens or historic artefacts. The institution combines animal display, pet education and rescue or rehoming messages, presenting numerous species alongside turtle-related objects. Its official information lists regular public opening hours, with Tuesday closures for maintenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.turtle-tortoise.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: turtle-tortoise.com">[turtle-tortoise.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">turtle-tortoise.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on turtle-tortoise.com.</span></span></span>

This is best understood as a privately operated animal collection using the language of a museum. The experience is more immediate than a natural-history gallery because the animals move, feed and interact with their environment. It also raises different questions of credibility and ethics. Visitors should judge it not only by the rarity or visual appeal of the animals but by enclosure quality, husbandry, conservation messaging and whether handling is controlled appropriately.

Its contrast with the Lee Kong Chian Natural History Museum is revealing. One preserves dead specimens as permanent scientific records; the other maintains living animals for public encounters and education. Both are collections, but they require entirely different standards of care and interpretation.

## Captivity and command underground

Singapore’s wartime museums contain fewer supernatural images, yet their preserved spaces and personal objects can feel more unsettling because the suffering they document was real.

Changi Chapel and Museum tells the story of prisoners of war and civilian internees held during the Japanese occupation. Its eight galleries contain more than 100 artefacts, including diaries, photographs, identity tags, drawings and objects secretly retained or made under imprisonment. The displays emphasise individual lives, resilience and creativity rather than presenting captivity as an abstract military episode.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritage.sg/changichapelmuseum/collection/artefact-highlights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritage.sg">[heritage.sg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritage.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heritage.sg.</span></span></span>

Among the most powerful items are ordinary things whose survival seems improbable: a diary written as letters to an absent wife, a small camera concealed from guards, handmade tools and objects associated with improvised religious practice. Their credibility comes from documented ownership and donation histories, often supplied by former internees’ families. These are not generic props added to create atmosphere; they are personal evidence.

The reconstructed chapel requires a little care in interpretation. It is modelled on one of the churches built by prisoners, rather than being an untouched wartime structure in its original position. That does not make it false, but visitors should distinguish commemorative reconstruction from surviving fabric.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritage.sg/changichapelmuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritage.sg">[heritage.sg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritage.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heritage.sg.</span></span></span>

The Battlebox beneath Fort Canning Hill offers the opposite wartime perspective: military command rather than imprisonment. The underground complex served as a bomb-proof headquarters during the Battle of Singapore and is associated with the British decision to surrender in February 1942. After periods of closure and changing operators, it reopened to visitors in February 2024 with audio interpretation and immersive projections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://battlebox.sg/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: battlebox.sg">[battlebox.sg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">battlebox.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on battlebox.sg.</span></span></span>

The bunker’s power comes primarily from the place itself: confined rooms, thick walls, communications spaces and the knowledge that commanders attempted to understand a collapsing military situation from underground. Modern projections and dramatic presentation can help visitors follow events, but they should remain secondary to the surviving site and historical record. It is a heritage attraction, not a paranormal location, even though underground wartime spaces frequently acquire ghost stories in popular tourism.

## How to judge Singapore’s unusual collections

Singapore’s curiosity sites make different kinds of truth claims. Treating them all alike produces confusion.

**Scientific collections** such as the Lee Kong Chian Natural History Museum depend on specimen labels, taxonomy, comparative study and institutional research. Their authority comes from documentation and repeatable examination.

**Historical museums** such as Changi Chapel and Museum and the Battlebox rely on artefact provenance, archival records, oral histories and surviving architecture. Reconstructions and immersive media should be identified clearly, but they can sit alongside authentic objects without invalidating the wider presentation.

**Devotional collections** such as the Buddha Tooth Relic Temple present objects whose importance is rooted in religious belief. A relic may be spiritually meaningful even when its physical identification is disputed. Visitors should distinguish respectful observation of belief from accepting an empirical claim without evidence.

**[Collector museums]({{ 'collector-museums/' | relative_url }})** such as The Intan, the Musical Box Museum and MINT can be exceptionally rich but inevitably reflect private selection. Their strengths are concentration, enthusiasm and specialist knowledge; their limitations may include restricted access, dependence on one curator and less visible institutional oversight.

**Commercial or entertainment-led interpretation** is most apparent at Hell’s Museum. Its vividness does not automatically make it inaccurate, but the Ten Courts should be read as staged moral imagery shaped by popular religion, historical family entertainment and modern tourism—not as a straightforward map of what every tradition teaches about the afterlife.

## What makes Singapore’s curiosity culture distinctive

Singapore’s strangest collections are not primarily about unexplained monsters or imported occult sensationalism. They reveal how a trading city classifies difficult subjects: death is converted into moral theatre, animals into scientific records, toys into social history, sacred matter into devotional experience, and wartime suffering into documented memory.

Haw Par Villa remains the indispensable site because it resists Singapore’s reputation for polished, orderly attractions. Its cracked-open world of demons, hybrid animals, heroes and punishments preserves a popular visual culture that could easily have disappeared. The natural-history and medical collections show a more disciplined form of curiosity, in which preservation serves research and public education. Private museums demonstrate what determined individuals can save, while temples and memorial institutions remind visitors that not every meaningful object belongs comfortably inside a secular display case.

Taken together, these places form a dispersed national cabinet of curiosities. Its contents are not always conventionally beautiful, and their claims are not equally testable. Their value lies precisely in those differences: they show the many ways Singapore has preserved fear, faith, machinery, bodies, memory and wonder.
