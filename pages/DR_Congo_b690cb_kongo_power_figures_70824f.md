## Introduction

Kongo power figures are among the most striking objects associated with the Democratic Republic of the Congo, yet they are also among the most misunderstood. Their dramatic appearance—wooden figures studded with iron nails or blades, often with mirrors set into the abdomen—has encouraged generations of visitors, collectors and popular media to describe them simply as "witchcraft objects". That label is misleading. These sculptures were not created as horror props or idols of evil. Instead, many served as public instruments of justice, healing, protection, oath-taking and conflict resolution within Kongo communities, with their power understood to come from ritual specialists, sacred substances and social agreements rather than from the carved wood alone. Modern museums increasingly interpret them in this broader cultural context, challenging sensational colonial-era descriptions that reduced them to exotic curiosities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/312221" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Power Figure: Male (Nkisi Nkondi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtPower Figure: Male (Nkisi Nkondi) - Kongo peoples - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

## Were Kongo Nail Figures Really Witchcraft Objects?

The short answer is no—at least not in the simple sense suggested by the word "witchcraft". Many Kongo communities distinguished between harmful spiritual aggression and the legitimate use of ritual knowledge to restore order, uncover hidden wrongdoing or protect individuals and communities. Power figures belonged to the latter sphere.

The best-known examples are often called *nkisi nkondi*, sometimes translated as "hunter" or "hunter of wrongdoers". They were commissioned for specific purposes and activated by trained ritual specialists rather than functioning as magical objects on their own. Their role could include settling disputes, witnessing agreements, identifying thieves, protecting settlements, healing illness or enforcing justice when ordinary social mechanisms failed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/312221" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Power Figure: Male (Nkisi Nkondi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtPower Figure: Male (Nkisi Nkondi) - Kongo peoples - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because colonial observers frequently used the broad label "fetish" to describe almost every unfamiliar African ritual object. That vocabulary encouraged European audiences to imagine irrational superstition while overlooking the legal, political and therapeutic roles these figures played within Kongo society. Contemporary museums increasingly avoid that language or explain its historical baggage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1905" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## How Power Figures Were Activated and Used

A power figure became effective only after collaboration between a woodcarver and a ritual specialist, often known as a *nganga*. The sculptor produced the physical form, but the ritual expert transformed it into a functioning ritual object through carefully prepared medicinal and symbolic materials.

These materials, often called *bilongo*, were placed inside cavities in the figure's head or abdomen. They could include selected soils, plant materials, minerals, shells or animal substances chosen for symbolic and ritual reasons rather than random decoration. Mirrors or pieces of reflective glass frequently sealed the abdominal cavity. Rather than serving as ornament, the reflective surface represented communication with the spirit world and the ability to detect hidden dangers or concealed wrongdoing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/311035" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Power Figure: Male (Nkisi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtPower Figure: Male (Nkisi) - Kongo peoples - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

The figure therefore acted less like an idol receiving worship than as a carefully prepared container through which spiritual forces could be directed towards specific community concerns.

## Why Nails, Mirrors and Animal Forms Mattered

The dense covering of iron nails is the feature that most often leads modern viewers to assume violence or torture. In reality, the nails usually recorded moments of ritual activation.

Each blade or nail commonly marked an oath, agreement, request or judgement. Driving metal into the figure could activate its power in response to a dispute or formal promise. In some traditions, people participating in the agreement touched or licked the blade before it was driven into the figure, sealing the commitment. More serious disputes might be represented by larger or more forcefully inserted blades. The figure therefore became a physical archive of social obligations accumulated over many years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://smarthistory.org/nkisi-nkondi-kongo-people/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smarthistory.org">[smarthistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smarthistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">– Power Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Kongo peoples</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smarthistory – Power Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Kongo peoples...</span></span></span>

Mirrors carried equally important symbolism. Rather than allowing the figure to "see" in a literal sense, reflective surfaces represented the boundary between the visible world and the world of spirits. Through this symbolic window, hidden truths could be revealed and supernatural assistance invoked against deception or injustice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://smarthistory.org/nkisi-nkondi-kongo-people/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smarthistory.org">[Smarthistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smarthistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">– Power Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Kongo peoples</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smarthistory – Power Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Kongo peoples...</span></span></span>

Not every power object was human-shaped. Dogs appeared frequently because they were associated with tracking what could not easily be found, making them appropriate symbols for uncovering concealed crimes or pursuing invisible threats. Animal imagery therefore reflected function rather than decoration or fantasy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1905" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## Why Museums and Markets Distort Their Meaning

The popular image of a nail-covered "voodoo doll" owes far more to colonial collecting and the international art market than to Kongo religious practice.

During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, European administrators, missionaries and collectors often removed these figures from their ritual settings. Once separated from the communities and specialists who maintained them, they were displayed as examples of "primitive fetishism" or evidence of supposed African superstition. Context about legal mediation, healing or community governance was frequently omitted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1905" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Commercial art markets reinforced this misunderstanding because dramatic objects with hundreds of nails proved visually compelling. Auction catalogues and popular books often emphasised mystery, curses or "dark magic", making the sculptures easier to sell but harder to understand historically.

Many museums have since revised their interpretation. Institutions such as the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the British Museum and educational organisations including Smarthistory and Khan Academy now explain that these figures emerged through collaboration between artists and ritual specialists and were embedded within systems of law, medicine, religion and community responsibility rather than existing as generic objects of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/312221" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Power Figure: Male (Nkisi Nkondi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtPower Figure: Male (Nkisi Nkondi) - Kongo peoples - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

## Seeing Kongo Power Figures in DR Congo Today

Visitors to museums in the Democratic Republic of the Congo are increasingly encouraged to view power figures as part of the country's cultural and historical heritage rather than as frightening curiosities. National museum displays place them alongside masks, royal sculpture and other ritual objects, presenting them within broader narratives about Kongo belief systems, political authority and artistic traditions.

Outside the country, many of the finest surviving examples remain in European and North American museums because of colonial collecting. This history has become part of the story the objects now tell. Questions about provenance, interpretation and the return of cultural heritage accompany discussions of their original ritual function.

For anyone interested in the [DR Congo]({{ 'what-makes-the-dr-congos-collections-so/' | relative_url }})'s unusual collections, Kongo power figures are compelling not because they confirm myths of African witchcraft, but because they demonstrate how spectacular objects can be misunderstood when removed from their cultural setting. Their nails, mirrors and medicines were never merely theatrical. They formed part of a sophisticated system through which communities sought justice, protection and healing, making these figures as much records of social relationships as works of sculpture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1905" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumfigure &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
