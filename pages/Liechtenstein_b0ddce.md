## Where the strangest objects are kept

## The National Museum’s cabinet-like side

Liechtenstein’s National Museum is broader than the project’s usual idea of a strange museum. It covers archaeology, political history, customs, natural history, industry and the country’s physical landscape. Yet its breadth creates startling transitions. The institution itself promotes a route running “from dinosaur bones to huge fasting cloths to modern industry”, an unusually compressed journey through deep time, religion and technological development.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/our-museums/nationalmuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein NationalMuseumFrom dinosaur bones to huge fasting cloths to modern industry: experience the history, culture and nature of...</span></span></span>

The enormous fasting cloth is a particularly distinctive survival. Such cloths were used to screen or veil church interiors during Lent, placing them somewhere between devotional object, sacred theatre and monumental textile. The example associated with Bendern parish church is preserved as an original object in the national collection. Rather than presenting religion only through paintings or precious metalwork, the museum retains an artefact that once altered how an entire worshipping community experienced sacred space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtensteinisches_Landesmuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liechtensteinisches Landesmuseum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtensteinisches Landesmuseum</span></span></span>

Natural-history material provides another curiosity strand. Their unusual quality comes from scale and juxtaposition, with prehistoric remains, church treasures, farming tools and industrial equipment used to construct the story of a country only about 25 kilometres long.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/our-museums/nationalmuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein NationalMuseumFrom dinosaur bones to huge fasting cloths to modern industry: experience the history, culture and nature of...</span></span></span>

## Folklore preserved beyond the display case

Liechtenstein’s richest public presentation of supernatural tradition is found in Triesenberg, where the Walser Museum documents the descendants of migrants from the Swiss Valais who settled in the mountain area during the Middle Ages. The museum is fundamentally a local-history institution, displaying domestic objects, agricultural equipment, woodworking and livestock traditions. Its importance to strange-history visitors lies in the way material culture is connected to a living body of legends, dialect and Alpine identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walsermuseum.li/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walsermuseum.li">[walsermuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walsermuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">8 am – 12 pm and 3 pm – 6 pm...</span></span></span>

A historical Walser house supplements the main exhibition. Its rooms reconstruct modest nineteenth-century domestic life, while the museum also preserves an old Alpine cabin and wooden sculptures by the local artist Rudolf Schädler. This is not an “occult museum”, and everyday tools greatly outnumber supernatural artefacts. Nevertheless, the setting makes the folklore credible as community history: stories of spirits and uncanny beings are presented alongside the kitchens, farming practices and isolated mountain environment in which those stories circulated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.tourismus.li/map/poi/walsermuseum-e7e9eda4-f07a-4f43-bb4b-f58006674111.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.tourismus.li">[Visit Liechtenstein]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.tourismus.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Liechtenstein Walser Museum, Triesenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit LiechtensteinWalser Museum, Triesenberg - LiechtensteinThe Walser Museum documents the culture and history of the Valais immigrants...</span></span></span>

The associated [Walser Legends]({{ 'walser-legends/' | relative_url }}) Trail takes the collection outdoors. Carved figures and storytelling stations introduce visitors to selected tales from the region’s larger folklore tradition. Liechtenstein’s recorded legends include dragons, devils, giants, dwarfs, hidden treasure, witches, ghosts, supernatural night processions and troublesome household beings. The country’s Historical Lexicon notes that one major published collection contained 205 legends, with a later supplement adding 31 more.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walsersagenweg.li/en/sagen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walsersagenweg.li">[walsersagenweg.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walsersagenweg.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on walsersagenweg.li.</span></span></span>

The trail should be understood as heritage interpretation, not an assertion that its creatures were real. It revives traditional narratives through tourism, sculpture and landscape, allowing visitors to encounter folklore in the sort of forests and steep terrain that shaped it. Compared with a glass-case museum, the experience is more atmospheric and more openly tourist-led, but it remains attached to a municipal museum and documented regional tradition rather than an invented monster franchise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walsersagenweg.li/en/sagen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walsersagenweg.li">[walsersagenweg.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walsersagenweg.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on walsersagenweg.li.</span></span></span>

## Witch trials without a witch museum

Liechtenstein has no prominent permanent museum devoted exclusively to witchcraft, despite possessing one of the darkest witch-persecution histories associated with its region. The absence matters because travellers searching for a “[Liechtenstein witch]({{ 'witch-trials-284786/' | relative_url }}) museum” may encounter scattered references and assume that a dedicated attraction exists. The subject is better approached through historical scholarship, the National Museum’s wider interpretation of early modern society and Triesenberg’s folklore displays.

The major persecutions took place before the modern Principality of Liechtenstein was created. Trials in the territories of Vaduz and Schellenberg became especially severe in the late seventeenth century. The Historical Lexicon describes the proceedings of 1679–80 as the second-largest witch-trial series of its period after the Salzburg persecutions, and records serious procedural abuses, including unusually cruel forms of torture. An imperial investigation eventually helped bring the prosecutions to an end.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Hexenverfolgung" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[historisches-lexikon.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hexenverfolgung – Historisches Lexikon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hexenverfolgung – Historisches Lexikon</span></span></span>

This history should not be blurred with the legends displayed around Triesenberg. Folkloric witches, magical beings and ghost stories belong to traditions of storytelling; the trials involved real accusations, coercive interrogation, confiscation and execution. The distinction is essential in any responsible presentation. Liechtenstein’s historical sources also show how difficult it could be for ordinary people, and even some clergy, to distinguish condemned “magic” from accepted Catholic practices involving blessed or protective objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Magie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[historisches-lexikon.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Magie – Historisches Lexikon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magie – Historisches Lexikon</span></span></span>

The persecutions even influenced the country’s later political development. According to the Historical Lexicon, the disorder surrounding the trials worsened an existing financial and governmental crisis under the Counts of Hohenems. That crisis contributed to the sale of Schellenberg in 1699 and Vaduz in 1712 to the House of Liechtenstein, whose name the principality later took. Witch-trial history is therefore not a macabre footnote: it is entangled with the transfer of the territories that became the modern state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Hexenverfolgung" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[historisches-lexikon.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hexenverfolgung – Historisches Lexikon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hexenverfolgung – Historisches Lexikon</span></span></span>

## Small museums built around obsessive subjects

The Liechtenstein Postal Museum is one of the country’s most specialised collections. It holds a copy of every postage stamp issued by Liechtenstein’s postal service, turning a tiny printed object into a near-complete visual archive of statehood. For much of the twentieth century, stamps were among Liechtenstein’s most internationally familiar products, collected far beyond the country by people who might never visit it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/our-museums/postalmuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landesmuseum.li.</span></span></span>

Its appeal is not merely philatelic. Stamps compress rulers, landscapes, animals, religious images, anniversaries and national myths into miniature official pictures. A complete run allows visitors to watch the country repeatedly redesign itself for an outside audience. The museum is public and institutionally curated, rather than a dealer’s showroom, although it naturally sits close to the commercial culture of collecting, limited issues and rarity.

The Lawena Museum in Triesen applies the same concentrated attention to electricity. Located at a hydroelectric power station built in 1927 and still operating, it contains roughly 500 objects dating from around 1900 onwards. Many can reportedly be demonstrated in working order. ## Houses preserved as collection objects

At the Farmhouse Museum in Schellenberg, the main curiosity is the building itself. The timber house dates from 1518 and has been moved more than once, following its owners before becoming a museum. It is known as the Biedermann House after the family who occupied it for more than 150 years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/our-museums/farmhousemuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landesmuseum.li.</span></span></span>

The structure preserves a late-medieval arrangement of living and working spaces, making it effectively a three-dimensional collection item large enough to walk through. This matters in Liechtenstein because comparatively few early buildings survived the country’s rapid twentieth-century transformation. The museum does not rely on supernatural stories or spectacular treasures; its strangeness lies in encountering an entire centuries-old house that proved mobile, adaptable and ultimately collectable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/our-museums/farmhousemuseum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landesmuseum.li.</span></span></span>

Access is more restricted than at the central Vaduz museums. Published schedules have typically been seasonal and limited to selected Sundays, so a spontaneous visit is less reliable than one to the Treasure Chamber or National Museum. That limited access also reflects the site’s character: it is a preserved historic environment, not a high-throughput tourist attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landesmuseum.li.</span></span></span>

## What Liechtenstein’s curiosity culture reveals

Liechtenstein’s unusual collections do not form a single “weird museum” scene. Instead, they reveal three overlapping habits of preservation.

First, valuable objects often enter public view through private collecting. Adulf Peter Goop’s Easter eggs and Rhine pictures are the clearest example, while the much larger Princely Collections remain a major private art holding associated with the ruling family. The latter encompass more than 30,000 objects across five centuries, although much of the collection is connected to the family’s palaces and exhibitions in Vienna rather than being continuously displayed as a public museum inside Liechtenstein.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landesmuseum.li/en/our-museums/treasurechamber" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landesmuseum.li">[landesmuseum.li]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landesmuseum.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein TreasureChamberIn addition to Fabergé&#x27;s famous Apple Blossom Egg, other eggs, some of which are set with precious stones, a...</span></span></span>

Second, local identity is preserved through small, intensely focused institutions. A complete national stamp archive, an electrical museum within a working power station and an inhabited landscape interpreted through Walser legends would be side galleries in a larger country. In Liechtenstein, each can serve as a distinct cultural destination.

Third, the country generally treats strange material cautiously. Moon rocks are explained through documented industrial history; legends are identified as folklore; witch persecutions belong to legal and social history rather than Halloween entertainment. The collections may be eccentric, but they are rarely presented as evidence for paranormal claims.

For the curious traveller, that restraint is part of the attraction. Liechtenstein’s most memorable objects are not gathered to prove that the impossible happened. They show how a very small state has turned private passions, fragile buildings, folk memory, sacred textiles, miniature images and even fragments of another world into evidence of who it believes itself to be.
