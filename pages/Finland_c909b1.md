## Introduction

For visitors, this creates a varied landscape rather than a neat trail. Some collections are academically researched but rarely displayed; others are commercial and playful; still others are artworks that function like open-air cabinets of curiosity. The strongest places are candid about whether they are interpreting belief, documenting lived experience or presenting spectacle. That distinction matters in Finland, where folklore, nationalism, war, border politics and modern tourism often meet inside the same object or exhibition.

## Where Finland’s strangest objects are kept

The closest thing Finland has to a classic occult cabinet is not a dedicated witchcraft museum but a dispersed body of folk-magic material held by major public institutions. Researchers have identified hundreds of objects in the National Museum of Finland, Tampere museum collections and the Museum Centre of Turku. Recorded examples include bear teeth and claws, unusual stones, animal bones, circular tree growths, protective pouches and frogs placed in miniature coffins. ## Sacred objects are not merely curiosities

Sámi heritage adds a necessary ethical boundary to any discussion of Finland’s unusual collections. At the Sámi Museum Siida in Inari, objects, photographs, archives, art and open-air buildings are maintained as the cultural heritage of a living Indigenous people. They are not presented simply as exotic relics from a vanished northern belief system. Siida’s national collecting role includes documentation and research as well as exhibition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/the-sami-museum/museum-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>

Drums associated with Sámi spiritual specialists are especially vulnerable to sensational treatment. Their painted surfaces have been interpreted as maps of relationships between people, animals, places and spiritual realms, while the drums could be used in divination and ritual practice. Yet many surviving examples entered European collections through missionary suppression, confiscation and unequal collecting. Calling them “shaman drums” may help a visitor understand their broad function, but reducing them to occult props obscures the histories of Christianisation and colonial control that helped remove them from their communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.openedition.org/clo/445?gathStatIcon=true&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.openedition.org">[openedition.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.openedition.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on openedition.org.</span></span></span>

This issue is not confined to distant history. The National Museum of Finland has undertaken work concerning the ownership and return of Sámi collections, reflecting a wider change in museum practice from possessing Indigenous culture to negotiating stewardship with the people whose heritage is represented. The result is one of the most important distinctions in Finland’s curiosity landscape: an object may be mysterious to tourists while remaining culturally specific, sacred or politically sensitive to others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kansallismuseo.fi/en/collections/kansatieteen-kokoelma" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kansallismuseo.fi">[Suomen kansallismuseo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kansallismuseo.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Suomen kansallismuseo Ethnological collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Suomen kansallismuseo Ethnological collections</span></span></span>

Siida is therefore a better place for understanding northern cosmology than for seeking a theatrical museum of sorcery. Its authority comes from community connection, research and cultural context. Visitors expecting gothic spectacle may find a more restrained institution, but they gain a clearer view of why certain objects cannot responsibly be separated from land, language and historical power.

## Prisons, spies and borders made visitable

Finland’s darker museums are most convincing when visitors can enter the original spaces. The Prison in Hämeenlinna occupies the former county prison beside Häme Castle. The building remained in use as a prison until 1993 and is now described as Finland’s largest prison museum. Its cells, corridors and institutional architecture provide the unsettling element without needing fictional hauntings or staged horror.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hameenlinna.fi/en/services/the-prison/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hameenlinna.fi">[Hämeenlinna]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hameenlinna.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hämeenlinna The Prison</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hämeenlinna The Prison</span></span></span>

What makes it more than a collection of restraints and uniforms is the opportunity to consider imprisonment as an everyday system. A cell door, sparse room or surveillance arrangement becomes evidence of how punishment organised time, privacy and human movement. The site is publicly operated as part of the National Museum of Finland, giving it a historical and educational purpose rather than the atmosphere of a commercial “crime experience”. It sits within a broader museum area that also includes Häme Castle and Museo Militaria.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hameenlinna.fi/en/tourism-and-events/see-and-experience/museums-and-galleries/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hameenlinna.fi">[Hämeenlinna]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hameenlinna.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hämeenlinna Museums and galleries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hämeenlinna Museums and galleries</span></span></span>

Tampere’s Spy Museum takes a more entertainment-led approach. Promoted as the world’s first museum devoted to espionage, it brings together intelligence history, surveillance techniques, concealed devices and the popular mythology of spies. It is a visitor attraction rather than a state intelligence archive, so its strength lies in making clandestine methods tangible and accessible. Its broad framing covers political, military, industrial and ideological espionage rather than concentrating solely on Finnish cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visittampere.fi/en/destination/spy-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visittampere.fi">[Visit Tampere]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visittampere.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Tampere Spy Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Tampere Spy Museum</span></span></span>

The difference between the two institutions is useful. The Prison derives its authority from place and institutional history; the Spy Museum uses themed objects and interactive curiosity. Both are legitimate, but they answer different questions. One shows what confinement felt like inside an authentic penal structure. The other demonstrates how secrecy and technical ingenuity can be packaged into an enjoyable museum visit.

Finland’s bunker museums extend this encounter with original environments into the landscape. The Salpa Line was a vast defensive system constructed along the eastern frontier after the Winter War. At the Salpa Line Museum in Miehikkälä, visitors can explore fortifications, shelters, trenches and anti-tank structures alongside indoor interpretation. The line was built as protection against a possible Soviet assault but was never tested in battle, giving it the peculiar status of an enormous military object whose historical meaning lies partly in what did not happen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://salpalinjamuseo.fi/en/home/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: salpalinjamuseo.fi">[Salpalinja-museo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">salpalinjamuseo.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Salpalinja-museo Etusivu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Salpalinja-museo Etusivu</span></span></span>

These sites are unusual because they turn fear, engineering and national survival strategy into physical tourism. They are neither ruins in the romantic sense nor collections assembled in neutral galleries. The terrain itself is the principal artefact.

## When political relics outlive their museum

Tampere once contained one of Finland’s most internationally distinctive ideological collections: the Lenin Museum, opened in 1946 in the Workers’ Hall where Vladimir Lenin and Joseph Stalin had met in 1905. Over time, the museum shifted from post-war friendship symbolism and Lenin biography towards a more critical treatment of Soviet history. Even so, its name increasingly suggested nostalgia or political allegiance that did not match its curatorial direction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yle.fi/a/74-20085401" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yle.fi">[Yle.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yle.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Goodbye Lenin — Tampere museum commemorating</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Goodbye Lenin — Tampere museum commemorating</span></span></span>

The Lenin Museum closed in November 2024. In February 2025 it was replaced in the same premises by Nootti, the Museum of Finnish–Russian Relations. The new institution covers subjects including Finnish independence, civil wars, Stalinist terror, the Winter War, Cold War diplomacy, trade, tourism, intelligence and Finland’s later turn towards NATO membership.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uk.icom.museum/finlands-lenin-museum-to-close-and-be-replaced-by-a-new-museum-of-russian-relations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uk.icom.museum">[icom.museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uk.icom.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icom.museum.</span></span></span>

This transformation is a revealing example of museum meaning changing faster than museum objects. Soviet memorabilia that could once be treated with humour or retro fascination became harder to display lightly after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The problem was not that the historical material ceased to matter, but that visitors interpreted the old brand through a changed political present. Nootti therefore preserves the site’s relationship with Russia while replacing the personality cult implied by a museum named after Lenin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2025/mar/10/goodbye-lenin-finnish-museum-reinvents-itself-in-response-to-shifting-relations-with-russia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

For curiosity travellers, Nootti remains unusual, although in a less theatrical way. It is a museum built partly from the afterlife of another museum: a case study in how propaganda objects, diplomatic souvenirs and ideological nostalgia can be recatalogued when national relationships deteriorate. Its credibility rests on acknowledging that exhibitions are not politically weightless and that the act of retaining, removing or reframing an artefact is itself a historical decision.

## Outsider environments that behave like museums

Some of Finland’s most memorable collections were never intended to operate as conventional museums. The Veijo Rönkkönen Sculpture Park near Parikkala contains hundreds of concrete figures made over nearly five decades around the artist’s home. The best-known group consists of more than 200 figures performing yoga, many resembling variations on the artist himself. Other statues combine animals, human bodies, plants, artificial teeth and intensely individual facial expressions. Estimates commonly place the total at roughly 500 to 560 works.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://noba.ac/en/exhibition/veijo-ronkkonen-mystical-sculpture-park" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noba.ac">[noba.ac]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noba.ac</span><span class="citation-popover-title">VEIJ O RÖNKKÖNEN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">VEIJ O RÖNKKÖNEN</span></span></span>

The park is often marketed as eerie, but “outsider art environment” is more informative than “horror attraction”. Rönkkönen created a private world through repetition, self-portraiture, bodily poses and garden architecture. Because the works remain close to the place where they were made, visitors experience an accumulation rather than a curated retrospective. The unsettling effect comes from encountering an entire personal symbolic system without a museum’s usual explanatory distance.

The site is publicly accessible and promoted by the local tourism organisation as a year-round destination. Its preservation has turned a private artistic environment into a form of open-air collection, but it remains less controlled than an indoor museum. Weather, vegetation and gradual material change are part of the experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitparikkala.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitparikkala.com">[visitParikkala.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitparikkala.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitparikkala.com.</span></span></span>

At Suomussalmi, the Silent People offers a different kind of collective oddity. Roughly a thousand human-shaped figures stand in a field beside Highway 5, with peat heads, straw hair and changing clothes. Created by artist Reijo Kela, the installation resembles a gathering, a memorial, an army or an abandoned population, depending on the viewer. Its official tourism presentation leaves the figures’ meaning open rather than imposing a single legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitfinland.com/en/product/54b61c48-70c2-45b6-89be-8bc5eeea5c6b/the-silent-people/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitfinland.com">[Visit Finland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitfinland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitfinland.com.</span></span></span>

Neither location is a museum in the strict institutional sense. There are no comprehensive object labels, accession systems or neutral gallery rooms. Yet both perform a museum-like function by preserving and presenting large bodies of unusual material to the public. They also show how readily ambiguity can be commercialised: “creepy statues” are easy to sell as roadside spectacle, while the works themselves support more complicated readings about isolation, memory, bodily identity and rural space.

## Playable collections and everyday technology

Not every strange Finnish collection is dark or folkloric. The Finnish Museum of Games at Vapriikki in Tampere treats digital games, board games, arcade machines and domestic play environments as cultural heritage. Its displays range from internationally recognised Finnish creations such as Angry Birds and Max Payne to older or more locally specific titles. Many exhibits are playable, allowing visitors to encounter obsolete hardware and software through use rather than through glass.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vapriikki.fi/en/the-museum/the-finnish-museum-of-games/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vapriikki.fi">[Museokeskus Vapriikki]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vapriikki.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the finnish museum of games</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the finnish museum of games</span></span></span>

The museum is unusual because digital objects challenge traditional preservation. A game is not fully represented by a cartridge, disc or computer casing: it also depends on software, controls, screens, social habits and the memory of how people played. Reconstructed rooms and operational machines therefore preserve behaviour as well as equipment. The result resembles a living technology cabinet in which visitors activate the collection.

Temporary displays have also used games to examine themes beyond entertainment. One exhibition traced Finnish games about health and wellbeing, including material designed to educate players about intoxicants, sexual health and social issues. Such objects may look quaint, awkward or unintentionally comic decades later, but they reveal how public institutions tried to make moral and medical instruction interactive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vapriikki.fi/en/the-museum/the-finnish-museum-of-games/temporary-exhibitions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vapriikki.fi">[Museokeskus Vapriikki]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vapriikki.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museokeskus Vapriikki Temporary exhibitions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museokeskus Vapriikki Temporary exhibitions</span></span></span>

This is a different branch of curiosity culture from folk magic, yet the underlying question is similar: how does an ordinary object acquire museum value? A bear claw becomes significant through ritual history; an old mobile-phone game becomes significant through collective memory and technical obsolescence. Finland’s strongest unusual collections make that transformation visible.

## What visitors should expect

Finland rewards travellers who look beyond the promise of a single spectacular “weird museum”. Its unusual heritage is spread across different levels of access and authority:

* **For documented folk belief:** consult the National Museum’s collections and research projects, while remembering that much material is stored or digitised rather than permanently exhibited. The Helsinki museum building is closed until its expected reopening in spring 2027.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kansallismuseo.fi/en/kansallismuseo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kansallismuseo.fi">[Suomen kansallismuseo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kansallismuseo.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kansallismuseo.fi.</span></span></span>
* **For Indigenous cultural interpretation:** visit Siida, where spiritual and ceremonial material is presented within living Sámi heritage rather than as anonymous occult exotica.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://siida.fi/en/the-sami-museum/museum-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: siida.fi">[siida.fi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">siida.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on siida.fi.</span></span></span>
* **For authentic dark-history environments:** the Prison at Hämeenlinna and the Salpa Line sites offer original architecture, cells, shelters and fortifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hameenlinna.fi/en/services/the-prison/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hameenlinna.fi">[Hämeenlinna]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hameenlinna.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hämeenlinna The Prison</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hämeenlinna The Prison</span></span></span>
* **For playful commercial curiosity:** Tampere’s Spy Museum foregrounds gadgets, secrecy and espionage stories in an accessible visitor format.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visittampere.fi/en/destination/spy-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visittampere.fi">[Visit Tampere]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visittampere.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Tampere Spy Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Tampere Spy Museum</span></span></span>
* **For outsider art and roadside uncanniness:** Parikkala Sculpture Park and the Silent People provide large-scale environments whose ambiguity is part of their appeal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitfinland.com/en/product/54b61c48-70c2-45b6-89be-8bc5eeea5c6b/the-silent-people/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitfinland.com">[Visit Finland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitfinland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitfinland.com.</span></span></span>
* **For contested political memory:** Nootti demonstrates how a museum can survive by changing the story told around its inherited site and objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museonootti.fi/en/the-new-museum-of-finnish-russian-relations-symbols-the-turn-in-foreign-policy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museonootti.fi">[Nootti]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museonootti.fi</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the new museum of finnish russian relations symbols the turn in foreign policy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the new museum of finnish russian relations symbols the turn in foreign policy</span></span></span>

The common thread is not a national obsession with monsters or the paranormal. It is Finland’s habit of preserving material at the boundary between private belief and public history: protective charms, confiscated sacred objects, Cold War souvenirs, obsolete games, unused bunkers and forests of handmade figures. The best collections do not ask visitors simply to believe or disbelieve. They show how an object becomes strange when its original world disappears, when its ownership is disputed, or when later generations decide that something once ignored is worth keeping.
