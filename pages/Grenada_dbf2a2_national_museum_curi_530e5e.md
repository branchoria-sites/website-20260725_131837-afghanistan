## Introduction

[Grenada]({{ 'where-does-grenada-keep-its-strangest/' | relative_url }}) National Museum is not a museum of curiosities in the traditional sense, yet it contains some of the country's most unusual public artefacts. Alongside archaeological finds and exhibits on slavery, fishing and the Grenada Revolution are a nineteenth-century rum still, equipment from Grenada's now-vanished whaling industry and a marble bathtub long claimed to have belonged to the future Empress Joséphine Bonaparte. These objects are fascinating precisely because they mix well-documented history with stories whose provenance is less certain. The museum itself adds another layer of intrigue, occupying a complex with connections to colonial administration, hotels and the island's former prison. For visitors interested in Grenada's stranger heritage, the museum is less about sensational oddities than about how ordinary historical collections can acquire legendary status over time. Recent closures and redevelopment plans have also made access less predictable than many guidebooks suggest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.gd/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.gd">[museum.gd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada National Museum About</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada National MuseumAbout - Grenada National Museum...</span></span></span>

## What Is Really Inside the Museum?

The Grenada National Museum was established in 1976 as the country's principal historical museum. Rather than specialising in one theme, it preserves collections spanning Indigenous archaeology, European colonisation, plantation society, maritime life, independence and the revolutionary period. Within that broad historical narrative are several displays that stand out as unusual even by Caribbean museum standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.gd/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.gd">[Grenada National Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada National Museum About</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada National MuseumAbout - Grenada National Museum...</span></span></span>

### Whaling, rum and archaeological collections

Among the museum's most distinctive exhibits are objects that document industries and practices now largely absent from Grenadian life.

The former whaling industry is represented by harpoons, processing equipment and related tools from the old whaling station at Glover Island. Unlike the better-known whaling museums of Iceland, Norway or New England, these displays reflect a comparatively small Caribbean industry that has largely disappeared from public memory. Their rarity makes them some of the museum's most unusual historical artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.puregrenada.com/grenada-national-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: puregrenada.com">[Grenada Tourism Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">puregrenada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada Tourism Authority Grenada National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada Tourism AuthorityGrenada National Museum - Grenada Tourism Authority...</span></span></span>

Nearby are pieces of agricultural machinery associated with sugar and rum production, including an early rum still. Although such equipment may appear industrial rather than eccentric, it illustrates the technological side of Grenada's plantation economy and links directly to the island's history of slavery, sugar cultivation and later rum production. These objects often attract attention because they survive as complete pieces of working equipment rather than as isolated archaeological fragments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.puregrenada.com/grenada-national-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: puregrenada.com">[Grenada Tourism Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">puregrenada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada Tourism Authority Grenada National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada Tourism AuthorityGrenada National Museum - Grenada Tourism Authority...</span></span></span>

The archaeological collection provides an even longer historical perspective. Pottery, stone tools and other artefacts associated with Grenada's Indigenous peoples are displayed alongside colonial collections. However, some researchers have noted that parts of the older archaeological collection have incomplete documentation, reflecting collecting practices from earlier decades rather than modern archaeological standards. That does not necessarily mean the artefacts are inauthentic, but it does mean that precise excavation histories are sometimes unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099525108232270022/pdf/P1757200f2318b0508ed00b5655fa2f0b7.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents1.worldbank.org">[World Bank]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents1.worldbank.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World BankWorld Bank DocumentMay 28, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2026</span></span></span>

## The Building Is Almost as Interesting as the Collection

Part of the museum's appeal comes from its setting rather than its displays alone.

The museum occupies one of the oldest building complexes in central St George's. Historical research undertaken by the museum itself indicates that sections of the site originated as auxiliary buildings associated with the colonial common gaol rather than the prison itself, before later becoming the Home Hotel and subsequently other commercial premises. This is more nuanced than the simplified claim that the museum simply occupies "the old prison". The actual prison buildings stood behind the current museum, around what became known as the Drill Yard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.gd/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.gd">[Grenada National Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada National Museum About</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada National MuseumAbout - Grenada National Museum...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters because many older guidebooks repeated the idea that visitors were walking through former prison cells. Museum historians have since clarified that this popular story overstated the case. The surviving prison ruins remain behind the museum complex and are separate from most exhibition spaces, although they form part of the site's wider historical landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.gd/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.gd">[Grenada National Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada National Museum About</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada National MuseumAbout - Grenada National Museum...</span></span></span>

The layered history gives the museum an atmosphere unusual for a national collection. Visitors encounter a building that has served military, judicial, hospitality and governmental purposes before becoming a museum, allowing the architecture itself to function as part of the exhibition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.gd/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.gd">[Grenada National Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada National Museum About</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada National MuseumAbout - Grenada National Museum...</span></span></span>

## Why the Marble Bathtub Is Controversial

No single object better illustrates the museum's mixture of history and legend than the famous marble bathtub traditionally linked to Joséphine Bonaparte.

Museum displays and tourism material have long described the bath as having been used by Joséphine during her childhood. The story connects to her family's ownership of estates in the French Caribbean before her marriage to Napoleon Bonaparte. As an exhibit, it is memorable because it appears unexpectedly domestic among archaeological and industrial collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.puregrenada.com/grenada-national-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: puregrenada.com">[Grenada Tourism Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">puregrenada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada Tourism Authority Grenada National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada Tourism AuthorityGrenada National Museum - Grenada Tourism Authority...</span></span></span>

The difficulty is provenance. While the bathtub itself is genuine antique marble, published evidence firmly connecting it to Joséphine is weak. Heritage researchers and museum planning documents increasingly describe it more cautiously as the "supposed" or "purported" bathtub of Joséphine rather than presenting the attribution as established fact. This shift reflects modern museum practice, which increasingly distinguishes traditional stories from documented ownership histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361495117_Re-envisioning_the_Grenada_National_Museum_a_Development_Plan_through_2026_and_beyond" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Re-envisioning the Grenada National Museum: a Development Plan through 2026 and beyond...</span></span></span>

Rather than diminishing the exhibit, the uncertainty makes it more interesting. It demonstrates how national museums sometimes preserve objects whose cultural importance lies partly in the stories communities have attached to them over generations, even when documentary proof remains incomplete.

## Why Access Has Been Uncertain

For several years, one of the biggest practical questions has not been what the museum contains but whether visitors can actually see it.

The museum closed during the COVID-19 period and subsequently became the subject of extensive redevelopment planning. Internal planning documents proposed major restoration of the historic buildings, new galleries, improved storage, reinterpretation of the collections and eventual reopening in stages. These plans also envisioned better use of the neighbouring Drill Yard and surviving prison remains as part of an expanded visitor experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361495117_Re-envisioning_the_Grenada_National_Museum_a_Development_Plan_through_2026_and_beyond" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Re-envisioning the Grenada National Museum: a Development Plan through 2026 and beyond...</span></span></span>

During this period, official information and third-party travel sites have not always agreed. Tourism promotions have continued to describe the museum as open, while the museum's own website has at times displayed prominent closure notices, and visitor reviews have reported unexpected closures or limited access. This inconsistency has understandably created confusion for travellers relying on older guidebooks or search results.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.gd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.gd">[museum.gd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.gd</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada National Museum The Grenada National Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenada National MuseumThe Grenada National Museum - Homepage...</span></span></span>

## Why It Matters in Grenada's Curiosity Landscape


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Grenada National Museum is unusual not because it displays monsters, occult objects or spectacular curiosities, but because it preserves the country's strangest surviving historical artefacts within their proper historical context.

</div>

The whaling equipment records an almost forgotten Caribbean industry. The rum still preserves the machinery behind Grenada's plantation economy. The marble bathtub illustrates how legend can become attached to museum collections without clear documentary proof. Meanwhile, the museum's own building embodies centuries of changing colonial and post-colonial uses, from military and judicial functions to hospitality and national heritage.

For readers exploring Grenada's stranger heritage, the museum serves as an essential companion to more unconventional attractions such as the [Molinere Underwater]({{ 'molinere/' | relative_url }}) Sculpture Park. One represents contemporary artistic curiosity beneath the sea; the other preserves the physical remnants of the island's complex past, including several objects whose stories remain as intriguing as the artefacts themselves.

        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
