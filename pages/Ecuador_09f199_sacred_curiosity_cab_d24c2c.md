## Introduction

[Ecuador]({{ 'what-makes-ecuadors-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s strangest collections are not always found in galleries devoted to the bizarre. Some of the country's most intriguing displays grew out of religious devotion rather than an intention to shock or entertain. Museums in Baos and Quito preserve miracle offerings, colonial images of suffering, preserved animals, domestic artefacts and sacred artworks that were once part of active spiritual lives. When these objects enter museums, they become something unusual: they remain devotional for many visitors while also becoming historical curiosities for everyone else.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Ecuador_09f199_sacred_curiosity_cab_d24c2c-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Sacred Objects illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Ecuador_09f199_sacred_curiosity_cab_d24c2c-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Ecuador_09f199_sacred_curiosity_cab_d24c2c-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This overlap between faith, memory and collecting gives Ecuador a distinctive form of "cabinet of curiosity". Instead of assembling exotic specimens from around the world, these collections gather evidence of answered prayers, miraculous survival, illness, healing and death. They reveal how ordinary people recorded extraordinary experiences, and how museums now interpret objects that still carry religious meaning for living communities.

## Why do miracle offerings become museum collections?

Across Catholic Latin America, believers have long left **votive offerings**, often called *ex-votos*, after believing that a saint or sacred image had intervened in their lives. The offering might be a handwritten testimony, a painted scene showing an accident that someone survived, jewellery, crutches, military medals, children's clothing or other personal possessions associated with the perceived miracle. Similar traditions are found across Europe and Latin America, where such gifts serve both as thanks and as public evidence of divine intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://erasmushouse.museum/en/the-unusual-objects-no-6/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: erasmushouse.museum">[Erasmus House]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">erasmushouse.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Erasmus House The unusual objects No. 6</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Erasmus HouseThe unusual objects No. 6 - Erasmus HouseMay 16, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 16, 2022</span></span></span>

Once displayed together over decades, these individual gifts become something larger than personal souvenirs. They create collective archives of local belief, preserving hundreds of stories that rarely appear in official histories. Museums therefore face an unusual challenge: the same object can simultaneously function as religious testimony, historical document and ethnographic artefact.

In Ecuador this transition is especially visible in pilgrimage centres where devotional collections gradually expanded beyond church walls into museums open to both pilgrims and tourists.

## Votive offerings at the Fray Enrique Mideros Museum

The clearest example is the **Fray Enrique Mideros Museum** in Baos de Agua Santa, one of Ecuador's best-known pilgrimage towns. Rather than concentrating on a single academic theme, the museum combines religious art, colonial objects, anthropological material and a large collection of gifts presented to the Virgin by worshippers grateful for favours they believed they had received. Local tourism authorities describe these gifts as one of the museum's defining features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.banostravel.com/museo-biblioteca" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: banostravel.com">[banostravel.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">banostravel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museo / Biblioteca Sitio Oficial de la Cmara de Turismo de Baos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo / Biblioteca Sitio Oficial de la Cmara de Turismo de Baos</span></span></span>

What makes the museum particularly unusual is the way different kinds of collections coexist. Visitors may move from displays of miracle offerings to colonial paintings, household antiques, preserved animals and historical objects within the same building. Instead of following modern museum categories, the arrangement resembles an older cabinet of curiosities, where natural specimens, sacred objects and historical artefacts sat side by side.

This mixture changes how visitors read the objects. A taxidermied animal may be viewed as natural history, while a silver devotional gift records a deeply personal religious experience. Together they produce an atmosphere where curiosity and reverence coexist rather than compete.

The museum also reflects the identity of Baos itself. The town's reputation as both a pilgrimage destination and an adventure tourism centre means that visitors often encounter miracle narratives alongside volcanoes, waterfalls and dramatic landscapes. The museum therefore preserves not only religious objects but also the cultural memory of why people have travelled to Baos for generations.

## Colonial images of suffering, saints and death

Quito's historic religious museums preserve another form of sacred curiosity. Their most striking objects were never intended to be museum exhibits. They were created to inspire devotion, repentance and meditation on mortality.

Colonial painters and sculptors working in Quito produced vivid representations of Christ's suffering, martyrdom, miracles, the Last Judgement and the lives of saints. These works often appear emotionally intense to modern audiences, with realistic wounds, dramatic facial expressions and highly theatrical compositions. Rather than celebrating violence, they formed part of the Counter-Reformation's emphasis on emotional religious experience and moral reflection. Museums interpreting seventeenth-century Quito explain how religious orders encouraged penitence and spiritual discipline through such imagery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fundacionmuseosquito.gob.ec/exposiciones/un-quito-penitencial-y-de-contrastes-socioeconomicos-siglo-xvii/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fundacionmuseosquito.gob.ec">[Museos de Quito]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fundacionmuseosquito.gob.ec</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museos de Quito Fundacin Museos de la Ciudad Quito &#124; FMC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museos de Quito Fundacin Museos de la Ciudad Quito &#124; FMC</span></span></span>

Modern visitors often experience these galleries differently from their original audiences. What once functioned primarily as devotional art can now feel uncanny, especially when displayed alongside relics, liturgical objects or funerary material. The sense of strangeness comes less from the objects themselves than from the distance between their original religious purpose and contemporary museum viewing.

The former Hospital of San Juan de Dios, today the Museum of the City, reinforces this connection between faith, illness and mortality. Because the museum occupies a building that served as a hospital for more than four centuries, exhibitions naturally connect religious care with physical suffering, medical history and changing attitudes towards death. Permanent displays reconstruct hospital life under religious orders, while temporary exhibitions have explored death from anthropological and cultural perspectives rather than treating it simply as a medical event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fundacionmuseosquito.gob.ec/exposiciones/sala-del-hospital/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fundacionmuseosquito.gob.ec">[gob.ec]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fundacionmuseosquito.gob.ec</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museos de Quito Fundacin Museos de la Ciudad Quito &#124; FMC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museos de Quito Fundacin Museos de la Ciudad Quito &#124; FMC</span></span></span>

## How devotional objects change inside museums

Moving a sacred object into a museum changes its meaning without necessarily removing its spiritual significance.

Several transformations take place at once:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Personal testimony becomes public history.** A gift once offered privately in gratitude becomes evidence of wider patterns of belief.
* **Objects gain new interpretations.** Curators explain artistic styles, materials and historical context alongside religious traditions.
* **Visitors arrive with different expectations.** Pilgrims may continue to pray before an image, while tourists may see the same object as art or cultural history.
* **Conservation replaces continual ritual use.** Museums protect fragile paintings, textiles and metal offerings that might otherwise deteriorate through constant handling.

</div>

This dual identity requires careful interpretation. Responsible museums generally avoid presenting reported miracles as historical facts while also respecting the beliefs that gave rise to the collections.

## Sacred curiosity rather than sensationalism

Unlike collections centred on alleged paranormal phenomena or deliberate oddities, Ecuador's sacred collections derive their power from sincerity. The objects are not strange because they were manufactured to attract attention. They are unusual because they preserve moments when ordinary people believed the boundary between everyday life and divine intervention had briefly disappeared.

That distinction matters. A silver heart offered after recovery from illness, a painting commemorating survival or a treasured personal object left before a revered image carries emotional weight regardless of whether a visitor shares the underlying beliefs. Museums therefore preserve not simply religious artefacts but records of hope, fear, gratitude and memory.

Within Ecuador's broader landscape of unusual collections, these devotional displays occupy a unique position. They demonstrate that curiosity does not always arise from the exotic or the mysterious. Sometimes it emerges from familiar objects whose meanings have accumulated through centuries of faith, storytelling and preservation, allowing today's visitors to encounter both a museum collection and a living religious tradition in the same space.
