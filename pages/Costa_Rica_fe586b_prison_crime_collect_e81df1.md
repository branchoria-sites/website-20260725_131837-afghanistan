## Introduction

[Costa Rica]({{ 'where-costa-rica-keeps-its-strangest/' | relative_url }}) has no large national museum devoted exclusively to crime, policing or punishment. Instead, it preserves difficult penal history through a combination of a former prison that has been transformed into a public cultural complex, a small penitentiary museum that interprets incarceration rather than glorifying it, and working judicial collections whose primary purpose remains legal evidence rather than tourism. This approach reflects a broader national preference for education, institutional memory and human rights over sensational displays.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Costa_Rica_fe586b_prison_crime_collect_e81df1-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Prisons and Crime illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Costa_Rica_fe586b_prison_crime_collect_e81df1-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Costa_Rica_fe586b_prison_crime_collect_e81df1-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For visitors interested in unusual museums, Costa Rica's prison and crime collections are therefore distinctive not because they showcase notorious criminals or gruesome artefacts, but because they reveal how the country chooses to remember confinement, criminal investigation and justice while keeping many original evidence collections under professional control.

## The Children's Museum Inside a Former Penitentiary

The most striking example of Costa Rica's approach is the **Museo de los Niños (Children's Museum)** in San José. The museum occupies the former **Central Penitentiary**, a fortress-like prison built between 1905 and 1910 that operated until its closure in 1979 after decades of overcrowding, deteriorating conditions and public criticism. Following years of abandonment, the complex was converted into the Costa Rican Centre of Science and Culture, opening as the Children's Museum in 1994.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asamblea.go.cr/ca/Lists/coleccion/DispForm.aspx?ContentTypeId=0x0100C97DC289459B304BA629D4F4451428AD&amp;ID=251" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asamblea.go.cr">[go.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asamblea.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Asamblea Legislativa de Costa Ricacoleccion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo de los NiñosMay 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2024</span></span></span>

The transformation is deliberately symbolic. Rather than demolishing the prison, Costa Rica retained its imposing walls, towers and much of its architectural character while replacing cells with educational exhibits, science displays and family attractions. Visitors therefore experience an unusual contrast: a building designed for punishment now functions as one of the country's best-known spaces for children's learning.

Unlike many prison museums elsewhere, the emphasis is not on recreating violent episodes or encouraging "dark tourism". The former prison provides historical context, but the dominant message is one of social transformation. Official accounts consistently present the building as a place where confinement gave way to education and culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asamblea.go.cr/ca/Lists/coleccion/DispForm.aspx?ContentTypeId=0x0100C97DC289459B304BA629D4F4451428AD&amp;ID=251" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asamblea.go.cr">[go.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asamblea.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Asamblea Legislativa de Costa Ricacoleccion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo de los NiñosMay 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2024</span></span></span>

## The Penitentiary Museum and the Memory of Confinement

Within the same cultural complex is the **Penitentiary Museum (Museo Penitenciario)**, which focuses specifically on the history of Costa Rica's prison system. This museum occupies preserved sections of the old prison and interprets daily life inside the Central Penitentiary, including original cells, prison routines and the changing philosophy of incarceration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asamblea.go.cr/ca/Lists/coleccion/DispForm.aspx?ContentTypeId=0x0100C97DC289459B304BA629D4F4451428AD&amp;ID=251" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asamblea.go.cr">[Asamblea Legislativa de Costa Rica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asamblea.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Asamblea Legislativa de Costa Ricacoleccion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo de los NiñosMay 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2024</span></span></span>

Rather than celebrating famous criminals, the museum concentrates on the institution itself:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* preserved prison cells showing the physical conditions of confinement;
* the evolution of Costa Rica's penal system;
* overcrowding and deteriorating living conditions that contributed to the prison's closure;
* the later conversion of the prison into a cultural and educational centre.

</div>

This interpretation aligns with Costa Rica's wider human-rights narrative. The museum acknowledges difficult aspects of prison history while avoiding theatrical reconstructions or commercialised horror.

An important example of this philosophy appeared in 2017 when the Ministry of Justice transferred doors and keys from the notorious isolation cells known as **"Las Tumbas"** ("The Tombs") at La Reforma prison to the Penitentiary Museum. These objects were preserved after the units had been closed because of long-standing criticism over their conditions and their incompatibility with modern human-rights standards. Rather than remaining symbols inside an operating prison, they became historical artefacts intended to document a period that officials described as one of the darkest chapters in Costa Rica's penitentiary history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mjp.go.cr/Comunicacion/Nota?nom=Ministra-entrego-llaves-y-puertas-de-las-Tumbas-al-Museo-Penitenciario" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mjp.go.cr">[mjp.go.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mjp.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MINISTR A ENTREGÓ LLAVES Y PUERTAS DE LAS “TUMBAS” AL MUSEO PENITENCIARIO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MINISTRA ENTREGÓ LLAVES Y PUERTAS DE LAS “TUMBAS” AL MUSEO PENITENCIARIO - Ministerio de Justicia y PazJune 22, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 22, 2017</span></span></span>

## Judicial Evidence, Virtual Access and Public Limits

Costa Rica also preserves crime-related collections through the **Organismo de Investigación Judicial (OIJ)**, the country's Judicial Investigation Agency. Here the purpose differs fundamentally from that of a museum.

The OIJ maintains both an **Object Repository** and a **Criminal Museum**, established under the agency's founding legislation in 1974. The law specifically states that the Criminal Museum exists to preserve significant objects connected with criminal investigations so they can serve as educational material for investigators and students within the judicial system. Courts may transfer confiscated weapons and other objects of lasting investigative interest after criminal proceedings have concluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pgrweb.go.cr/scij/Busqueda/Normativa/Normas/nrm_texto_completo.aspx?nValor1=1&amp;nValor2=16037&amp;nValor3=0&amp;param1=NRTC&amp;strTipM=TC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pgrweb.go.cr">[PGR Web]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pgrweb.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PGR Web Sistema Costarricense de Información Jurídica</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PGR WebSistema Costarricense de Información JurídicaJuly 14, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 14, 2026</span></span></span>

This legal framework illustrates an important distinction:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* evidence is preserved primarily for institutional learning and legal documentation;
* only selected material becomes part of the Criminal Museum;
* most seized property remains under controlled judicial custody rather than becoming public exhibits.

</div>

Consequently, Costa Rica has relatively little public access to genuine criminal evidence compared with countries that operate visitor-oriented police museums.

To improve public understanding without exposing sensitive collections, the OIJ has developed a **Virtual Museum** and online historical resources. These explain the organisation's development, notable investigations and institutional history while avoiding unrestricted display of active forensic collections or sensitive evidence. The agency also publishes historical case summaries to illustrate investigative practice rather than to sensationalise individual crimes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sitiooij.poder-judicial.go.cr/index.php/institucion/historia/museo-virtual" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sitiooij.poder-judicial.go.cr">[sitiooij.poder-judicial.go.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sitiooij.poder-judicial.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museo Virtual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo Virtual</span></span></span>

## Why Access Is Limited

Readers expecting a gallery of notorious murder weapons or extensive displays of criminal evidence may be surprised by Costa Rica's restrained approach.

Several factors explain the limited public access:

* **Legal responsibility:** many objects remain potential judicial records or must follow formal disposal procedures established by law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pgrweb.go.cr/scij/Busqueda/Normativa/Normas/nrm_texto_completo.aspx?nValor1=1&amp;nValor2=16037&amp;nValor3=0&amp;param1=NRTC&amp;strTipM=TC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pgrweb.go.cr">[PGR Web]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pgrweb.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PGR Web Sistema Costarricense de Información Jurídica</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PGR WebSistema Costarricense de Información JurídicaJuly 14, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 14, 2026</span></span></span>
* **Human rights considerations:** modern penal policy increasingly treats prison history as a subject for reflection rather than entertainment, influencing what is displayed and how it is interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mjp.go.cr/Comunicacion/Nota?nom=Ministra-entrego-llaves-y-puertas-de-las-Tumbas-al-Museo-Penitenciario" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mjp.go.cr">[mjp.go.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mjp.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MINISTR A ENTREGÓ LLAVES Y PUERTAS DE LAS “TUMBAS” AL MUSEO PENITENCIARIO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MINISTRA ENTREGÓ LLAVES Y PUERTAS DE LAS “TUMBAS” AL MUSEO PENITENCIARIO - Ministerio de Justicia y PazJune 22, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 22, 2017</span></span></span>

## What Makes Costa Rica's Crime Heritage Distinctive?

Within Costa Rica's wider landscape of unusual museums and collections, prison and crime history stands out less because of shocking artefacts than because of the way the country frames difficult history.

The Children's Museum demonstrates how a former prison can be repurposed into a national educational landmark without erasing its past. The Penitentiary Museum preserves the memory of confinement while encouraging reflection on prison reform rather than nostalgia for punishment. Meanwhile, the OIJ's Criminal Museum and evidence repositories illustrate how working judicial institutions preserve objects for professional education and legal memory, with only limited public display.

Together these institutions show that Costa Rica's crime collections are governed more by documentation, institutional accountability and historical interpretation than by spectacle. For visitors interested in unusual museums, they offer an uncommon perspective on how a country can preserve the material culture of crime and punishment while keeping the emphasis firmly on learning, remembrance and responsible stewardship.
