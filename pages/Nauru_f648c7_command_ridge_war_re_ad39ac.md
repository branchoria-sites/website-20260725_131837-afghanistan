## Introduction

Command Ridge is the closest thing [Nauru]({{ 'where-nauru-keeps-its-strangest-history/' | relative_url }}) has to an open-air war museum. Rather than displaying military artefacts inside galleries, the island's highest point preserves rusting anti-aircraft guns, concrete bunkers, command posts and defensive works where they were originally built during the Japanese occupation of the Second World War. For visitors interested in unusual military heritage, the site offers a rare chance to see an almost intact battlefield landscape rather than a reconstructed museum exhibit. At the same time, it is not a curated attraction. Interpretation is limited, many relics remain exposed to the tropical climate, and the site represents a painful history of occupation, forced labour and wartime suffering rather than military nostalgia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command_Ridge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Command Ridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Command Ridge</span></span></span>

## Is Command Ridge Nauru's Open-Air War Museum?

Calling Command Ridge an "open-air war museum" is useful as a way of understanding the experience, but it should not be taken literally. There is no staffed museum building on the ridge, no formal exhibition route and only limited interpretation. Instead, the landscape itself functions as the collection.

## Japanese occupation and island fortification

Japanese forces occupied Nauru in August 1942 and rapidly transformed the island into a fortified outpost. The highest ground at Command Ridge became the centre of military observation and air defence because it overlooked much of the island and surrounding coastline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_Nauru" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Japanese occupation of Nauru</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Japanese occupation of Nauru</span></span></span>

The occupation was not simply a military engineering project. Construction relied heavily on imported Japanese and Korean workers together with forced labour imposed on Nauruans, Chinese residents and people deported from the Gilbert Islands. Defensive works, roads and the airfield were created alongside severe restrictions on the civilian population. The surviving bunkers therefore represent both military architecture and evidence of wartime coercion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_Nauru" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Japanese occupation of Nauru</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Japanese occupation of Nauru</span></span></span>

The Japanese built an interconnected defensive network that included:

## What survives at Command Ridge today

The site's most striking relics are the large naval anti-aircraft guns, especially surviving twin 127 mm Type 89 gun mountings that remain in situ. Australian occupation forces disabled many weapons after Japan's surrender by removing breech mechanisms, but the mountings themselves survived and remain among the best-preserved examples of their type in the Pacific.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificwrecks.com/gun/127mm-type89/nauru.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificwrecks.com">[Pacific Wrecks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificwrecks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pacific WrecksPacific Wrecks - 127mm Anti-Aircraft Gun Type 89 (Nauru No. 1)April 19, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2021</span></span></span>

## Why the landscape matters more than the individual artefacts

The significance of Command Ridge lies less in the rarity of any single gun than in the survival of an entire military environment.

Across much of the Pacific, wartime equipment was dismantled, scrapped or relocated after 1945. Nauru's isolation, small population and limited redevelopment allowed substantial portions of its Japanese defensive system to remain where they were abandoned. This preserves relationships between bunkers, artillery positions, observation points and terrain that are often lost when artefacts are collected individually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificwrecks.com/people/visitors/gajda/nauru.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificwrecks.com">[Pacific Wrecks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificwrecks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pacific Wrecks Stan Gajda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pacific WrecksStan Gajda - Nauru...</span></span></span>

For historians, this helps explain how Japanese planners adapted a tiny phosphate island into a fortified military base. For visitors, it creates a more immersive experience than viewing cleaned and labelled weapons inside display cases.

The site also illustrates how industrial and military histories overlap on Nauru. Wartime defences sit within a landscape already transformed by phosphate mining, making the island's twentieth-century history visible through layers of extraction, occupation and abandonment.

## Access, safety and respectful interpretation

Command Ridge is accessible by road and track and is regularly visited by travellers exploring the island. However, it should be approached as a historic site rather than an adventure playground.

Visitors should expect uneven ground, deteriorating concrete, rusted metal and tropical vegetation. Entering confined bunkers or climbing onto artillery pieces carries obvious risks, particularly where structures have experienced decades of weathering. Although large quantities of ammunition were removed after the war, unexploded ordnance has occasionally been discovered elsewhere on Nauru during construction and excavation, reminding visitors that wartime remains should never be disturbed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificwrecks.com/location/nauru_island.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificwrecks.com">[Pacific Wrecks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificwrecks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pacific WrecksPacific Wrecks - Nauru Island, Republic of Nauru (Nauru, Nauru Island)...</span></span></span>

Respect is equally important. The surviving fortifications commemorate a period that included forced labour, civilian hardship, deportations and aerial bombardment. The attraction lies not in military spectacle but in the remarkable survival of authentic historical remains that continue to shape Nauru's understanding of its past.

Within Nauru's wider landscape of unusual heritage, Command Ridge stands apart because it functions simultaneously as an archaeological site, an outdoor military collection and a memorial embedded in the island's highest ground. Few places demonstrate so clearly how an entire wartime defensive system can survive in the open, allowing history to be read directly from the landscape rather than solely from museum displays.
