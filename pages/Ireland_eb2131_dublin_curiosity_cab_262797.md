## Introduction

Dublin’s curiosity collections show how an old-fashioned fascination with strange objects has found a modern audience. The city does not have a single historic “cabinet of curiosities” preserved intact, but it has developed several places where unusual objects, medical history, natural specimens and personal collections are brought together to explore why people collect things that are rare, unsettling or simply difficult to forget.

The clearest modern example is the Museum of Curiosities, a privately created collection presented as a contemporary cabinet of curiosities. Its displays include taxidermy, anatomical curiosities, Victorian objects, medical devices, mummified remains and other unusual artefacts. Rather than claiming that every object is a genuine mystery, the attraction uses strange items as starting points for stories about science, superstition, entertainment and changing ideas about what counts as extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumofcuriosities.ie/exhibits/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcuriosities.ie">[museumofcuriosities.ie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcuriosities.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exhibits &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exhibits &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span></span></span>

Alongside this modern collection are Dublin institutions with older collecting traditions, including natural history and medical collections that preserve specimens once used for scientific study. Together, these collections reveal a broader pattern: strange objects are rarely interesting only because they are odd. They matter because they show how societies have understood bodies, animals, belief, death and the boundaries between knowledge and imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Museums/Decorative-Arts-History/Exhibitions/Dead-Zoo-Lab" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ie">[National Museum of Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.ie.</span></span></span>

## The modern cabinet of curiosities tradition

The cabinet of curiosities was historically a way of bringing together unusual examples from nature, art, science and human invention. Before modern museums separated collections into specialised departments, collectors often displayed objects side by side because they inspired wonder and raised questions about the world. Dublin’s newest curiosity collections revive this approach by focusing less on strict classification and more on storytelling.

The Museum of Curiosities is a particularly direct example of this revival. The collection describes itself as a “living cabinet of curiosities”, built around strange artefacts and the stories attached to them. Its creator, known as Monsieur Pompier, developed the project from an earlier online collection of curious images and forgotten historical material before turning it into a physical exhibition space in Dublin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumofcuriosities.ie/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcuriosities.ie">[museumofcuriosities.ie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcuriosities.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span></span></span>

What makes this style of collecting different from a conventional museum is the relationship between object and narrative. A medical instrument, old toy or preserved specimen is not displayed simply as an isolated item. Instead, visitors are encouraged to ask why people made it, believed in it, feared it or decided it was worth preserving.

This approach places Dublin’s curiosity culture somewhere between entertainment and heritage interpretation. The collection is visitor-led and theatrical, but it also reflects a genuine historical tradition: people have long collected objects that sit between science and folklore because they reveal the limits of what was understood at a particular moment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumofcuriosities.ie/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcuriosities.ie">[museumofcuriosities.ie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcuriosities.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span></span></span>

## Medical oddities and unusual displays

Medical objects have always been among the most powerful forms of curiosity collecting because they reveal changing attitudes towards the human body. Instruments, anatomical specimens and historical treatments can appear unsettling today, but they often represent attempts by earlier generations to investigate disease, injury and anatomy.

Dublin’s curiosity landscape includes this medical dimension through both private displays and institutional collections. The Museum of Curiosities features antique medical devices and objects connected with forgotten medical practices, presenting them alongside other unusual artefacts rather than as part of a formal medical history collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumofcuriosities.ie/exhibits/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcuriosities.ie">[museumofcuriosities.ie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcuriosities.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exhibits &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exhibits &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span></span></span>

A different model can be seen in Dublin’s academic and heritage collections. The medical collections associated with surgical and anatomical institutions preserve objects for education and research rather than spectacle. These collections show how medical knowledge developed through instruments, specimens and recorded case histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcseng.ac.uk/about-the-rcs/visit-the-college/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcseng.ac.uk">[Royal College of Surgeons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcseng.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal College of Surgeons Visit the College — Royal College of Surgeons</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Royal College of Surgeons Visit the College — Royal College of Surgeons</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. A curiosity display may ask why an object fascinates the public, while a medical archive asks what the object can teach about professional practice and scientific progress. The same type of item — for example, an old surgical tool or anatomical specimen — can therefore have very different meanings depending on whether it is presented as a wonder, a historical document or a teaching resource.

## Natural specimens, taxidermy and the appeal of preserved life

Taxidermy has a special place in curiosity collections because it combines scientific preservation with emotional reactions. A preserved animal can be a research specimen, a reminder of Victorian collecting habits, an example of craftsmanship or simply an object that visitors find strange.

Dublin’s long association with unusual natural history displays is most famously represented by the National Museum of [Ireland]({{ 'where-irelands-oddest-stories-come-alive/' | relative_url }})’s Natural History Museum, widely known as the “Dead Zoo”. The collection contains thousands of specimens representing Ireland’s natural heritage and global biodiversity. While it is a scientific museum rather than a curiosity attraction, its dense displays of preserved animals connect strongly with the older tradition of collecting the unusual and unfamiliar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Museums/Natural-History" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ie">[National Museum of Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of Ireland Natural History &#124; National Museum of Ireland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of Ireland Natural History &#124; National Museum of Ireland</span></span></span>

During refurbishment of the Merrion Street building, selected material has been displayed through the Dead Zoo Lab at Collins Barracks, allowing visitors to encounter parts of the collection while the main building is closed. The project also highlights how historic specimen collections can be reinterpreted for modern audiences rather than simply preserved as relics of nineteenth-century collecting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Museums/Decorative-Arts-History/Exhibitions/Dead-Zoo-Lab" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ie">[National Museum of Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.ie.</span></span></span>

The appeal of taxidermy in curiosity culture is therefore not only about the unusual appearance of preserved animals. It also raises questions about collecting, conservation, scientific knowledge and changing attitudes towards the natural world.

## Why strange objects attract collectors

Strange objects endure because they create a personal connection with the past. A rare artefact may be historically important, but a curious object often succeeds because it creates an immediate emotional response: surprise, discomfort, amusement or fascination.

Collectors are often drawn to objects that sit outside ordinary categories. A conventional museum label might explain an object’s date, maker and function. A curiosity collection often adds another layer by asking why the object feels memorable. The interest may come from a forgotten belief, an unusual manufacturing choice, a strange historical episode or the fact that previous owners treated it as special.

Dublin’s modern curiosity collections reflect this more personal side of collecting. The Museum of Curiosities combines objects associated with folklore, medical history, Victorian culture and popular imagination, creating a collection that works through association rather than a strict academic sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumofcuriosities.ie/exhibits/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcuriosities.ie">[museumofcuriosities.ie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcuriosities.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exhibits &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exhibits &#124; Museum Of Curiosities</span></span></span>

This does not mean unusual collections reject scholarship. Instead, they operate differently from research museums. Their strength is often in opening questions: why did people once believe this object mattered? Why was it preserved? Why do modern visitors still find it fascinating?

## Dublin’s unusual objects and the wider Irish curiosity landscape

Dublin’s curiosity collections are important because they sit between several traditions found across Ireland: folklore, scientific collecting, religious heritage, archaeology and storytelling. They show that unusual objects do not need to be proven mysterious to be culturally valuable.

The most interesting objects are often those with uncertain or changing meanings. A preserved specimen can move from scientific tool to historical curiosity. A medical device can shift from practical equipment to a reminder of earlier ideas about the body. A strange object collected for amusement can become evidence of how people in the past understood fear, wonder and imagination.

For visitors, the appeal of Dublin’s curiosity collections is therefore not simply seeing something bizarre. It is seeing how objects become meaningful — through science, belief, memory and the human desire to keep hold of things that seem extraordinary.
