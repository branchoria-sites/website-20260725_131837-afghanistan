## Introduction

Across São Tomé and Príncipe, many former cocoa and coffee plantations have become something that falls between a historic site, an inhabited village and an open-air industrial collection. Rather than preserving machinery behind museum glass, these former estates leave factories, rail tracks, hospitals, workers' housing and processing equipment in the landscapes where they were originally used. Visitors walk through places where industrial technology, colonial architecture and everyday life still overlap, often with only limited interpretation. The result is an unusual form of heritage: entire plantation systems functioning as unofficial museums that reveal how a global cocoa economy shaped the islands, while also exposing the coercive labour system that sustained it. The best-known examples include Água Izé and Monte Café, although similar remains are scattered across both islands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6644" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLes roças de Monte Café, Agua-lzé e Sundy de São Tomé e Príncipe - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 30, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 30, 2023</span></span></span>

## How the estate system controlled work and daily life

The plantation, or *roça*, was far more than a farm. It was a self-contained industrial settlement designed to organise production and control labour. During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, São Tomé and Príncipe became one of the world's leading cocoa producers, and virtually every accessible part of the islands was reorganised into plantation estates linked by transport networks and processing facilities. Even after legal slavery ended, labour was largely supplied through systems of contract migration that historians describe as coercive, with workers recruited from other Portuguese African territories under harsh conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6644" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLes roças de Monte Café, Agua-lzé e Sundy de São Tomé e Príncipe - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 30, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 30, 2023</span></span></span>

Unlike many plantation landscapes elsewhere, the larger *roças* contained almost everything needed for daily life. Estates typically included:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Processing factories for coffee or cocoa.
* Warehouses and drying areas.
* Narrow-gauge railways connecting fields, factories and ports.
* Hospitals and medical facilities.
* Schools and nurseries.
* Churches and recreational buildings.
* Housing arranged according to strict social hierarchy, separating managers from administrative staff and labourers.

</div>

This concentration of industrial, residential and social infrastructure makes the plantation ruins feel less like isolated factories and more like abandoned company towns. UNESCO's nomination of several *roças* for World Heritage recognition highlights precisely this combination of industrial production and social organisation as internationally distinctive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6644" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLes roças de Monte Café, Agua-lzé e Sundy de São Tomé e Príncipe - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 30, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 30, 2023</span></span></span>

For visitors interested in unusual heritage, the fascination lies in seeing how machinery and architecture still express that hierarchy. Grand plantation houses overlook workers' settlements, factory chimneys dominate village skylines, and transport routes reveal how every movement of crops—and often of people—was carefully organised.

## Ruined hospitals, railways and processing buildings

Many estates were never converted into conventional museums after independence in 1975. Instead, economic decline left factories, warehouses and transport systems gradually deteriorating while residents continued living among them. This creates an unusual experience in which industrial archaeology remains embedded within functioning communities rather than being separated into protected heritage parks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saotome-paradise.com/discover-sao-tome/the-roca-plantations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saotome-paradise.com">[São Tomé Paradise Tours]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saotome-paradise.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">São Tomé Paradise Tours Roça Plantations in São Tomé: History, Culture &amp; Tours</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">São Tomé Paradise Tours Roça Plantations in São Tomé: History, Culture &amp; Tours</span></span></span>

The surviving features often include remarkably complete industrial landscapes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Rusting cocoa and coffee machinery left inside former processing halls.
* Narrow-gauge railway embankments and bridges once used to move crops.
* Drying platforms and warehouses where beans were prepared for export.
* Plantation hospitals that illustrate how employers controlled workers' health as part of maintaining production.
* Workers' housing blocks that remain occupied or partially reused.
* Administrative buildings whose imposing architecture contrasts sharply with labour accommodation.

</div>

Nature also plays an important role in these sites. Tropical vegetation grows through roofs, roots penetrate masonry and forest slowly reclaims industrial infrastructure. ## Água Izé: an industrial landscape still inhabited

Roça Água Izé is among the clearest examples of a plantation functioning as an open-air industrial collection rather than a formal museum. Most of its surviving buildings date from the early twentieth century, including extensive processing facilities and residential quarters originally built for plantation workers. The former estate remains integrated with the modern village, meaning visitors encounter heritage as part of everyday life rather than within fenced exhibition grounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81gua_Iz%C3%A9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Água Izé</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Água Izé</span></span></span>

Walking through Água Izé reveals how industrial production extended beyond the factory itself. Warehouses, workers' accommodation and administrative buildings collectively explain the plantation economy more effectively than a display of machinery alone. The surviving complex also illustrates how the wealth generated by cocoa exports depended upon tightly managed labour systems whose human costs are now central to historical interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6644" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLes roças de Monte Café, Agua-lzé e Sundy de São Tomé e Príncipe - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 30, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 30, 2023</span></span></span>

Because restoration has been limited, visitors should expect uneven preservation rather than polished museum displays. Some structures remain inhabited, others stand partially ruined, and interpretation varies depending on whether one visits independently or with a knowledgeable local guide.

## Monte Café: machinery, tours and uneven interpretation

Monte Café offers the closest experience to a conventional industrial museum while still preserving much of its original plantation landscape. Established in the nineteenth century as one of the islands' major coffee estates, it combines restored buildings, surviving machinery and guided visits with a living community that continues producing coffee on a smaller scale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.saotomeexpert.pt/en/sao-tome-tour-rota-do-cafe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saotomeexpert.pt">[SaoTomeExpert]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saotomeexpert.pt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sao Tome Expert Sao Tome sightseeing: tour to coffee plantation Roça Monte Café</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sao Tome Expert Sao Tome sightseeing: tour to coffee plantation Roça Monte Café</span></span></span>

The Coffee Museum occupies part of the former plantation complex and displays historic equipment, photographs and explanations of coffee processing. Guided tours usually extend beyond the museum building into the wider estate, allowing visitors to follow the route from plantation fields to drying facilities and processing machinery before ending with coffee tasting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saotome-paradise.com/itinerary/coffee-experience-excursion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saotome-paradise.com">[saotome-paradise.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saotome-paradise.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">São Tomé Paradise Tours Coffee Experience Excursion &#124; São Tomé</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">São Tomé Paradise Tours Coffee Experience Excursion &#124; São Tomé</span></span></span>

Even here, however, interpretation remains uneven. Some tours focus primarily on coffee production and contemporary farming, while others devote more attention to colonial history and the difficult conditions experienced by contracted labourers. ## Why these ruins matter as unusual collections

The plantation ruins occupy an unusual place within São Tomé and Príncipe's heritage. They are neither abandoned industrial relics nor fully curated museums. Instead, they preserve complete systems of production, transport and social control across entire landscapes.

This makes them distinctive within the country's wider curiosity culture. Rather than displaying isolated artefacts, they preserve relationships between buildings, machinery and communities. Visitors can see how factories connected to hospitals, how railways linked plantations to ports, and how residential layouts reflected colonial authority.

Current heritage initiatives increasingly treat the *roças* as cultural landscapes rather than individual monuments. International recognition efforts emphasise that their significance lies not simply in architecture or industrial technology, but in the intertwined histories of forced migration, cocoa production, colonial power and the communities that continue to inhabit these places today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/document/226090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreEvaluations of Nominations of Cultural and Mixed Properties...</span></span></span>
