## Introduction

[Samoa]({{ 'where-samoa-keeps-its-strangest-stories/' | relative_url }})'s earliest surviving artefacts are modest in appearance but extraordinary in importance. Broken pottery fragments, basalt adzes and the evidence of ancient craft techniques provide the clearest physical record of how the islands were first settled and how people built houses, carved canoes and established communities nearly 3,000 years ago. Unlike countries with vast archaeological museums, Samoa's prehistoric collections are relatively small, making each object unusually significant.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Samoa_f56806_ancient_artefacts_re_f5de90-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Ancient Artefacts illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Samoa_f56806_ancient_artefacts_re_f5de90-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Samoa_f56806_ancient_artefacts_re_f5de90-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Today, the story extends beyond museum display cases. Archaeologists continue to study the artefacts from the submerged Lapita site at Mulifanua, while heritage organisations are experimenting with recreating ancient technologies through practical workshops. The result is an unusual form of living archaeology in which ancient craftsmanship is not simply preserved but actively relearned.

## The submerged Lapita settlement at Mulifanua

The single most important archaeological discovery in Samoa emerged almost by accident. During expansion work at the Mulifanua ferry berth on Upolu in 1973, dredging uncovered thousands of pottery fragments buried beneath the lagoon floor. Later geological work showed that the settlement had originally stood on a coastal beach before gradual subsidence and changing sea levels left it underwater.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/radiocarbon-determinations-from-the-mulifanua-lapita-site-upolu-western-samoa/8B9D5F36E62589F5B3C26C870F1C90AE" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentRadiocarbon Determinations from the Mulifanua Lapita Site, Upolu, Western Samoa &#124; Radiocarbon &#124; Ca...</span></span></span>

Mulifanua remains exceptional because it is the only confirmed Samoan site to have produced decorated dentate-stamped Lapita pottery. More than 4,000 pottery sherds were recovered together with two stone adzes, making the site the easternmost confirmed occurrence of classic Lapita ceramics in the Pacific. Radiocarbon dating places occupation at roughly 2880–2750 years before present (around 930–800 BC), firmly linking Samoa to the great expansion of Lapita seafarers across Remote Oceania.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/radiocarbon-determinations-from-the-mulifanua-lapita-site-upolu-western-samoa/8B9D5F36E62589F5B3C26C870F1C90AE" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentRadiocarbon Determinations from the Mulifanua Lapita Site, Upolu, Western Samoa &#124; Radiocarbon &#124; Ca...</span></span></span>

The collection is unusual partly because so little survives. Rather than complete pots, archaeologists work with broken fragments whose stamped patterns, clay composition and manufacturing techniques reveal migration routes, trade connections and technological knowledge. Every sherd therefore contributes to reconstructing an otherwise largely invisible prehistoric society.

The site also illustrates the limits of archaeological certainty. Because the artefacts were recovered during dredging rather than a planned excavation, parts of the original context were disturbed before researchers could document them fully. This has led to decades of debate over precise dating, settlement duration and the relationship between decorated Lapita pottery and the later undecorated ceramics found elsewhere in Samoa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X16300463" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Refining the chronology for west polynesian colonization: New data from the Samoan archipelago - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## What stone adzes reveal about Samoan life

If Lapita pottery tells archaeologists who first reached Samoa, stone adzes reveal how those communities actually lived.

A basalt adze was one of the most valuable tools in prehistoric Samoa. Before metal, these carefully shaped cutting tools made it possible to fell trees, hollow canoe hulls, construct houses and produce ceremonial carvings. Their quality directly affected the efficiency of daily life.

Archaeologists study adzes in several ways:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Stone source:** Geochemical analysis can identify where the basalt originated, revealing exchange networks between islands.
* **Manufacturing style:** Grinding marks, polishing and hammer dressing show how different communities shaped their tools.
* **Wear patterns:** Damage to cutting edges indicates whether an adze was mainly used for woodworking, heavy construction or finer carving.

</div>

The two adzes from Mulifanua are especially informative because they differ in form. One displays hammer dressing more commonly associated with eastern Lapita traditions elsewhere in the Pacific, suggesting technological connections beyond Samoa itself, while the other resembles forms that later became characteristic of Samoan stone-working traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sadil.ws/bitstream/handle/123456789/4598/Archaeology_in_Samoa_The_Pulemelei_Inves.pdf?sequence=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sadil.ws">[sadil.ws]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sadil.ws</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archaeology in Samoa – the Pulemelei Investigations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 2, 2025</span></span></span>

Later archaeological collections across Samoa contain many more plain stone adzes than decorated pottery. This imbalance reflects a broader cultural transition. Decorated ceramics disappear relatively early from the archaeological record, whereas expertly made stone tools continued to evolve for many centuries. Consequently, stone artefacts often provide the strongest evidence for understanding changing craft traditions after the Lapita period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X16300463" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Refining the chronology for west polynesian colonization: New data from the Samoan archipelago - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

For museum visitors these objects can seem surprisingly plain. Their importance lies less in visual spectacle than in the information they preserve about technology, resource use and everyday work in prehistoric Polynesia.

## Tiapapata and the revival of traditional making

Ancient technologies are increasingly being treated as practical skills rather than purely archaeological subjects.

The Tiapapata Art Centre has developed heritage programmes that reconnect modern Samoans with traditional forms of making, including stone-tool production, pottery inspired by Lapita traditions, fibre work and other historic crafts. Rather than claiming uninterrupted continuity with prehistoric methods, the centre presents these activities as cultural revival informed by archaeological research, experimentation and collaboration with artists and communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritage.creativesamoa.com/crafts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritage.creativesamoa.com">[Reviving Samoan Cultural Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritage.creativesamoa.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reviving Samoan Cultural Heritage Crafts &#124; Reviving Samoan Cultural Heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reviving Samoan Cultural Heritage Crafts &#124; Reviving Samoan Cultural Heritage</span></span></span>

This approach changes the role of archaeological collections. Instead of serving only as museum specimens, ancient artefacts become teaching resources that help answer practical questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* How were stone adzes shaped without metal?<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.creativesamoa.com/stone-adzes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: creativesamoa.com">[creativesamoa.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">creativesamoa.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* Which local materials produce workable clay?
* How were pottery vessels formed and fired?
* Which traditional techniques remain useful for contemporary crafts?

</div>

Experimental reconstruction cannot prove every detail of ancient manufacture, but it allows researchers and craftspeople to test archaeological interpretations against real materials. Failed experiments can be just as informative as successful ones, revealing which techniques are unlikely to have been used in the past.

For visitors, workshops also make prehistoric technology more accessible. A polished basalt adze or reconstructed pottery vessel is easier to understand after watching the labour required to reproduce it.

## Why these collections matter

Samoa's archaeological collections differ from many famous museum treasures because their value lies in interpretation rather than rarity alone.

The Mulifanua pottery fragments anchor Samoa within one of humanity's great episodes of oceanic exploration. Stone adzes demonstrate how those first settlers transformed forests into villages and voyaging canoes. Modern heritage projects then complete the story by asking whether ancient knowledge can still be practised rather than merely displayed.

Together, these artefacts illustrate a distinctive feature of Samoa's curiosity culture. The country's most remarkable collections are not cabinets of mysterious relics but carefully studied fragments of ordinary tools and pottery whose significance grows through research, reconstruction and living craftsmanship. They show that some of the most compelling archaeological objects are not the most spectacular to look at, but the ones that continue to teach new generations how an ancient society actually worked.

      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
