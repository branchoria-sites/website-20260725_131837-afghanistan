## Introduction

Buganda royal objects are unusual museum pieces because they are not simply historical artefacts. Royal drums, stools, spears, garments, and ceremonial items connected with the Buganda Kingdom continue to exist within a living system of authority, ritual, memory, and identity. For visitors interested in [Uganda]({{ 'where-ugandas-strangest-collections/' | relative_url }})’s unusual collections, these objects sit in an unusual middle ground: they can be preserved in museums, researched by scholars, displayed for tourists, and still remain meaningful to communities that regard them as part of an ongoing heritage tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museums.tourism.go.ug/collections/ethnology-cultural-heritage-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.tourism.go.ug">[Uganda Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.tourism.go.ug</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uganda Museums Ethnology &amp; Cultural Heritage Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uganda MuseumsEthnology &amp; Cultural Heritage Collection - Uganda Museums...</span></span></span>

The debate around Buganda royal objects is therefore not only about where an artefact is stored. It is about who has the right to interpret it, care for it, access it, and decide whether it is a museum object, a sacred item, a symbol of political identity, or all of these at once. The Uganda [National Museum]({{ 'national-museum-8dd206/' | relative_url }})’s ethnology collection includes royal regalia such as drums, stools, spears, and ceremonial garments, while noting that some objects remain connected to community stewardship arrangements with Buganda Palace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museums.tourism.go.ug/collections/ethnology-cultural-heritage-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.tourism.go.ug">[Uganda Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.tourism.go.ug</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uganda Museums Ethnology &amp; Cultural Heritage Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uganda MuseumsEthnology &amp; Cultural Heritage Collection - Uganda Museums...</span></span></span>

## Drums, regalia, and symbols of authority

Among the most recognisable Buganda royal objects are drums. In Buganda tradition, drums have long carried meanings beyond music: they can announce royal events, mark ceremonies, and represent the presence and continuity of the kingdom. The royal drum house at the Kasubi Tombs, known as Ndoga-Obukaba, remains one of the clearest examples of how an object collection can function as a living heritage space rather than a static display. The drum house sits within a wider ceremonial landscape that includes the entrance gatehouse, courtyards, and the main tomb building of the Kasubi royal complex.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kasubitombs.org/en/description/overview.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kasubitombs.org">[Kasubi Tombs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kasubitombs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kasubi Tombs Site Overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kasubi Tombs Site Overview</span></span></span>

The Uganda National Museum holds examples of Buganda royal drums, including the engalabi, a long ceremonial drum recorded in its collections. These objects are catalogued as heritage artefacts, but their significance comes from their relationship with royal practice, craftsmanship, and historical memory rather than their physical form alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museums.tourism.go.ug/collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.tourism.go.ug">[Uganda Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.tourism.go.ug</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uganda Museums Collections Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uganda MuseumsCollections Archive - Uganda Museums...</span></span></span>

Royal regalia also includes objects associated with kingship and public authority. Stools, spears, garments, and other ceremonial items communicate ideas of legitimacy and continuity. In many museum settings, such objects might be interpreted mainly through dates, materials, and makers. In Buganda, however, their meaning is tied to institutions and relationships that continue into the present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museums.tourism.go.ug/collections/ethnology-cultural-heritage-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.tourism.go.ug">[Uganda Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.tourism.go.ug</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uganda Museums Ethnology &amp; Cultural Heritage Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uganda MuseumsEthnology &amp; Cultural Heritage Collection - Uganda Museums...</span></span></span>

This creates a distinctive challenge for collections managers. A royal drum displayed behind glass can be historically informative, but a drum understood within Buganda traditions may also represent a connection between generations, ceremonies, and political memory. The object’s meaning does not disappear when it enters a collection; instead, museums must decide how to represent meanings that extend beyond the display case.

## Sacred meaning beyond museum display

The strongest example of living heritage connected to Buganda royal objects is the Kasubi Tombs in Kampala. The site is a burial place for four Buganda kings and is recognised by UNESCO as a place whose importance lies not only in architecture but in continuing beliefs, spirituality, rituals, and identity. UNESCO describes Kasubi as a major spiritual centre for the Baganda and emphasises the continuation of cultural practices associated with the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1022" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Tombs of Buganda Kings at Kasubi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreTombs of Buganda Kings at Kasubi - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Kasubi demonstrates why royal heritage can be difficult to separate into “museum objects” and “religious objects”. The site’s buildings, drums, ritual spaces, and associated traditions form a connected heritage environment. A visitor does not encounter isolated artefacts but a cultural system in which objects gain meaning from ceremonies and community knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1022/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Tombs of Buganda Kings at Kasubi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreTombs of Buganda Kings at Kasubi - UNESCO World Heritage Centre</span></span></span>

The 2010 fire at Kasubi highlighted these issues dramatically. The main tomb building was severely damaged, leading to a major restoration programme supported by Uganda, UNESCO, and international partners. The site was later removed from UNESCO’s List of World Heritage in Danger in 2023 after restoration work was completed. The recovery process was not simply a reconstruction of an old building; it involved restoring a place where traditional practices and skills remained important.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/2607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreUganda’s Tombs of the Kings of Buganda at Kasubi removed from the List of World Heritage in Danger - UNESCO W...</span></span></span>

For heritage specialists, Kasubi is a useful reminder that authenticity is not always about keeping an object untouched. In living traditions, rebuilding, maintaining, performing, and passing knowledge between generations can be part of what makes heritage authentic. UNESCO’s assessment of Kasubi places particular importance on continuity of cultural practices alongside the physical site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1022/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Tombs of Buganda Kings at Kasubi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreTombs of Buganda Kings at Kasubi - UNESCO World Heritage Centre</span></span></span>

## Community stewardship and heritage debates

The central debate around Buganda royal objects is whether heritage should be treated mainly as a collection of things or as a relationship between objects, people, and traditions. Museums traditionally preserve objects by controlling storage conditions, documentation, and public access. Communities may place greater emphasis on custodianship, ritual responsibilities, and maintaining connections between objects and descendants.

Uganda’s museum collections already reflect a more complex model than simple ownership. The Uganda Museums collection description states that some cultural objects remain under community stewardship arrangements with Buganda Palace. This suggests a shared approach in which national collections and traditional institutions both have roles in caring for heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museums.tourism.go.ug/collections/ethnology-cultural-heritage-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museums.tourism.go.ug">[Uganda Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museums.tourism.go.ug</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uganda Museums Ethnology &amp; Cultural Heritage Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uganda MuseumsEthnology &amp; Cultural Heritage Collection - Uganda Museums...</span></span></span>

The wider international debate about colonial collecting also affects how royal objects are discussed. Many African heritage institutions and communities have questioned whether objects collected during colonial periods were removed under unequal conditions and whether modern museums should reconsider their relationships with source communities. Similar debates elsewhere, including discussions around royal collections in Africa, have encouraged museums to consider partnerships, returns, loans, and shared authority rather than treating collections as permanently detached from their origins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vam.ac.uk/info/restitution-and-repatriation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vam.ac.uk">[Victoria and Albert Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Victoria and Albert Museum Restitution and repatriation · V&amp;A</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Victoria and Albert Museum Restitution and repatriation · V&amp;A</span></span></span>

For Buganda objects, however, the debate is not only about colonial history. It is also about the practical question of how a living kingdom and a modern state museum can work together. A royal drum may be valuable as a historical record, but it may also remain part of a cultural landscape where knowledge, ceremony, and identity continue.

<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Buganda royal objects are unusual because they challenge the usual idea of a museum collection. They are not curiosities because they are mysterious or exotic; they are curious because they resist being reduced to ordinary exhibits. A drum, stool, or ceremonial garment can simultaneously be an artwork, a historical document, a political symbol, and part of a living tradition.
