## Introduction

The Great [Zimbabwe]({{ 'where-zimbabwes-strangest-histories-come/' | relative_url }}) Birds are among Zimbabwe’s most recognisable heritage objects: carved soapstone sculptures that transformed from sacred-looking objects at an ancient royal centre into symbols of a modern nation. Found at the Great Zimbabwe archaeological site near Masvingo, the birds are unusual because their meaning is not fixed. They have been interpreted as signs of royal power, links to ancestors, religious objects, and symbols of political identity, yet no surviving written record gives a definitive explanation of what they meant to the people who created them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/great-zimbabwe-11th-15th-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Great Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtGreat Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century) - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 1, 2001...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2001</span></span></span>

For visitors interested in unusual collections and historical mysteries, the Zimbabwe Birds are compelling because the mystery is genuine rather than manufactured. The uncertainty comes from the limits of [archaeology]({{ 'archaeology/' | relative_url }}): the carvings survive, but the original ceremonies, stories and beliefs surrounding them are only partly recoverable. Today, the birds sit at the intersection of archaeology, spirituality, colonial collecting and national identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greatzimbabwewhs.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greatzimbabwewhs.com">[greatzimbabwewhs.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greatzimbabwewhs.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Great Zimbabwe World Heritage Site – Great Zimbabwe World Heritage Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Great Zimbabwe World Heritage Site – Great Zimbabwe World Heritage Site</span></span></span>

## Ancient carvings and their discovery

The soapstone birds were created at Great Zimbabwe, a major stone-built settlement occupied mainly between the 11th and 15th centuries. The site was a centre of political authority, trade and ritual life, with monumental stone structures that remain among the most important archaeological achievements in southern Africa. Great Zimbabwe became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/364" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Great Zimbabwe National Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreGreat Zimbabwe National Monument - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

The sculptures are distinctive because they combine bird-like and human-like features. Some have carved eyes, beaks and wings, while their proportions and details do not represent a simple naturalistic bird. Scholars have noted that the figures blend avian forms with human characteristics, creating symbolic objects rather than ordinary animal sculptures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/great-zimbabwe-11th-15th-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Great Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtGreat Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century) - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 1, 2001...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2001</span></span></span>

Several birds were found in the Hill Complex, particularly in the Eastern Enclosure, where they appear to have been associated with important spaces rather than ordinary domestic areas. Their original arrangement is uncertain because archaeological disturbance and early collecting damaged much of the context that might have explained their purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/great-zimbabwe-11th-15th-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Great Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtGreat Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century) - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 1, 2001...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2001</span></span></span>

The earliest modern investigations of Great Zimbabwe were shaped by colonial attitudes. European explorers and excavators often struggled to accept that an African society had built such a large and complex stone settlement. Some promoted theories involving foreign builders, despite later archaeological work demonstrating the site’s African origins. The soapstone birds became caught up in this wider struggle over who had the right to define the history of Great Zimbabwe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/great-zimbabwe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: education.nationalgeographic.org">[National Geographic Education]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">education.nationalgeographic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Geographic Education Great Zimbabwe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic EducationGreat ZimbabweOctober 19, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2023</span></span></span>

### A collection shaped by removal and return

The birds also have a history beyond the ruins themselves. During the colonial period, some sculptures were removed from Great Zimbabwe and entered overseas collections. One example recorded by the British Museum was removed during an early excavation connected with James Theodore Bent’s work after Cecil Rhodes commissioned investigations at the site. Several complete birds were later returned to Zimbabwe after independence, although the wider history of collecting remains part of continuing debates about heritage ownership.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_CRS-73" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumcast; sculpture &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumcast; sculpture &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

This history makes the birds unusual museum objects: they are not simply archaeological artefacts displayed for curiosity. They are objects whose physical movement across borders became part of their meaning.

## Symbols, beliefs and changing interpretations

The central mystery of the Zimbabwe Birds is what they originally represented. Archaeologists generally agree that they were important symbolic objects, but interpretations differ because there is limited direct evidence from the period when Great Zimbabwe flourished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/great-zimbabwe-11th-15th-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Great Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtGreat Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century) - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 1, 2001...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2001</span></span></span>

One influential interpretation connects the birds with royal authority. Because they were associated with elite spaces, some scholars have suggested that they represented rulers, ancestors or spiritual connections supporting kingship. The bird may have symbolised a link between political leadership and the supernatural world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/great-zimbabwe-11th-15th-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Great Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtGreat Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century) - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 1, 2001...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2001</span></span></span>

Another approach emphasises religious significance. UNESCO describes the Zimbabwe Birds as evidence that Great Zimbabwe functioned as a place of worship, with the sculptures linked to continuing spiritual importance at the site. The exact beliefs behind them cannot be reconstructed with certainty, but their placement suggests that they belonged to a world where political power, sacred practice and ancestry were closely connected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/364" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Great Zimbabwe National Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreGreat Zimbabwe National Monument - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Some modern interpretations have attempted to identify the bird species represented, with suggestions including birds of prey such as the bateleur eagle or fish eagle. However, identifying the animal alone does not solve the larger question of meaning. A carved bird at a royal centre could carry symbolic meanings that go beyond the creature itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://smarthistory.org/great-zimbabwe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smarthistory.org">[Smarthistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smarthistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">– Great Zimbabwe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smarthistory – Great ZimbabweJune 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2026</span></span></span>

The uncertainty is part of what makes the birds historically important. Unlike a written inscription that states “this object represents X”, the carvings require interpretation through archaeology, comparison with later traditions and careful study of the site.

## Colonial removal and modern identity

Few archaeological objects show the transformation from ancient artefact to national emblem as clearly as the Great Zimbabwe Birds. During colonial rule, the sculptures were removed from their original setting and became objects studied and displayed through colonial institutions. After independence in 1980, the same images became central symbols of Zimbabwean identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_CRS-73" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumcast; sculpture &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumcast; sculpture &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

The Zimbabwe Bird appears on the national flag and other state symbols, turning an uncertain ancient image into a clear modern message: a connection between contemporary Zimbabwe and the achievements of the civilisation that built Great Zimbabwe. The symbol also appears on coins and official designs, reinforcing its role as a national emblem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumcoin &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumcoin &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

This creates an interesting tension. The modern meaning of the bird is widely understood, but its ancient meaning remains debated. The state uses it as a symbol of heritage and independence, while scholars continue to examine what the original communities intended. The object therefore carries two histories at once: the history of Great Zimbabwe itself and the modern history of reclaiming African heritage after colonial interpretations challenged local authorship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/great-zimbabwe-11th-15th-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Great Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtGreat Zimbabwe (11th–15th Century) - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 1, 2001...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2001</span></span></span>

For visitors, this is what makes the birds different from ordinary museum curiosities. Their mystery is not based on claims of the unexplained. Instead, it comes from a real historical gap between a surviving object and a lost world of meanings.

## Seeing the birds today

The Great Zimbabwe Birds are closely connected with the Great Zimbabwe National Monument and its site museum, managed by the National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe. The experience is unusual because the landscape itself is part of the collection: the stone walls, archaeological remains and surrounding environment provide the setting needed to understand why the carvings mattered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nmmz.co.zw/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nmmz.co.zw">[NMMZ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nmmz.co.zw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museums &amp; Monuments of ZimbabweDecember 10, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2019</span></span></span>

Visitors should expect a heritage site rather than a conventional gallery of isolated objects. The birds are best understood alongside the architecture of Great Zimbabwe, where royal authority, trade, ritual and memory came together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.greatzimbabwewhs.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greatzimbabwewhs.com">[greatzimbabwewhs.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greatzimbabwewhs.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Great Zimbabwe World Heritage Site – Great Zimbabwe World Heritage Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Great Zimbabwe World Heritage Site – Great Zimbabwe World Heritage Site</span></span></span>

The lasting appeal of the Zimbabwe Birds lies in their ability to hold several meanings at once. They are archaeological evidence, sacred heritage objects, contested colonial-era collections and modern national symbols. Their unanswered questions are not weaknesses; they are the reason these small stone sculptures continue to attract attention as some of Zimbabwe’s most powerful cultural curiosities.
