## Introduction

Today, these collections sit at the centre of a wider debate about the treatment of Indigenous human remains. The question is no longer simply how such objects should be displayed, but whether they should remain in foreign museums at all. For the Solomon Islands, the discussion combines issues of cultural heritage, colonial history, museum ethics and the practical realities of repatriation.

## Why ancestral skulls are different from ordinary museum objects

In parts of the western Solomon Islands, particularly around the New Georgia group, the skulls of respected ancestors were preserved after death and incorporated into shrine traditions. Some were overmodelled with black vegetable putty, fitted with shell eyes, decorated with fibre hair and placed alongside other sacred objects. They were understood to retain aspects of ancestral power and formed part of the spiritual life of particular descent groups rather than serving as memorial displays.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1902" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumhuman remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumhuman remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

This cultural role distinguishes them from archaeological skeletons or anonymous historical remains. In many descendant communities, these skulls are still regarded as ancestors rather than artefacts. That distinction has become increasingly important in museum policy, where human remains are now often treated under different ethical standards from other cultural collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/human-remains" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Complicating matters further is the uncertainty surrounding some specimens. Museum records acknowledge that while many overmodelled skulls probably belonged to important ancestors, others may have been enemy trophy skulls or objects produced for sale to European collectors after outside demand encouraged a commercial market. This means that not every specimen has an identical history or cultural meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1902" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumhuman remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumhuman remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## How Solomon Islands skulls entered overseas collections

Many ancestral skulls left the Solomon Islands during a period of intense colonial collecting between the 1870s and the early twentieth century.

The circumstances varied considerably:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Some were purchased from local intermediaries.
* Others were acquired by resident traders.
* Some may have been removed from abandoned or poorly protected shrines.
* Museum documentation occasionally admits that European demand itself encouraged the removal or manufacture of ceremonial objects for export.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1902" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumhuman remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumhuman remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

</div>

One well-documented example in the British Museum is an overmodelled skull from Ranongga Island, donated in 1902 by trader Frank Wickham. Museum records note both its probable ritual significance and scholarly uncertainty over whether comparable skulls in museum collections always originated from recognised ancestral shrines. The object is no longer publicly illustrated online because of contemporary cultural restrictions relating to images of human remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1902" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumhuman remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumhuman remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Other Solomon Islands human remains entered museums through naval expeditions, scientific collecting and colonial-era acquisitions, including carved coffins containing skulls from islands such as Owaraha (Santa Ana).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumcoffin; human skeletal remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 21, 1904...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 1904</span></span></span>

## What makes repatriation especially complex?

Unlike famous disputes over monumental sculptures or looted paintings, ancestral skulls involve both cultural property and human remains.

That creates several overlapping questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Who has authority to request their return?** Modern nation states, provincial governments, customary landowners and descendant clans may all have legitimate interests.
* **Can an individual skull be linked to a particular community?** Documentation is often incomplete or uses outdated geographical names.
* **Were the remains obtained with meaningful consent?** Records from the colonial period rarely meet today's ethical expectations.
* **Should all human remains automatically be returned?** Museums and governments differ in how they answer this question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/human-remains" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

</div>

For the Solomon Islands, identifying the appropriate receiving community can be particularly difficult because collections were assembled over more than a century, sometimes with limited documentation about their original shrine or lineage.

## Museum attitudes have changed

The debate today differs markedly from the attitudes that shaped early museum collecting.

During the late nineteenth century, many museums displayed ancestral skulls primarily as ethnographic curiosities or evidence of supposedly "exotic" customs. Their religious importance was often minimised, while headhunting attracted disproportionate attention.

Modern museum practice has shifted significantly. Many institutions now:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* recognise ancestral remains as culturally sensitive;
* restrict photography or online publication;
* remove some human remains from permanent display;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/human-remains" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>
* consult descendant communities about appropriate care;
* publish policies explaining how repatriation requests are assessed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/human-remains" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

</div>

The British Museum's current policy, for example, distinguishes human remains from ordinary artefacts and sets out procedures through which communities may seek the transfer of remains that fall within the scope of UK legislation. The museum also continues to research the provenance of its collections, acknowledging that acquisition histories can change as new evidence emerges.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/human-remains" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## Has the Solomon Islands sought widespread repatriation?

Compared with countries such as Australia, New Zealand or several Native American nations, the Solomon Islands has not pursued a large, internationally publicised programme focused specifically on ancestral skull repatriation.

That does not necessarily indicate a lack of interest. Instead, several practical factors shape the situation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* many collections remain incompletely documented;
* customary authority may rest with particular communities rather than the national government alone;
* conservation facilities and museum resources within the Solomon Islands are more limited than in wealthier countries;
* public attention has often focused on broader cultural heritage priorities alongside development needs.

</div>

As a result, discussion has tended to remain within wider international debates about Indigenous human remains rather than generating numerous headline legal disputes centred on Solomon Islands collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/human-remains" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Human remains &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## Why the debate matters beyond ownership

The strongest arguments for repatriation are not primarily financial or nationalistic. Instead, they focus on restoring dignity to ancestors whose remains were collected during unequal colonial relationships.

For many Indigenous communities worldwide, human remains are not historical specimens but family members and spiritual beings whose continued storage overseas interrupts customary obligations. Recent international repatriations—from the Pacific, Africa and elsewhere—have reinforced this broader ethical movement, even though each case depends on its own legal and cultural circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/vanuatu-ancestral-relics-trafficked-art-new-york-return-home-with-fbi-escort-2024-08-29/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[reuters.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell. Museums, meanwhile, often argue that collections also serve educational and research purposes and that each claim requires careful examination of provenance, legal ownership and the wishes of claimant communities. ## What visitors should understand today

Visitors encountering Solomon Islands ancestral skulls in museums should view them less as macabre curiosities than as evidence of a profound shift in museum ethics.

A century ago they were commonly interpreted through the lens of colonial anthropology and fascination with headhunting. Today they are more likely to be presented as sacred ancestral remains whose removal raises difficult questions about consent, colonial collecting and cultural responsibility.

For anyone exploring the Solomon Islands' unusual heritage, these skulls illustrate that the country's most remarkable collections are not simply about strange objects. They are also about changing ideas of respect, ownership and the responsibilities museums owe to the communities whose ancestors became part of their collections.

    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
