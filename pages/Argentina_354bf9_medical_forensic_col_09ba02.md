## Introduction

[Argentina]({{ 'what-makes-argentinas-strangest-museums/' | relative_url }})'s most unsettling medical collections are not designed as horror attractions. Instead, they exist inside working academic and judicial institutions where preserved human remains, pathological specimens and forensic evidence serve teaching, research and legal investigation. In Buenos Aires especially, these collections blur the line between museum, laboratory and archive. Some can be visited through guided tours or special arrangements, while others remain largely inaccessible because they continue to support medical education or forensic practice.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Argentina_354bf9_medical_forensic_col_09ba02-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Medical Museums illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Argentina_354bf9_medical_forensic_col_09ba02-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Argentina_354bf9_medical_forensic_col_09ba02-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Their unusual character comes not from sensationalism but from purpose. Every preserved organ, congenital abnormality or forensic specimen represents a real patient or legal case. Modern curators increasingly emphasise that these collections should be understood as historical records of disease, changing medical knowledge and professional training rather than spectacles of death. At the same time, they raise difficult questions about consent, dignity, privacy and whether specimens collected under older medical practices should continue to be displayed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fmed.uba.ar/index.php/departamentos_y_catedras/museo-de-patologia/patrimonio" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fmed.uba.ar">[uba.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fmed.uba.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Patrimonio &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Patrimonio &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span></span></span>

## Pathology specimens as medical teaching tools

The best-known example is the Museum of Pathology at the University of Buenos Aires Faculty of Medicine. Founded in 1887 alongside the university's pathology department, it is regarded as the university's oldest museum and one of Argentina's most significant historical pathology collections. Rather than presenting curiosities for entertainment, the museum documents the development of disease before modern treatments transformed medicine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fmed.uba.ar/index.php/departamentos_y_catedras/museo-de-patologia/historia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fmed.uba.ar">[fmed.uba.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fmed.uba.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historia &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historia &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span></span></span>

Its collection contains around 2,000 anatomical pathology specimens on display, with a similar number held in storage. Many originated from autopsies carried out at the Hospital de Clínicas and were later supplemented by material transferred from other Buenos Aires hospitals whose own teaching museums disappeared during the twentieth century. The result is an unusually comprehensive record of disease patterns across several generations of Argentine medicine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fmed.uba.ar/index.php/departamentos_y_catedras/museo-de-patologia/patrimonio" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fmed.uba.ar">[fmed.uba.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fmed.uba.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Patrimonio &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Patrimonio &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span></span></span>

Among the most striking exhibits are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Preserved organs showing the progression of major diseases.
* Congenital malformations and teratology specimens used in medical education.
* Obstetric pathology collections.
* Animal pathology used for comparative study.
* Rare or now largely eradicated infectious diseases, including smallpox and yellow fever.

</div>

These displays are unsettling because they preserve conditions that modern doctors may rarely encounter. Instead of illustrating textbook diagrams, they preserve the physical evidence of diseases that shaped Argentina's public health history before vaccination programmes, antibiotics and improved sanitation dramatically reduced many of them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fmed.uba.ar/index.php/departamentos_y_catedras/museo-de-patologia/patrimonio" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fmed.uba.ar">[fmed.uba.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fmed.uba.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Patrimonio &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Patrimonio &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span></span></span>

The museum's importance extends beyond preserved organs. Its holdings include nineteenth-century medical books, historical photographs, autopsy protocols dating back to 1898, microscope collections, teaching slides and glass photographic negatives that document the evolution of pathology as a scientific discipline. Together these archives make the museum as much a historical research centre as a specimen collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fmed.uba.ar/index.php/departamentos_y_catedras/museo-de-patologia/patrimonio" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fmed.uba.ar">[fmed.uba.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fmed.uba.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Patrimonio &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Patrimonio &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span></span></span>

## Forensic collections inside a working morgue

An even more unusual institution operates within Argentina's National Judicial Morgue in Buenos Aires. The Forensic Museum "Dr Juan Bautista Bafico", established in 1935, forms part of the country's principal medico-legal service rather than an independent museum. It was created primarily to train forensic physicians, judges and specialists involved in criminal investigations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csjn.gov.ar/cmfcs/files/museo.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csjn.gov.ar">[csjn.gov.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csjn.gov.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cuerpo Medico Forense</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Argentina...</span></span></span>

Its collections differ significantly from university pathology museums. Instead of illustrating disease, they focus on injuries, trauma and medico-legal interpretation. Preserved anatomical specimens demonstrate wounds, fractures and other forensic findings, while plaster casts, photographs and specialised teaching materials help explain how forensic experts distinguish natural death, accidents and criminal violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csjn.gov.ar/cmfcs/files/museo.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csjn.gov.ar">[csjn.gov.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csjn.gov.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cuerpo Medico Forense</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Argentina...</span></span></span>

Because the museum sits inside an operational forensic institution, visitors encounter an environment where education remains closely connected to contemporary casework. The surrounding departments continue to carry out autopsies, forensic imaging, anthropology and laboratory examinations for the justice system. The museum therefore functions as an educational extension of an active medico-legal service rather than a historical curiosity preserved in isolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csjn.gov.ar/cmfcs/files/museo.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csjn.gov.ar">[csjn.gov.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csjn.gov.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cuerpo Medico Forense</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Argentina...</span></span></span>

This institutional setting also explains why public access has traditionally been more limited than conventional museums. Guided visits generally require advance booking and are organised around educational purposes rather than casual tourism. The institution itself warns that many images and specimens may be unsuitable for children or sensitive visitors because they depict genuine forensic injuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csjn.gov.ar/cmfcs/files/museo.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csjn.gov.ar">[csjn.gov.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csjn.gov.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cuerpo Medico Forense</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Argentina...</span></span></span>

## Why these collections remain largely behind closed doors

Unlike many unusual museums elsewhere, Buenos Aires' medical collections deliberately limit access for practical and ethical reasons.

First, many remain integrated into working universities or forensic services. Teaching, diagnosis and legal investigations continue alongside historical preservation, making unrestricted public access difficult.

Second, human remains require careful ethical handling. Specimens collected during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries were often acquired under standards that differ markedly from modern expectations regarding informed consent. Contemporary curators increasingly frame these collections through medical history and education rather than spectacle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fmed.uba.ar/index.php/departamentos_y_catedras/departamento-de-patologia/museo-de-patologia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fmed.uba.ar">[fmed.uba.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fmed.uba.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museo de Patología &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo de Patología &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span></span></span>

Third, forensic institutions have additional legal responsibilities. Evidence connected to criminal investigations, personal records and medico-legal documentation must be protected by confidentiality rules. The museum represents only a carefully selected educational component of a much larger working forensic system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csjn.gov.ar/cmfcs/files/museo.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csjn.gov.ar">[csjn.gov.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csjn.gov.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cuerpo Medico Forense</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Argentina...</span></span></span>

## Visitor access, distress and ethical interpretation

Visitors expecting a conventional museum experience may be surprised by the atmosphere. These collections are quiet, highly structured and organised around scientific classification rather than dramatic presentation. Labels typically explain pathological processes or forensic principles instead of emphasising shocking details.

Even so, the emotional impact can be considerable. Preserved human organs, congenital abnormalities and forensic injuries confront visitors with the physical reality of illness and mortality in ways that photographs rarely can. The educational value depends on maintaining respect for the individuals represented, recognising that every specimen originated from a real person's life rather than an anonymous curiosity.

Modern interpretation increasingly reflects this shift. The University of Buenos Aires describes its pathology museum as a scientific and educational institution intended to connect historical collections with contemporary learning and public understanding, not simply to preserve unusual objects. Likewise, the judicial forensic museum presents its collections as teaching resources for medico-legal education, illustrating professional practice rather than encouraging morbid fascination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fmed.uba.ar/index.php/departamentos_y_catedras/departamento-de-patologia/museo-de-patologia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fmed.uba.ar">[fmed.uba.ar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fmed.uba.ar</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museo de Patología &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo de Patología &#124; Facultad de Medicina UBA</span></span></span>

Within Argentina's wider landscape of unusual collections, these institutions stand apart because they were never assembled to entertain. Their enduring significance lies in preserving the material history of medicine, public health and forensic science while continually forcing visitors to confront the ethical responsibilities that accompany the preservation and display of human remains.
