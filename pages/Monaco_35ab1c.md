## Introduction

For visitors, the essential distinction is between collections that are permanently accessible, objects that appear only in temporary exhibitions and older attractions that have closed but survive within institutional stores. Monaco’s oddities are usually carefully conserved and professionally interpreted rather than presented as supernatural proof. Their strangeness comes from deep-sea creatures, archaeological remains, mechanical imitation of life, ritual objects and the intense collecting habits of the ruling family.

## The ocean as Monaco’s great cabinet of curiosities

The Oceanographic Museum is Monaco’s clearest answer to a traditional cabinet of curiosities. Opened in 1910, it combines a scientific institution, historical expedition museum and public aquarium in a monumental building rising from the cliffs of Monaco-Ville. Its historical holdings grew largely from the 28 research campaigns undertaken by Prince Albert I between 1884 and 1915 in the Mediterranean, North Atlantic and Arctic regions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://musee.oceano.org/en/our-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: musee.oceano.org">[Musée Océanographique de Monaco]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">musee.oceano.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Musée Océanographique de Monaco Our collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Musée Océanographique de MonacoOur collections - Musée Océanographique de MonacoMuseum holds the richest collection of oceanographic inst...</span></span></span>

The museum’s unusual character is most obvious in its vast mixture of natural specimens and human attempts to investigate the sea. Skeletons, fossils, preserved creatures, model vessels, diving equipment, collecting devices and experimental instruments turn oceanography into a history of encounters with an environment that once seemed almost unknowable. The institution describes its instrument holdings as exceptionally rich and includes prototypes used on Albert I’s expeditions. These are not theatrical props: they are remnants of genuine research campaigns and the developing technology of marine science.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://musee.oceano.org/en/our-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: musee.oceano.org">[Musée Océanographique de Monaco]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">musee.oceano.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Musée Océanographique de Monaco Our collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Musée Océanographique de MonacoOur collections - Musée Océanographique de MonacoMuseum holds the richest collection of oceanographic inst...</span></span></span>

Rather than presenting every object in a clean scientific sequence, it evokes the crowded display style of an older natural-history cabinet. Marine skeletons, fossils, books, models and obsolete equipment are brought together to show how collecting, classification and wonder have shaped the human picture of the ocean. The arrangement is artistic, but the underlying objects belong to the museum’s historical collections rather than being fabricated oddities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonaco.com/en/explore/the-12-must-sees-in-monaco/oceanographic-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonaco.com">[visitmonaco.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonaco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit MonacoOceanographic Museum of Monaco: a must-see attraction...A cabinet of curiosities, this space takes us on a journey through t...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. The museum once participated in a tradition in which unfamiliar animals and objects could be framed as trophies from mysterious regions. ## Bones, caves and ritual belief

Monaco’s Museum of Prehistoric Anthropology provides a darker and more archaeological form of curiosity. Founded by Prince Albert I in 1902, it developed from excavations and discoveries in Monaco and the surrounding region, including work associated with the caves near the modern French-Italian border. Its collections include stone tools, animal remains, human evidence and fossils used to reconstruct the lives of prehistoric populations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonaco.com/en/explore/activities/museums-and-attractions-in-monaco/monaco-museum-of-prehistoric-anthropology-a-unique-place" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonaco.com">[Visit Monaco]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonaco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmonaco.com.</span></span></span>

The museum stands beside the Observatory Cave, a site containing a long sequence of prehistoric occupation. This gives the displays an unusually direct connection with the landscape: the bones and artefacts are not merely exotic objects brought into Monaco but evidence from caves, deposits and archaeological sites around the western Mediterranean. The institution remains involved in research and public education, making it closer to a working archaeological museum than to a commercial chamber of horrors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://culture.mc/en/cultural-figures/cultural-figures/museums/monaco-museum-of-prehistoric-anthropology-founded-by-prince-albert-i/evenements/night-of-museums-at-the-monaco-museum-of-prehistoric-anthropology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: culture.mc">[Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">culture.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on culture.mc.</span></span></span>

For visitors interested in mummies, funerary archaeology and human remains, one of the museum’s most striking reported holdings is the mummy of an Egyptian woman known as Merit, believed to date from around the third century BC. Her presence also shows that the collection extends beyond Monaco’s local prehistory. However, such material needs careful interpretation: a mummy is both an archaeological source and a deceased person, and modern museums increasingly face questions about respectful display, scientific sampling and provenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Prehistoric_Anthropology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum of Prehistoric Anthropology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum of Prehistoric Anthropology</span></span></span>

The museum also uses temporary exhibitions to broaden its remit beyond stone tools and regional excavation. Running from December 2025 to December 2026, **Magies d’ailleurs** examines ritual practices and objects from Africa and Oceania, including masks, altars, cult figures and objects associated with protection, healing and spiritual mediation. It is unusually close to the project’s interest in magic and occult collections, but it should not be mistaken for a museum endorsing supernatural claims. The exhibition approaches magic as a social and cultural system: a way communities have understood danger, authority, illness, protection and relationships with unseen forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media-trade.visitmonaco.com/en/editorial-resources/press-releases/magies-d-ailleurs-the-new-temporary-exhibition-at-the-museum-of-prehistoric-anthropology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media-trade.visitmonaco.com">[visitmonaco.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media-trade.visitmonaco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmonaco.com.</span></span></span>

This kind of exhibition also requires historical caution. Labels such as “magic”, “fetish” or “primitive belief” have often been imposed on non-European objects by collectors and colonial museums. A credible modern display should explain who made and used an object, how it left its original community and whether the museum knows its precise function. The available promotional descriptions emphasise ritual roles and inherited practices, but visitors seeking detailed provenance may need to examine the exhibition labels and catalogue rather than relying on the more atmospheric publicity.

## Monaco’s lost museum of living machines

One of Monaco’s most distinctive historical attractions was the collection of dolls, mechanical toys and automata assembled by Madeleine de Galéa. It was displayed at Villa Sauber for approximately 35 years, creating the impression of a national museum devoted to figures that mimic human or animal movement. The separate attraction closed in 2009 when Villa Sauber became part of the Nouveau Musée National de Monaco, and older listings that describe it as a currently operating museum are therefore misleading. NMNM - Nouveau Musée National de Monaco<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nmnm.mc/en/exhibitions/construire-une-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nmnm.mc">[nmnm.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nmnm.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nouveau Musée National de Monaco Construire une collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nouveau Musée National de Monaco Construire une collection</span></span></span>

The collection itself did not disappear. It contains more than a hundred historic pieces and has been subject to conservation, documentation and restoration by the national museum. During the museum’s **LAB** project, automata and mechanical toys were shown as objects under active study, while electrical replicas made by Monaco watchmaker André Soriano in the 1980s could be operated for visitors. NMNM - Nouveau Musée National de Monaco<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nmnm.mc/en/events/loeil-sur-les-automates/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nmnm.mc">[nmnm.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nmnm.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nmnm.mc.</span></span></span>

Automata are unusually rich objects for a curiosity collection because they sit between toy, sculpture, clockwork technology and illusion. Their movements can appear lifelike, yet every gesture is mechanically predetermined. Nineteenth-century examples also reveal less charming histories: some drew on colonial stereotypes and fantasies of distant peoples. An NMNM exhibition examining the collection noted that these salon toys reflected both French colonial history and the period’s taste for exoticism. NMNM - Nouveau Musée National de Monaco<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nmnm.mc/en/exhibitions/latifa-echakhch-le-jardin-mecanique/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nmnm.mc">[nmnm.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nmnm.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nouveau Musée National de Monaco Latifa Echakhch, le jardin mécanique</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nouveau Musée National de Monaco Latifa Echakhch, le jardin mécanique</span></span></span>

The result is more complicated than a nostalgic doll display. The figures can be admired for their craftsmanship while also being studied as evidence of class, entertainment, industrial skill and racialised popular culture. Their intermittent display is a reminder that a closed museum and a lost collection are not the same thing. The old automata museum is gone as a permanent visitor attraction, but the objects remain within Monaco’s national holdings and may reappear through exhibitions, conservation events or collection-focused programmes.

The Nouveau Musée National also holds other performance-related curiosities, including theatrical models, costumes, props and archives. Its holdings include material connected with dancer and choreographer Serge Lifar, while earlier displays have treated Josephine Baker’s stage costumes almost as modern relics. These collections link Monaco’s mechanical figures with the wider artificial world of theatre: painted scenery, transformed bodies, illusion machinery and preserved traces of performances that no longer exist. ## Tiny objects that represent a tiny state

The Museum of Stamps and Coins appears conventional beside mummies and automata, but its collections reveal another distinctive Monegasque habit: compressing national identity into meticulously controlled objects. The museum presents rare stamps, designs and production material tracing Monaco’s postal history, alongside coins issued by its sovereign princes and monetary finds discovered within the Principality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonaco.com/en/explore/activities/museums-and-attractions-in-monaco/monaco-stamp-and-coin-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonaco.com">[visitmonaco.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonaco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmonaco.com.</span></span></span>

Its unusual appeal lies partly in the machinery and preparatory objects behind the finished collectables. Visitors can encounter printing materials, minting tools, plaster models and other stages in the creation of stamps and coins. The coin sequence reaches back to issues produced under Prince Honoré II in 1640, while Monaco’s first postage stamps were issued under Prince Charles III in 1885.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Stamps_and_Coins" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum of Stamps and Coins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museum of Stamps and Coins</span></span></span>

For a microstate, these are more than small pieces of metal and paper. Stamps travel internationally bearing portraits, anniversaries, animals, sporting events and royal imagery; coins place Monaco’s symbols into everyday circulation. Collecting them therefore becomes a study of how the Principality presents itself to the outside world. The museum is simultaneously public institution, princely collection and part of an active collectors’ market, with philatelic and numismatic products sold on site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://monservicepublic.gouv.mc/en/themes/free-time/sport-and-recreation/recreation/how-to-collect-monaco-stamps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: monservicepublic.gouv.mc">[monservicepublic.gouv.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">monservicepublic.gouv.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">how to collect monaco stamps</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">how to collect monaco stamps</span></span></span>

The surrounding collecting culture is unusually prestigious. Monaco is associated with Monacophil, an international philatelic event created through cooperation between the stamp office, the museum and the Club de Monte-Carlo. This moves the collection beyond simple nostalgia: rare stamps become luxury objects, historical documents and instruments of cultural diplomacy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.monacophil.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: monacophil.com">[Monacophil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">monacophil.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on monacophil.com.</span></span></span>

## What Monaco’s strange collections have in common

Monaco’s curiosity landscape is shaped less by folk monsters or roadside eccentricity than by the institutions and interests of the princely state. Prince Albert I’s expeditions helped create both the Oceanographic Museum and the Museum of Prehistoric Anthropology. Prince Rainier III’s collecting played an important role in the stamps-and-coins museum, while donations and princely loans have also fed the national art and performance collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://musee.oceano.org/en/our-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: musee.oceano.org">[oceano.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">musee.oceano.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Musée Océanographique de Monaco Our collections</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Musée Océanographique de MonacoOur collections - Musée Océanographique de MonacoMuseum holds the richest collection of oceanographic inst...</span></span></span>

This gives the country’s unusual collections a recognisable character:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **They are generally official rather than outsider-led.** Monaco’s oddities tend to be housed in state-supported museums, scientific foundations or national collections.
* **They mix display with prestige.** Expeditions, rare stamps, archaeological discoveries and elaborate automata all reflect collecting as a form of cultural status.
* **They often cross categories.** A marine specimen can be scientific evidence, an expedition trophy and an aesthetic curiosity; an automaton can be a toy, machine, sculpture and historical stereotype.
* **Not everything is permanently visible.** The Oceanographic Museum and Museum of Prehistoric Anthropology are reliable public destinations, while dolls, automata, costumes and archival curiosities may appear only in temporary or research-led displays.
* **The supernatural is usually treated culturally.** Ritual and magical objects may be exhibited, but Monaco’s major museums frame them through anthropology, history or art rather than presenting paranormal claims as proven fact.

</div>

The Principality therefore rewards a different kind of strange-museum visitor. There may be no permanent cryptid hall or witchcraft museum, but there are preserved deep-sea remnants, obsolete exploration machines, prehistoric bones, a mummy, ritual objects, clockwork people and national symbols reduced to miniature works of art. Monaco’s most memorable curiosities are not isolated novelties. They show how science, royalty, collecting and spectacle have repeatedly overlapped within one exceptionally small territory.
