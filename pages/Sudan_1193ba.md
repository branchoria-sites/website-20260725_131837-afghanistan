## The National Museum’s world of mummies and rescued temples

Before the war, the Sudan National Museum in Khartoum was the country’s clearest answer to an archaeological cabinet of curiosities. Its collection traced human life along the Nile from prehistory through the kingdoms of Kerma, Kush, Napata and Meroë, followed by medieval Christian states and later Islamic periods. Visitors encountered monumental statues, pottery, jewellery, funerary objects and inscriptions in the still only partly understood Meroitic script. Upstairs, medieval wall paintings offered an unexpected vision of Christian Nubia far removed from the popular stereotype of Sudan as an exclusively Islamic historical landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.modern-sudan.com/sudan-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: modern-sudan.com">[modern-sudan.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">modern-sudan.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Modern Sudan CollectiveSudan National MuseumThe National Museum was officially inaugurated on May 28, 1971. ## Khalifa House: relics from a revolutionary state

[Khalifa House]({{ 'khalifa-house/' | relative_url }}) in Omdurman was perhaps Sudan’s most atmospheric historic collection. Built in the late nineteenth century, it served as the residence and administrative headquarters of Khalifa Abdallahi, successor to the religious leader Muhammad Ahmad, who proclaimed himself the Mahdi and led an uprising that overthrew Turco-Egyptian rule. The resulting Mahdist state governed much of Sudan from 1885 until its defeat by Anglo-Egyptian forces in 1898.

The building was converted into a museum under colonial rule in 1928, making its interpretation inherently complicated. It preserved the material culture of a movement celebrated by many Sudanese as anti-imperial resistance, but it also reflected decades of British framing of the Mahdist period as fanaticism, disorder and military threat. Modern restoration work sought to present the house less as a trophy room of colonial victory and more as a place where Sudanese communities could explore the period’s competing memories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sudanmemory.org/cms/137/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sudanmemory.org">[sudanmemory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sudanmemory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sudanmemory.org.</span></span></span>

Its collection had the dense, miscellaneous quality of a historical curiosity house. Displays included suits of chain mail, swords, spears, firearms, banners, coins, photographs, household utensils, beds and possessions associated with the Khalifa. The museum also held equipment connected with government and communication, including an early printing press retained and used by the Mahdist administration. In the courtyards were larger objects, among them the surviving cupola from the original tomb of the Mahdi and an early motor vehicle associated with Sudan’s transport history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sudanow-magazine.net/page.php?Id=1232&amp;subId=20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sudanow-magazine.net">[Sudanow Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sudanow-magazine.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sudanow-magazine.net.</span></span></span>

The strangest items were not supernatural relics but objects caught between devotion, national mythology and battlefield collecting. Weapons associated with commanders could function simultaneously as personal possessions, military evidence and symbols of resistance. Displays relating to the Battle of Omdurman included industrial firearms alongside handmade armour and banners, making the technological imbalance behind the Mahdist defeat physically visible.

The museum underwent conservation and reinterpretation between 2018 and 2021 as part of the Western Sudan Community Museums project. Its collections were photographed, storage was improved and staff received conservation training. Those preparations could not protect it fully from the later conflict. UNESCO subsequently reported thefts from Khalifa House, and assessments described damage to the building and losses from its historically important collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org/projects/western-sudan-community-museums-nyala-el-obaid-omdurman" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org">[britishcouncil.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishcouncil.org.</span></span></span>

## Regional museums where archaeology met living tradition

Sudan’s smaller museums were often less polished than the national institution, but their mixed collections made them especially relevant to unusual-museum travellers. The Sheikan Museum in El Obeid, the South Darfur Museum in Nyala and Khalifa House formed a network along an old route between western Sudan and the Nile capital. Their displays brought archaeological finds into the same institutions as household objects, oral histories, photographs, weapons, crafts and material connected with pastoral life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org/projects/western-sudan-community-museums-nyala-el-obaid-omdurman" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org">[cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishcouncil.org.</span></span></span>

The South Darfur Museum held archaeological objects from sites across Sudan together with an extensive ethnographic collection and photographs of historical figures. Such combinations can appear eccentric to visitors accustomed to strict divisions between archaeology, social history and art. In Sudan, however, they reflected the practical role of regional museums: preserving ancient material while also recording clothing, tools, ceremonies, livelihoods and memories that remained part of living communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sudanmemory.org/cms/139/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sudanmemory.org">[sudanmemory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sudanmemory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sudanmemory.org.</span></span></span>

The community-museum programme attempted to move these institutions away from static presentations of anonymous “tribal” objects. Local pastoralists, craftspeople, musicians and elders were involved in recording traditions and explaining how objects were used. Film archives associated with the project include material on camel breeding, religious education, landscape, farming and regional identity. The result was closer to a collaborative archive of living practices than a conventional glass-case museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sslh.info/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sslh.info">[sslh.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sslh.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sslh.info.</span></span></span>

This distinction matters when encountering ritual or ceremonial material. Jewellery, wedding equipment, protective ornaments or objects associated with religious education should not automatically be labelled “magic” because they look mysterious to outsiders. Their meaning depends on local testimony, use and belief. The strongest Sudanese museum projects treated communities as interpreters of their own material culture rather than presenting unusual objects merely as exotic curiosities.

War has again altered what survives. UNESCO confirmed reports of stolen material from Nyala, while later assessments described the museums of Nyala and El Geneina as almost completely destroyed. By late 2025, officials estimated that about 700 objects were missing from museums in Darfur, part of a nationwide count of roughly 4,000 unaccounted-for antiquities. These figures were still provisional and may change as inventories continue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/sudan-unesco-raises-alarm-reports-illicit-trafficking-cultural-heritage" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

## The Ethnographic Museum and the problem of the “exotic”

Khartoum’s Ethnographic Museum offered the country’s broadest collection of everyday cultural objects. Its galleries represented different regions and environments through musical instruments, boats, food preparation equipment, clothing, crafts, wedding material and objects associated with religious schooling. Older displays included geographic groupings and dioramas intended to show how particular communities lived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DF3E9D9EC1C8D76146648B96D49DE131/S0003598X25101749a.pdf/archaeology_and_cultural_heritage_in_wartime_sudan_20232025.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">archaeology and cultural heritage in wartime sudan 20232025</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">archaeology and cultural heritage in wartime sudan 20232025</span></span></span>

Among its more memorable holdings were grave sculptures from communities in what is now South Sudan, camel and cattle decorations, bridal accessories, beadwork and a large wooden war drum associated with the ruler of Yambio. These objects could easily be marketed as “strange”, but the museum’s history illustrates why that label requires care. Much of the collection was formed during the colonial and early postcolonial periods, when ethnographic museums often reduced diverse peoples to fixed visual types.

A planned renovation was intended to reopen the previously inaccessible building, protect it from flooding and involve communities from Darfur and Kordofan in documenting their own heritage. The approach recognised that an ethnographic object is not self-explanatory: a drum, bridal mat or camel ornament becomes meaningful only when connected to the people who made, used and remembered it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org/projects/safeguarding-living-heritage-sudan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org">[cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultural-protection-fund.britishcouncil.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishcouncil.org.</span></span></span>

The war interrupted that work. The museum suffered vandalism and looting, and reports described extensive damage to cultural institutions across Khartoum. Its present public status should therefore be checked through current heritage authorities rather than assumed from older travel guides.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DF3E9D9EC1C8D76146648B96D49DE131/S0003598X25101749a.pdf/archaeology_and_cultural_heritage_in_wartime_sudan_20232025.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">archaeology and cultural heritage in wartime sudan 20232025</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">archaeology and cultural heritage in wartime sudan 20232025</span></span></span>

## Lost scientific collections and political curiosities

Not all of Sudan’s unusual collections were archaeological. Khartoum’s Natural History Museum preserved zoological and scientific specimens used for teaching and research. Such museums commonly contain taxidermy, skeletons, preserved animals and comparative specimens: material that can feel like a traditional cabinet of natural curiosities even when its purpose is academic. UNESCO’s project records now describe the museum as completely destroyed during the war, making it one of the starkest losses in Sudan’s scientific heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://core.unesco.org/en/project/3210523081" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: core.unesco.org">[UNESCO Core Data Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">core.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Looting and plundering of major heritage sites on the Tentative List such as Kerma, Sai Island.... The Republican Palace Museum occupied a converted church within the presidential complex. Its collection included portraits, photographs, official objects and vehicles used by successive rulers and visiting dignitaries. This mixture of sacred architecture, state memorabilia and ceremonial transport made it an unusual political museum, charting changes of regime through the possessions and imagery of power rather than through archaeology alone.

By September 2025, the interior was reportedly filled with charred debris, while antique cars outside had smashed windows and damaged fittings. The destruction transformed what had been a curated procession of presidents and governments into another damaged site awaiting assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/sudan-preservationists-struggle-restore-countrys-shattered-cultural-treasures-2025-09-30/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on reuters.com.</span></span></span>

Sudan also possessed specialist archives and laboratories that were never tourist attractions but belonged to the wider culture of collecting. Archaeological stores, photographic archives, university collections and bioarchaeology laboratories preserved excavated human remains and research records. ## Folklore, religion and what Sudan does not display

There is little reliable evidence for a permanent Sudanese museum devoted specifically to witchcraft, spirits, monsters, occultism or UFOs. That absence does not mean Sudan lacks rich supernatural traditions. Beliefs concerning spirit possession, healing, saints, protective objects, dreams and sacred places form part of the country’s diverse religious and folkloric life. They have generally been preserved through community practice, oral history, religious institutions and ethnographic research rather than commercial museums designed for strange tourism.

Visitors should therefore be cautious about treating devotional or protective objects as occult curios. An amulet may be a living religious item; a ceremonial instrument may belong to a healing or communal practice; and a ruler’s tomb may function as a place of reverence rather than an exhibit. The category of “weird museum” can be useful for finding unexpected material, but it can also flatten distinctions between faith, folklore, scholarship and entertainment.

Sudan’s most compelling uncanny heritage comes instead from the collision of different kinds of evidence: preserved bodies beside scientific records, temples removed from drowned landscapes, undeciphered writing, battlefield relics turned into national symbols, and everyday cultural objects once classified by outsiders but now increasingly interpreted by their communities.

## Can these places be visited?

Older guidebooks and travel pages should not be relied upon for present access. The Sudan National Museum, Khalifa House, the Ethnographic Museum, the Republican Palace Museum and several regional institutions have suffered serious damage, theft or occupation since 2023. Even where a building remains standing, unexploded ordnance, unstable structures, missing infrastructure and continuing insecurity may make public entry impossible. UNESCO’s response has concentrated on emergency stabilisation, inventories, anti-trafficking work and institutional recovery rather than normal tourism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/fighting-against-illicit-trafficking-sudanese-cultural-property-and-strengthening-institutional?hub=180364" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fighting Against the Illicit Trafficking of Sudanese Cultural Property</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fighting Against the Illicit Trafficking of Sudanese Cultural Property</span></span></span>

The most dependable access to many collections is currently digital. Sudan Memory and Safeguarding Sudan’s [Living Heritage]({{ 'living-heritage/' | relative_url }}) preserve photographs, catalogue descriptions, films and community testimony connected with museums across the country. These platforms cannot reproduce the physical experience of standing inside Khalifa House or beside a reconstructed temple, but they retain essential information about objects that may be damaged, dispersed or missing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sudanmemory.org/cms/137/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sudanmemory.org">[sudanmemory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sudanmemory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sudanmemory.org.</span></span></span>

For future travellers, the practical rule is simple: regard every Sudanese museum listing as historical until current, institution-specific confirmation shows that it has reopened. Sudan’s unusual collections remain culturally important, but at present they are also evidence from an active heritage emergency.

## Why Sudan’s strange collections matter


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Sudan’s museums complicate familiar stories about north-east Africa. They show that monumental Nile civilisations were not confined to modern Egypt; that powerful Christian kingdoms flourished in medieval Nubia; that Islamic, African and Mediterranean histories overlapped; and that objects from pastoral communities deserve interpretation as living heritage rather than anonymous “tribal art”.

</div>

Their recent destruction has also changed the meaning of collecting. A mummy or ceremonial sword is no longer simply an intriguing exhibit. It may be a missing object sought by international authorities, a fragment awaiting conservation or the only surviving material witness to a community’s history. UNESCO has warned that illicit trafficking from Sudan has reached an exceptional level, prompting cooperation with police, customs officials and neighbouring states.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/sudan-unesco-raises-alarm-reports-illicit-trafficking-cultural-heritage" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Sudan’s curiosity landscape is therefore not a conventional trail of quirky attractions. It is a network of ancient bodies, rescued monuments, revolutionary relics, ethnographic objects, scientific specimens and digital records whose survival is uncertain. What makes it extraordinary is not manufactured mystery, but the depth of the histories held inside objects—and the urgency of preserving enough context for those objects to remain more than displaced curiosities.
