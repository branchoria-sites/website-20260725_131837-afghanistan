## Introduction

The best starting point is Panama City’s [Mola Museum]({{ 'mola-museum/' | relative_url }}), where layered cloth panels become records of Guna identity, spirituality and political survival. Beyond the capital, the Manuel F. Zárate Museum preserves costumes and objects linked to folk performance, while Portobelo’s Congo culture turns masks, ritual combat and symbolic devils into living heritage. Colonial religious art supplies reliquary-like objects and unsettling sacred sculpture; Coiba offers the remains of an island penal colony reclaimed by forest. Together, these places show that Panama’s curiosity culture is less about manufactured mystery than about objects whose meanings remain active, contested or difficult to separate from ritual life.

## Where Panama’s strangest collections are found

The country’s unusual heritage is dispersed across several types of institution. Some are conventional museums with catalogued objects and fixed galleries. Others are community cultural centres, archaeological sites or landscapes where ruins and traditions matter more than glass cases. ## The Mola Museum: cloth as cosmology and memory

The Mola Museum in Panama City’s historic district is Panama’s clearest example of a small, specialised collection that rewards curiosity. It is devoted to the layered textile panels associated with Guna women and presents them through five exhibitions dealing with technique, historical development, surroundings, storytelling, protection and worldview. Admission is currently advertised as free, and the museum’s own site describes the collection as an artistic exploration rather than a souvenir display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museodelamola.org/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museodelamola.org">[Museo de la mola]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museodelamola.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museo de la mola Home &#124; Mola Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo de la molaHome &#124; Mola Museum - Panama29 Jan 2026 — MuMo invites visitors to explore and learn about the mola from an artistic viewp...</span></span></span>

Molas are constructed by layering cloth, cutting through selected sections and sewing the exposed edges to create intricate designs. The resulting images may be geometric, botanical, animal, political, religious or drawn from modern popular culture. More than 200 pieces have been reported in the museum’s displays, allowing visitors to compare closely controlled stitching with bolder narrative scenes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://americasquarterly.org/article/a-communitys-life-told-in-textiles/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: americasquarterly.org">[Americas Quarterly]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">americasquarterly.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">a communitys life told in textiles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Americas QuarterlyA Community&#x27;s Life, Told in Textiles25 Jul 2023 — More than 200 molas are exhibited in the Museo de la Mola, opened in...</span></span></span>

What makes the museum unusual is not simply the visual density of the textiles. It treats cloth as a means of recording experience. Guna makers have used molas to represent daily life, contact with outsiders, environmental change and understandings of the visible and spiritual worlds. One account of the collection describes molas as a form of writing through which Guna women portray their community’s reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://americasquarterly.org/article/a-communitys-life-told-in-textiles/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: americasquarterly.org">[Americas Quarterly]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">americasquarterly.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">a communitys life told in textiles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Americas QuarterlyA Community&#x27;s Life, Told in Textiles25 Jul 2023 — More than 200 molas are exhibited in the Museo de la Mola, opened in...</span></span></span>

The museum also changes the meaning of objects commonly sold to tourists. Removed from their cultural setting, mola panels can be reduced to colourful handicrafts. Displayed alongside explanations of dress, identity and Guna history, they become evidence of women’s artistic authority and of a people’s resistance to cultural assimilation. Broader museum scholarship describes the mola as both a cultural marker and an art form through which women help construct Guna identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clevelandart.org/articles/fashioning-identity-mola-textiles-panama" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clevelandart.org">[Cleveland Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clevelandart.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fashioning identity mola textiles panama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cleveland Museum of ArtFashioning Identity: Mola Textiles of Panamá8 Oct 2020 — These blouses, called molas, are so visually striking and...</span></span></span>

Visitors should nevertheless remember that this is a privately initiated museum interpreting a living Indigenous tradition, not a sealed record of a vanished culture. The most responsible reading is to see its exhibits as an introduction to Guna art and history, then recognise that the tradition continues in communities, clothing, commerce and political life beyond the museum.

## Folklore museums and Panama’s masked devils

Panama’s richest concentration of uncanny imagery appears in folklore rather than in horror museums. Costumes, masks and dance traditions from the central provinces and the Caribbean coast populate a world of devils, queens, animals, mock authorities and ritual confrontations. These figures may appear visually sinister, but their meanings are theatrical, religious and historical rather than simply frightening.

The Manuel F. Zárate Museum in Guararé is the most important museum anchor for this material. Opened in 1969, it honours folklorist Manuel F. Zárate and his study of Panamanian popular culture. The institution preserves clothing, documents and objects associated with national and regional traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://micultura.gob.pa/museo-exhibe-nuevo-busto-en-honor-a-dora-perez-de-zarate/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: micultura.gob.pa">[Mi Cultura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">micultura.gob.pa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">museo exhibe nuevo busto en honor a dora perez de zarate</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">museo exhibe nuevo busto en honor a dora perez de zarate</span></span></span>

One of its notable exhibits is an old ceremonial dress reported by the Ministry of Culture to be about 160 years old. Its exact age is presented as an estimate rather than a firmly documented date, which is a useful reminder that folklore collections often contain objects whose family histories are clearer than their formal provenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://micultura.gob.pa/antigua-pollera-se-mantiene-expuesta-en-el-museo-manuel-f-zarate/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: micultura.gob.pa">[Mi Cultura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">micultura.gob.pa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">antigua pollera se mantiene expuesta en el museo manuel f zarate</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">antigua pollera se mantiene expuesta en el museo manuel f zarate</span></span></span>

The wider fascination lies in how such museums preserve the material half of traditions that only make full sense in performance. Devil masks may be displayed as carved or painted objects, but during festivals they become characters within dramas of temptation, resistance, disorder and Christian triumph. Costumes can encode social rank, comic exaggeration or racial history. A static case therefore shows only one stage in an object’s life.

### Portobelo’s Congo tradition

On Panama’s Caribbean coast, Congo culture offers the country’s most compelling meeting of masks, historical memory and ritual theatre. The tradition grew among Afro-Panamanian communities shaped by slavery, resistance and cultural survival. Its performances combine music, dance, improvised speech, mock courts and symbolic battles. Panama’s tourism authority directs visitors towards the Casa de la Cultura Congo in the Portobelo region for workshops and cultural interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitpanama.com/a-donde-ir/colon/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitpanama.com">[Visit Panama]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitpanama.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitpanama.com.</span></span></span>

The devil figures within Congo performance are especially relevant to strange-history travellers. UNESCO documentation describes ritual sequences in which Congo rulers overcome the devils, remove their masks and symbolically baptise or neutralise them. The imagery is vivid, but the devils are not evidence of occult worship. They belong to a dramatic system in which evil, colonial authority and social inversion can be mocked, confronted and defeated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/38918-EN.doc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ich.unesco.org">[ICH UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ich.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">38918 EN.doc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">38918 EN.doc</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. Commercial descriptions may sell masked dances as exotic spectacle, while purely religious descriptions can flatten their humour and historical resistance. The strongest interpretation holds both together: Congo performance is a living cultural practice with Catholic elements, African-derived expression, memories of enslavement and a deliberate use of disorder as theatre.

## Colonial religious objects: devotion, fear and disputed survival

Panama’s Colonial Religious Art Museum provides another form of curiosity collection: [sacred objects]({{ 'sacred-objects-2a8962/' | relative_url }}) whose emotional power survives even when their original devotional setting has changed. Its holdings have included paintings, polychrome sculpture, carved wood, medallions, bells and objects made from ivory, marble, silver and stone, with material spanning several colonial centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://recorriendopanama.com/en/colonial-religious-art-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: recorriendopanama.com">[Recorriendo Panamá]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">recorriendopanama.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Recorriendo PanamáColonial Religious Art Museum in Casco Antiguo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recorriendo PanamáColonial Religious Art Museum in Casco Antiguo</span></span></span>

To a modern secular visitor, colonial saints and suffering figures can look severe or macabre. Wounds, martyrdom, humility and divine punishment were deliberately made visible because sacred images were expected to teach and move worshippers. These works were not created as gothic entertainment. Their intensity belongs to a religious culture in which material objects mediated between earthly life and the divine.

The collection also raises questions about provenance and cultural mixture. Colonial church art in Panama reflects European styles, local craftsmanship and Indigenous labour, but the documentary record is incomplete. Research on the museum’s baroque works notes substantial gaps in the historiography of Panamanian colonial art, particularly regarding art produced by Indigenous makers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366510322_mirada_al_arte_barroco_del_Museo_de_Arte_Religioso_Colonial_Ciudad_de_Panama_el_Senor_de_la_Paciencia_y_la_Humildad_la_Inmaculada_Concepcion_y_santa_Barbara" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) mirada al arte barroco del Museo de Arte Religioso</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) mirada al arte barroco del Museo de Arte Religioso</span></span></span>

Visitors should verify the museum’s present location and opening status before relying on older guidebooks. Accounts of closure, structural problems and later relocation have circulated over time, and official cultural institutions have continued preservation work. This is a case where the collection is real and historically important, but practical information can lag behind administrative change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lonelyplanet.com/points-of-interest/museo-de-arte-religioso-colonial/432916" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lonelyplanet.com">[Lonely Planet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lonelyplanet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lonely Planet Museo de Arte Religioso Colonial</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lonely Planet Museo de Arte Religioso Colonial</span></span></span>

The museum matters within Panama’s strange-history landscape because it preserves objects at the point where art, belief and fear overlap. Bells, statues and silver church vessels were never curiosities to their first users. They became museum objects only after changes in worship, architecture and heritage policy separated them from everyday ritual use.

## Coiba: a prison ruin inside a natural sanctuary

Coiba is not a conventional prison museum. It is a vast Pacific island and national park containing the remains of a penal colony that operated for much of the twentieth century. Its unusual power comes from the collision between human violence and ecological abundance: abandoned buildings and prison traces sit within a landscape now promoted for forests, reefs, sharks, whales and other wildlife.

Panama’s official tourism information states that Coiba served as a prison for almost 100 years. The island’s enforced isolation restricted settlement and development, inadvertently protecting large areas of forest and surrounding marine habitat. Today, Coiba National Park covers 38 islands and more than 270,000 hectares of terrestrial and marine environments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tourismpanama.com/outdoors-and-nature/nature-parks/national-parks/coiba-national-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tourismpanama.com">[Tourism Panama]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tourismpanama.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tourismpanama.com.</span></span></span>

The former colony is often marketed as Panama’s answer to Alcatraz, but this comparison can obscure more than it explains. Coiba was not designed as a visitor attraction, and the surviving prison area is a ruin rather than a polished institution with a stable exhibition narrative. Commercial operators do advertise visits to the former prison, usually as an addition to boat, wildlife or diving excursions, but access depends on permits, conditions and tour arrangements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/coiba-island-penal-colony" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Coiba Island Penal Colony in El Trapiche</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Coiba Island Penal Colony in El Trapiche</span></span></span>

That lack of conventional curation is part of the site’s appeal and its problem. Visitors may encounter deteriorating structures with limited interpretation, leaving dramatic stories to circulate through guides and tourism copy. Claims about brutality, political prisoners and notorious inmates should be treated carefully unless tied to documentary evidence. The physical remains are authentic; individual stories attached to them may be unevenly sourced.

Coiba matters because it demonstrates how uncomfortable heritage can be absorbed into a new national story. The prison’s isolation helped preserve the ecosystems for which the island is now celebrated, so conservation and incarceration cannot be neatly separated. Nature has not erased the penal colony; it has grown around it.

## Archaeological creatures, graves and canal relics

Panama’s archaeological collections contain some of the country’s most visually extraordinary objects, particularly funerary pieces decorated with bats, crocodiles and composite beings. Such imagery can look cryptid-like to a modern audience, but it belongs to pre-Columbian systems of status, transformation and animal symbolism rather than to evidence of unknown creatures.

The Panama Canal Museum is not an oddities museum, yet its collection of more than 30,000 objects includes an unusually broad mixture: cannonballs, French lighthouse equipment, funerary urns, treaties, maps, industrial material and the Panamanian flag associated with the events of 9 January 1964. Museo del Canal Interoceánico de Panamá<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museodelcanal.com/en/exhibition-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museodelcanal.com">[museodelcanal.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museodelcanal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museo del Canal Interoceánico de PanamáExhibitions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museo del Canal Interoceánico de PanamáExhibitions</span></span></span>

This range makes it closer to a modern cabinet of national curiosities than its name suggests. The canal story required archaeology, engineering, disease control, imperial ambition, migration and political conflict. A visitor may move from ancient burial objects to machinery and diplomatic documents within a single institutional narrative.

The museum is strongly curated rather than mysterious. Its objects are intended to explain how Panama’s position between oceans became both an engineering opportunity and a source of foreign intervention. The strange effect comes from scale and juxtaposition: human remains and urns belong to one historical world, French industrial relics to another, while flags and treaties show how physical objects can become politically charged national symbols.

## Frogs, extinction and scientific wonder

For natural curiosities, the Smithsonian’s Punta Culebra Nature Center is more relevant than a conventional taxidermy hall. Its exhibits combine marine organisms, forest ecology and living animals, including a programme devoted to Panama’s frogs. The Smithsonian notes that the country has nearly 200 frog species and that its frog exhibit includes critically endangered animals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stri.si.edu/visit/punta-culebra" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stri.si.edu">[Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stri.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

The experience is scientific and family-oriented rather than macabre, but threatened amphibians bring an uncomfortable element to the displays. Visitors are not merely seeing unusual shapes and colours; they are encountering species affected by habitat loss and disease. The collection therefore sits between public attraction, research outreach and conservation warning.

The Biomuseo expands this natural-history theme on a much larger scale. Its eight principal galleries explain biodiversity, the geological formation of the isthmus, the movement of animals between continents and the separation of two oceans. Displays include a multi-level projection space, an aquarium and a large sculptural representation of ecological relationships.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biomuseo.org/en/permanentes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biomuseo.org">[Biomuseo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biomuseo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on biomuseo.org.</span></span></span>

Neither institution is “strange” in the occult sense. Their relevance comes from presenting Panama itself as a biological anomaly: a narrow land bridge whose formation altered ocean currents, migration routes and the distribution of life. The most surprising exhibit is ultimately the country’s geography.

## Commercial illusion versus cultural mystery

Panama City also has a Museum of Illusions, part of an international entertainment model based on optical tricks, distorted rooms and interactive photographs. It openly describes itself as an educational-entertainment attraction, and visitors should understand it as commercial leisure rather than as a collection rooted in Panamanian folklore or museum scholarship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://moipanama.com/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: moipanama.com">[Museum of Illusions Panama]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">moipanama.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on moipanama.com.</span></span></span>

It can still suit travellers seeking playful oddity, but it occupies a different category from the Mola Museum, Coiba or the folklore institutions. Nothing in an illusion room claims historical authenticity. The attraction engineers temporary sensory confusion; Panama’s culturally significant collections preserve objects whose meanings developed over generations.

That contrast offers a useful test for evaluating unusual attractions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Is the object original, reproduced or purpose-built for photographs?**
* **Does interpretation identify the community or belief system behind it?**
* **Is uncertainty admitted when provenance is weak?**
* **Is a living tradition being explained or merely decorated?**
* **Does the institution preserve material, stage an experience or combine both?**

</div>

These questions are especially important in Panama, where the most memorable “strange” material often belongs to communities whose art and ritual have been repeatedly commercialised.

## What Panama’s curiosity culture reveals

Panama’s unusual collections are strongest when they reveal how objects move between worlds. A mola can be clothing, storytelling, political identity, fine art and tourist merchandise. A devil mask can be sculpture in a museum and a dangerous comic character in performance. A colonial saint can shift from an object of prayer to an art-historical exhibit. A prison can become a ruin inside a biodiversity reserve.

This mobility explains why Panama does not need a grand museum of the paranormal to possess a distinctive strange-history landscape. Its most compelling curiosities are not presented as unexplained relics. They are objects whose meanings have accumulated through colonial rule, Indigenous resistance, African diasporic memory, Catholic devotion, archaeological discovery and tourism.

The Mola Museum offers the most coherent visitor experience, while the Manuel F. Zárate Museum and Portobelo’s cultural institutions provide routes into living folklore. Colonial religious collections supply the darker visual language of sacred art. Coiba preserves a difficult history without fully transforming it into a museum, and Panama’s scientific centres turn frogs, reefs and continental change into natural wonder.

The result is a curiosity culture grounded less in hoaxes and monsters than in transformation. Panama’s strangest collections ask what happens when belief becomes heritage, when ritual objects enter display cases and when places created for control are reclaimed by memory, tourism or nature.

    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
