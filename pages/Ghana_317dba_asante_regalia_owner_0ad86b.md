## Introduction

The royal treasures displayed at Manhyia Palace Museum are not simply beautiful examples of goldsmithing. For the Asante Kingdom, they are living symbols of political legitimacy, ancestral continuity and sacred authority. That distinction explains why debates over their ownership have become some of the most significant restitution discussions in the museum world. While many visitors see dazzling gold ornaments, ceremonial swords and royal insignia, the Asante court sees objects that continue to belong within an active royal institution rather than a vanished historical civilisation.

This makes Manhyia Palace Museum unusual within [Ghana]({{ 'where-ghanas-strangest-objects-still/' | relative_url }})'s museum landscape. It is not only interpreting the history of the regalia; it is doing so from inside the institution that created, used and continues to invest these objects with meaning. The result is a museum where questions of heritage, law, spirituality and colonial history remain very much alive.

## How regalia carries political and spiritual authority

To understand the ownership debate, it is necessary to understand what Asante regalia actually represents.

Gold has long symbolised wealth in the Asante Kingdom, but royal regalia carries a deeper significance than its material value. Ceremonial swords, state umbrellas, necklaces, badges of office, stools, staffs and ornaments identify legitimate political authority. Many are regarded as "stool property"—belonging not to an individual ruler but to the office, the kingdom and its continuing institutions. Successive rulers become custodians rather than personal owners of these objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/315887" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Asante-Akan artist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtAsante-Akan artist - Ȯkyeame poma (royal spokesperson&#x27;s staff) with stool, chain, and crossed swords - Asa...</span></span></span>

The most famous example is the Golden Stool, which Asante tradition holds descended from heaven during the kingdom's foundation. Although it is not normally displayed publicly and is absent from ordinary museum collections, it illustrates how royal objects are understood within Asante political philosophy. The stool is believed to embody the nation itself rather than functioning as a ceremonial seat. Its importance helps explain why other pieces of royal regalia are also treated as extensions of state authority rather than decorative artworks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manhyiapalacemuseum.org/a-history-of-asante-gold-at-the-va/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manhyiapalacemuseum.org">[Manhyia Palace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manhyiapalacemuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Manhyia Palace A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A – Manhyia Palace</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhyia Palace A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A – Manhyia Palace</span></span></span>

Within palace ceremonies, regalia also regulates hierarchy. Specific swords, staffs, ornaments and insignia may only be carried by particular office holders. Their use signals constitutional roles inside the traditional kingdom, not simply ceremonial costume. Even today, the Asantehene has publicly criticised chiefs who improperly adopt regalia associated with higher offices, demonstrating that these objects continue to govern living traditions rather than existing solely as museum exhibits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manhyiapalace.org/asantehene-warns-chiefs-against-misuse-of-traditional-regalia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manhyiapalace.org">[Manhyia Palace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manhyiapalace.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhyia PalaceAsantehene Warns Chiefs Against Misuse of Traditional Regalia – Manhyia PalaceOctober 16, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2025</span></span></span>

For visitors, this creates an important difference in interpretation. A gold sword ornament displayed in London may appear as an exquisite work of metalwork. Displayed at Manhyia Palace, it is presented as one component within an enduring political and spiritual system.

## The return of looted objects to Kumasi

Many of the most celebrated Asante treasures left Ghana during Britain's military campaigns against the Asante Kingdom in the nineteenth century.

Following the Anglo-Asante Wars—particularly the sack of Kumasi in 1874, and later campaigns in 1896 and 1900—British soldiers removed substantial quantities of royal regalia. Some objects entered military collections as war trophies. Others were sold through dealers or acquired by museums, including the British Museum and the Victoria and Albert Museum (V&A). Additional pieces eventually reached collections elsewhere, including the Fowler Museum in California.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/british-museum-story/contested-objects-collection/asante-gold-regalia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Asante Gold Regalia &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Asante Gold Regalia &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

For decades the Asante Kingdom sought their return. A formal request was made through the Ghanaian government as early as 1974, but British legislation governing national museum collections prevented permanent deaccessioning of many objects. Instead, collaboration gradually shifted towards museum partnerships, conservation training and discussions about future loans.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/british-museum-story/contested-objects-collection/asante-gold-regalia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Asante Gold Regalia &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Asante Gold Regalia &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

A major breakthrough came in 2024.

To coincide with commemorations marking both the 150th anniversary of the 1874 conflict and the Silver Jubilee of Asantehene Otumfuo Osei Tutu II, seventeen objects from the V&A and fifteen from the British Museum travelled to Manhyia Palace Museum under renewable long-term loan agreements. At roughly the same time, seven royal objects from the Fowler Museum were permanently returned to the Asante Kingdom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/a-history-of-asante-gold-at-the-vanda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vam.ac.uk">[vam.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Victoria and Albert Museum A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A · V&amp;A</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Victoria and Albert Museum A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A · V&amp;A</span></span></span>

For visitors to Kumasi, this changed the museum experience dramatically. Objects absent from the palace for around 150 years could once again be viewed within the royal institution that originally commissioned and used them.

## Loans, permanent restitution and disputed ownership

The arrival of the regalia in Kumasi did not end the ownership debate. Instead, it highlighted competing understandings of what "return" actually means.

From the perspective of British museums, renewable loans represent a practical solution that allows important objects to be displayed in their place of origin while remaining consistent with legislation that restricts permanent disposal from national collections. Both the British Museum and the V&A describe the agreements as partnerships built around conservation, research and shared access. The V&A loans are structured in renewable three-year periods, subject to regulatory approval.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/a-history-of-asante-gold-at-the-vanda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vam.ac.uk">[Victoria and Albert Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Victoria and Albert Museum A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A · V&amp;A</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Victoria and Albert Museum A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A · V&amp;A</span></span></span>

Many Ghanaian commentators, however, argue that objects removed through colonial violence should not remain legally owned by institutions that acquired them after military campaigns. From this perspective, calling the arrangement a "loan" implies that the original owners must borrow back their own cultural heritage. Critics therefore distinguish sharply between physical return and legal restitution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e5aa77f9c07ab8952e507dd01e983c76" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This collaboration serves as a workaround to U.K. laws that prohibit the repatriation of cultural treasures. The permanent return of the Fowler Museum objects demonstrates another possible model. Unlike the British loans, ownership was transferred outright, meaning the regalia became part of the Asante royal collection once again rather than remaining the legal property of a foreign museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/d4165d0364c59aaadb1a4f4dfdab9dbb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This repatriation marks a significant event for the Asante kingdom with the returned items including a gold necklace, two bracelets, a go...</span></span></span>

The disagreement is therefore not simply about where the objects are displayed. It concerns several separate questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Legal ownership:** Which institution or community should hold title to the objects?
* **Moral ownership:** Does colonial acquisition invalidate later legal ownership?
* **Spiritual ownership:** Can sacred regalia truly belong outside the political system that gives it meaning?
* **Public access:** Should globally significant collections remain internationally distributed or be reunited where they originated?

</div>

Each question has different answers depending on whether the discussion is framed in museum law, international ethics or Asante tradition.

## Why Manhyia Palace Museum changes the debate

Many restitution disputes are discussed in distant museums or courtrooms. Manhyia Palace Museum offers something different: visitors can see how the objects function within the culture that continues to recognise their authority.

Rather than presenting the regalia primarily as archaeological treasures, the museum interprets them through royal history, ceremonial practice and the continuity of the Asante Kingdom. This reinforces the Asante argument that these are not relics from an extinct civilisation but components of a living constitutional and cultural system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manhyiapalacemuseum.org/a-history-of-asante-gold-at-the-va/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manhyiapalacemuseum.org">[Manhyia Palace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manhyiapalacemuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Manhyia Palace A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A – Manhyia Palace</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhyia Palace A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A – Manhyia Palace</span></span></span>

The museum therefore occupies an unusual position. It is simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a public museum welcoming visitors;
* part of an active royal palace complex;
* a centre for interpreting Asante political history;
* and an important participant in international restitution negotiations.

</div>

That combination makes it one of Ghana's most distinctive heritage institutions.

## Why this matters beyond Ghana

The Asante regalia dispute has become a prominent example in wider debates over colonial collections.

Unlike arguments centred solely on artistic masterpieces, the Asante case emphasises that some museum objects continue to possess constitutional, ceremonial and spiritual functions for the communities from which they came. Their significance cannot be measured only by age, rarity or market value.

The current arrangement also illustrates a compromise increasingly discussed in international museum practice. Long-term renewable loans allow important objects to return physically while legal ownership remains unresolved. Supporters see this as a pragmatic route towards cultural reconnection. Critics regard it as an incomplete response that leaves colonial-era ownership structures fundamentally unchanged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/a-history-of-asante-gold-at-the-vanda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vam.ac.uk">[vam.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Victoria and Albert Museum A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A · V&amp;A</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Victoria and Albert Museum A history of Asante gold at the V&amp;A · V&amp;A</span></span></span>

For visitors to Manhyia Palace Museum, the experience is therefore about far more than viewing remarkable gold objects. It offers a rare opportunity to encounter royal regalia where its creators believe it belongs, while witnessing an international debate over history, justice and cultural authority that is still unfolding.
