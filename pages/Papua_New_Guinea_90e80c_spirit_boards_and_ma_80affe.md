## Introduction

Many of [Papua New Guinea]({{ 'where-papua-new-guinea-keeps-its/' | relative_url }})'s best-known ceremonial objects are displayed today as masterpieces of carving, yet they were not originally created as decorative art. In the Gulf region especially, spirit boards and ceremonial masks were understood as active presences connected with named ancestral or spirit beings. Their significance depended not only on their carved form but also on songs, ritual knowledge, movement, men's ceremonial houses and carefully controlled access. Once separated from those settings and placed behind museum glass, they remain visually striking, but much of the meaning that gave them power becomes difficult to communicate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/313901" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Spirit Board (Gope</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtSpirit Board (Gope) - Kerewa people - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

Understanding these objects as living presences rather than sculptures changes how they are viewed. Instead of asking whether a carving is beautiful, the more important historical question is what relationships it embodied, who was allowed to approach it and what role it played within the spiritual life of its community.

## How Gulf spirit boards represented ancestral beings

The carved spirit boards of the Papuan Gulf—often called *gope*, though different communities used different names such as *koi* or *hohao*—were not simply symbolic portraits. Ethnographic research and museum documentation consistently describe them as dwelling places for powerful spiritual beings associated with particular clans, landscapes, rivers or stretches of coastline. Each board represented a specific supernatural presence rather than a generic ancestor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/313901" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Spirit Board (Gope</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtSpirit Board (Gope) - Kerewa people - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

These boards were traditionally kept inside men's ceremonial houses, where each clan maintained its own sacred area. Alongside spirit boards were other restricted ritual objects, including carved figures and ancestral remains. Access was carefully controlled because the objects were believed to participate in relationships between the living community and powerful unseen beings rather than merely reminding people of them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/313901" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Spirit Board (Gope</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtSpirit Board (Gope) - Kerewa people - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

The carved designs therefore carried more than artistic style. They identified particular beings, histories and clan identities. Damage, movement or removal of a board could affect more than the physical object because its importance depended upon continuing ritual relationships and inherited knowledge.

## Why ceremonial masks were more than disguises

Visitors sometimes imagine ceremonial masks as costumes that allowed performers to "pretend" to become spirits. That interpretation misses the way many Papua New Guinean ceremonies understood performance itself.

Across parts of the Papuan Gulf, masks became meaningful only when combined with dance, music, body movement, costume and ritual performance. During major ceremonial cycles among the Western Elema, enormous barkcloth masks were created inside specially constructed men's houses over many years. Their making involved secrecy, feasting, initiation and exchanges between communities before they finally appeared in public ceremonies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/papua-new-guinea/ceremonial-masks-of-papua-new-guinea" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Ceremonial masks of Papua New Guinea &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Ceremonial masks of Papua New Guinea &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

Rather than disguising an ordinary person, the performer allowed a spiritual presence to become visible through an accepted ceremonial form. The mask, costume, songs and choreography together produced the being encountered by spectators. Seen in isolation inside a museum, only one part of that larger ritual mechanism survives.

Many historical masks were also intentionally temporary. Some were designed to be destroyed after ceremonies, making surviving early examples unusually rare because they escaped the normal ritual cycle through collection by museums and overseas expeditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/papua-new-guinea/ceremonial-masks-of-papua-new-guinea" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Ceremonial masks of Papua New Guinea &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Ceremonial masks of Papua New Guinea &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

## What museum display removes from ritual objects

Museum displays preserve fragile objects that might otherwise have disappeared, but they inevitably change how visitors understand them.

Inside a gallery, a spirit board becomes a labelled artwork with information about wood, pigments and date. Historically, however, its significance depended on factors that cannot easily be exhibited:

* its place inside a particular clan's ceremonial house;
* the restricted knowledge explaining the identity of the spirit represented;
* ritual performances involving music, movement and spoken traditions;
* relationships with other sacred objects kept nearby;
* the continuing authority of community members responsible for its care.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/313901" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Spirit Board (Gope</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtSpirit Board (Gope) - Kerewa people - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

Modern museums increasingly acknowledge this loss of context by presenting ceremonial objects as parts of living cultural traditions rather than isolated examples of "tribal art". Labels now often emphasise that these carvings functioned within complex religious and social systems instead of existing solely as aesthetic creations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/313901" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Spirit Board (Gope</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtSpirit Board (Gope) - Kerewa people - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

## Living presence versus art object

The contrast between traditional understanding and museum presentation helps explain why debates about ownership and display remain complex.

For many twentieth-century collectors, spirit boards and masks were valued primarily for their artistic innovation or rarity. Anthropological evidence, however, records that originating communities often understood them through relationships of responsibility, ancestry and spiritual presence rather than through artistic appreciation alone. The object's significance was inseparable from ceremony, place and inherited knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/313901" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Spirit Board (Gope</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtSpirit Board (Gope) - Kerewa people - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

This difference also explains why discussions about repatriation cannot assume that every community wants every historical object returned. In Papua New Guinea, some ceremonial objects continue to hold deep cultural meaning, while others have become detached from the knowledge systems that once animated them. ## Why this perspective matters

Seeing Gulf spirit boards and ceremonial masks as living presences rather than decorative carvings transforms a museum visit. The carvings become evidence of entire ceremonial worlds in which sculpture, architecture, dance, music, initiation and ancestral relationships formed a single system.

That perspective also encourages a more careful reading of Papua New Guinea's collections. Many of the country's most remarkable museum objects were never intended to stand alone in display cases.     return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
