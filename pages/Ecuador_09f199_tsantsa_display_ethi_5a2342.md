## Introduction

Should [Ecuador]({{ 'what-makes-ecuadors-strangest/' | relative_url }})'s tsantsa be displayed at all? There is no simple yes-or-no answer. Today, most museum professionals, Indigenous representatives and heritage authorities agree that tsantsa are not merely unusual artefacts or tourist curiosities. They are human remains with deep cultural significance, shaped by a history of ritual practice, colonial collecting and an international market that encouraged exploitation. The debate has therefore shifted away from whether shrunken heads are fascinating towards how they should be interpreted, who has the authority to decide their future, and whether display, storage, repatriation or ceremonial care is the most respectful option. Recent returns of tsantsa to Ecuador and collaborations with Shuar communities show that museums are increasingly treating them as living cultural heritage rather than macabre exhibits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/tzantza-restituted-ecuador" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">tzantza restituted ecuador</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Tzantza Restituted to Ecuador &#124; UNESCOJune 13, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 13, 2025</span></span></span>

## What tsantsa meant in Shuar and Achuar ritual life

Public discussion often reduces tsantsa to "shrunken heads", but that description obscures their original cultural context. Ceremonial tsantsa were created by some Indigenous peoples of the upper Amazon, particularly the Shuar and, to a lesser extent in historical accounts, the Achuar, as part of complex social and spiritual traditions rather than as decorative objects or collectibles. Their significance was bound to ideas about warfare, kinship, ritual power and relationships between communities.

Modern museums increasingly stress that these objects cannot be understood simply through their physical appearance. Detached from their original ceremonial setting, they easily become spectacles that reinforce stereotypes portraying Amazonian peoples as violent or "primitive". This concern has become one of the strongest arguments for rethinking traditional museum displays.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.prm.ox.ac.uk/proyecto-tsantsa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prm.ox.ac.uk">[prm.ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prm.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Proyecto Tsantsa &#124; Pitt Rivers Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Proyecto Tsantsa &#124; Pitt Rivers Museum</span></span></span>

## How collecting created fakes and lost identities

The greatest ethical difficulty is not only that many tsantsa entered museums through unequal colonial relationships, but that the nineteenth- and early twentieth-century international curiosity trade fundamentally changed what was being collected.

European and North American demand for exotic souvenirs created a profitable market. Historical research indicates that commercial demand encouraged the manufacture of additional heads specifically for sale, while skilled forgers also produced convincing imitations using animal remains or other materials. As a result, museum collections around the world contain mixtures of authentic ceremonial tsantsa, commercial-era examples and outright fakes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-021-00518-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The authentication and repatriation of a ceremonial tsantsa to its country of origin (Ecuador) &#124; npj Heritage Science...</span></span></span>

This has several consequences.

First, provenance is frequently poor. Many museums cannot identify the individual, community or exact circumstances under which a particular tsantsa was acquired.

Second, authenticity is uncertain. Modern scientific examination—including anatomical study, imaging and forensic analysis—is often required before museums can determine whether a specimen is genuinely human and whether it was produced using traditional techniques.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-021-00518-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The authentication and repatriation of a ceremonial tsantsa to its country of origin (Ecuador) &#124; npj Heritage Science...</span></span></span>

Third, commercial collecting erased personal identities. Many surviving specimens are catalogued only as anonymous ethnographic objects, making it impossible to reconnect them with named individuals or specific communities.

## Why display has become so controversial

The ethical debate today is less about preserving tsantsa than about displaying them.

Traditional ethnographic museums frequently exhibited shrunken heads alongside weapons and "primitive" curiosities, encouraging visitors to view them as shocking trophies. Critics argue that this presentation stripped them of cultural meaning while reinforcing racist narratives about Indigenous peoples.

A growing number of museums have therefore removed tsantsa from permanent public galleries. One of the best-known examples is the Pitt Rivers Museum in Oxford, which withdrew its tsantsa from display after concluding that visitors often interpreted them through stereotypes rather than understanding their cultural context. The museum now works directly with Shuar representatives to decide whether and how such remains should ever be exhibited again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.prm.ox.ac.uk/proyecto-tsantsa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prm.ox.ac.uk">[prm.ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prm.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Proyecto Tsantsa &#124; Pitt Rivers Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Proyecto Tsantsa &#124; Pitt Rivers Museum</span></span></span>

The shift reflects a broader principle increasingly applied to human remains worldwide: educational value alone is no longer considered sufficient justification for permanent display if descendant communities object or if the display causes continuing harm.

## Authentication, repatriation and respectful display

Recent events involving Ecuador illustrate how museum practice is changing.

A tsantsa held for decades in the Vatican Museums was returned to Ecuador after technical examination confirmed it to be an authentic human ceremonial object associated with the Shuar. Following conservation and study by Ecuador's National Institute of Cultural Heritage, it was transferred to the Pumapungo Museum in Cuenca, where authorities intended it to be interpreted within Ecuador's most important ethnographic collection rather than as an isolated curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.patrimoniocultural.gob.ec/tzantza-ecuatoriana-que-reposaba-en-el-vaticano-llega-a-pumapungo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: patrimoniocultural.gob.ec">[patrimoniocultural.gob.ec]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">patrimoniocultural.gob.ec</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.ec.</span></span></span>

The process demonstrated several modern principles:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Scientific authentication** before making decisions about ownership or display.
* **Recognition as cultural heritage**, not merely an anthropological specimen.
* **Government-to-government cooperation** in returning culturally significant human remains.
* **Controlled interpretation** that explains historical and Indigenous context instead of presenting sensational imagery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.patrimoniocultural.gob.ec/tzantza-ecuatoriana-que-reposaba-en-el-vaticano-llega-a-pumapungo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: patrimoniocultural.gob.ec">[gob.ec]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">patrimoniocultural.gob.ec</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.ec.</span></span></span>

</div>

The process has continued internationally. In 2025, a Swiss family voluntarily returned another authenticated tsantsa to Ecuador with support from UNESCO and specialists at the University of Geneva after learning more about its cultural significance. The case reflects a growing willingness among private holders to treat such objects as heritage requiring respectful return rather than collectible curiosities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/tzantza-restituted-ecuador" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">tzantza restituted ecuador</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Tzantza Restituted to Ecuador &#124; UNESCOJune 13, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 13, 2025</span></span></span>

## Does repatriation always mean public display ends?

Not necessarily.

Repatriation and display are separate questions. Returning a tsantsa to Ecuador does not automatically mean it will disappear into storage, nor does keeping one in a museum automatically imply disrespect.

Instead, museums increasingly ask several practical questions before deciding what should happen:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Is the tsantsa authentic?
* Is its provenance adequately documented?
* Have descendant communities been consulted?
* Does public display educate visitors without sensationalising human remains?
* Are there cultural or spiritual reasons that favour restricted access instead?

</div>

These questions recognise that no single solution fits every case. Different Indigenous communities may have different expectations regarding ancestral remains, and decisions increasingly depend on consultation rather than assumptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.prm.ox.ac.uk/proyecto-tsantsa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prm.ox.ac.uk">[prm.ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prm.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Proyecto Tsantsa &#124; Pitt Rivers Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Proyecto Tsantsa &#124; Pitt Rivers Museum</span></span></span>

## What visitors should expect in Ecuador today

Visitors expecting horror-museum presentations are likely to be surprised.

Where tsantsa are displayed in Ecuador's major public institutions, interpretation increasingly emphasises Indigenous history, colonial collecting and cultural meaning instead of shock value. Displays tend to be incorporated into wider ethnographic exhibitions that explain Amazonian societies rather than isolating the heads as bizarre curiosities.

This approach also reflects a broader change in Ecuador's heritage policy. Human remains are increasingly treated as subjects requiring dignity, research and cultural dialogue rather than simply as museum attractions.     Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
