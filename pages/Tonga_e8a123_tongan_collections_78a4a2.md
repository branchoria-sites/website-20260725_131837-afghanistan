## Introduction

Tongan artefact collections are unusual because many of the most important examples of Tongan heritage are not held only in [Tonga]({{ 'where-tongas-strangest-treasures-still/' | relative_url }}). Royal objects, ceremonial items, barkcloth, weapons, tools, and everyday objects connected with status and exchange are spread across museums in Tonga, New Zealand, Australia, Britain, the United States, and elsewhere. Their journeys overseas reflect exploration, collecting, gifting, trade, missionary activity, academic research, and changing ideas about what museums should preserve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

For visitors interested in unusual collections, the story is less about a single “lost treasure” and more about a scattered heritage landscape. A Tongan object in an overseas museum can be a record of craftsmanship, royal authority, family relationships, and ceremony — but also a reminder that museums must now ask who collected it, how it travelled, and whose knowledge should shape its interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/learn/for-museums-and-galleries/how-guides/collection-management/collection-management-repatriatio-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Repatriation research: Provenance research &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Repatriation research: Provenance research &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

## Where Tonga’s lost collections can be found

Tongan heritage collections exist in several different forms. The Tonga National Museum represents the country’s own effort to gather and interpret national heritage, while larger overseas collections preserve thousands of Pacific objects collected over centuries. Institutions such as the Museum of New Zealand Te Papa Tongarewa, the Australian Museum, and the British Museum hold Tongan material ranging from ceremonial textiles to carved objects and weapons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

This creates a distinctive situation: a visitor researching Tonga’s heritage may need to look beyond Tonga itself. A royal object or textile may survive in an overseas catalogue with detailed records, while similar objects in Tonga may continue to exist as living cultural items used in ceremonies rather than simply displayed behind glass.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

### Royal objects, textiles, and ceremonial items

Among the most recognisable Tongan objects in overseas collections are ngatu (barkcloth), ceremonial textiles made from prepared bark fibres and decorated with patterns that can record identity, history, and important occasions. Museum catalogues show that Tongan barkcloth has travelled widely: the British Museum, for example, holds multiple examples ranging from modern pieces collected in the twentieth century to older examples whose acquisition histories are more complex.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1978" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumbarkcloth &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumbarkcloth &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Ngatu is particularly important because it challenges the idea that museum objects are simply “things”. In Tonga, barkcloth has long been connected with major social events, including births, marriages, funerals, and exchanges that reinforce relationships. A museum display that presents it only as a decorative textile can miss the wider social system that gives it meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_2016-Q-62" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumbarkcloth &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumbarkcloth &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Other collections include clubs, combs, baskets, tools, and objects connected with chiefly status. Te Papa’s digital collections, for example, include Tongan material such as ceremonial baskets, woodcarving, figurative works, ngatu, kava clubs, and other objects illustrating different aspects of Tongan life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span> The British Museum’s Tongan collections similarly include weapons and tools, with catalogue records showing objects acquired through purchases, donations, and earlier collecting networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1982" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumclub &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumclub &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

## Why Tongan artefacts travelled overseas

The movement of Tongan objects abroad began through several overlapping processes rather than one single event. European voyages through the Pacific created early opportunities for collecting, while later periods saw objects acquired by missionaries, colonial officials, researchers, travellers, and private collectors. Some objects entered museums through direct gifts or exchanges; others arrived through less clearly documented routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/x56059" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Collections Online &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

Museum catalogues often reveal how varied these histories are. A Tongan barkcloth object in the British Museum, for example, may have a precise record showing a donor, collector, location, and date, while another object may have only a broad origin or an uncertain acquisition history. This difference matters because provenance — the record of an object’s origin and ownership history — affects how museums understand responsibility and interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Oc1978" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumbarkcloth &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumbarkcloth &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

### Collecting, exchange, and changing ideas of ownership

Not every overseas Tongan object was taken without consent. Pacific collecting histories include genuine exchanges, diplomatic gifts, and relationships between visitors and local communities. The Australian Museum’s Muriel Snell Collection is one example: Snell lived in Tonga as a teacher in the 1950s and collected or received around 200 objects from Tonga and Papua New Guinea through relationships formed during her time there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://australian.museum/learn/cultures/pasifika-collections/tongan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: australian.museum">[The Australian Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">australian.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Australian Museum Tongan cultural material</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Australian MuseumTongan cultural material - The Australian Museum...</span></span></span>

However, the existence of a gift or purchase record does not always settle modern questions about cultural ownership. Many communities now argue that museums must consider not only legal acquisition but also cultural connections, descendants, and the continuing significance of objects. This has shifted museum practice away from treating collections as permanently separated from their communities of origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/learn/for-museums-and-galleries/how-guides/collection-management/collection-management-repatriatio-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Repatriation research: Provenance research &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Repatriation research: Provenance research &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

## Communities reshaping museum interpretations

The most significant change in recent decades has been the move towards collaboration. Overseas museums increasingly work with Pacific communities to improve cataloguing, interpretation, and access. Instead of viewing Tongan artefacts only as historical specimens, museums are beginning to present them as parts of continuing cultural traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

This matters because older museum descriptions were often written from an outsider’s perspective. A carved object might once have been labelled simply as a “weapon” or “decorative item”, while community knowledge can reveal connections to status, genealogy, ceremony, or personal history. The object itself may not have changed, but the meaning presented to visitors can change significantly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/learn/for-museums-and-galleries/how-guides/collection-management/collection-management-repatriatio-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Repatriation research: Provenance research &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Repatriation research: Provenance research &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

Digital collections have become especially important in this process. Online databases allow researchers and communities in Tonga and the wider Pacific to examine objects held overseas without requiring every item to physically return. Te Papa’s online Tonga collections, for example, provide public access to records and images of Tongan objects held in its care.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

## The debate over return, access, and cultural stewardship

The question surrounding overseas Tongan collections is not simply whether objects should stay abroad or return home. Different objects have different histories, and communities may have different priorities. Some may seek physical return; others may value loans, shared exhibitions, improved documentation, or stronger community involvement in interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/learn/for-museums-and-galleries/how-guides/collection-management/collection-management-repatriatio-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Repatriation research: Provenance research &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Repatriation research: Provenance research &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

For Tonga, dispersed collections can be both a challenge and an opportunity. Overseas museums preserve important examples of Tongan artistry and history, sometimes with detailed conservation facilities and research resources. At the same time, the separation of objects from their cultural settings raises questions about whether a museum can fully explain an item without the voices of the people whose traditions created it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tepapa.govt.nz">[Te Papa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tepapa.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Papa Tonga &#124; Te Papa</span></span></span>

The unusual character of Tongan artefact collections therefore lies in their double identity. They are museum objects preserved around the world, but they are also parts of living cultural systems.
