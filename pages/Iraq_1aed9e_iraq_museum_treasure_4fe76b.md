## Introduction

The Iraq Museum in Baghdad is one of the world’s most important collections of ancient objects connected with humanity’s earliest ideas about gods, protection and the unseen world. It is not a “magic museum” in the modern fantasy sense, but its galleries preserve real objects that show how Mesopotamian people tried to influence forces they could not see: illness, demons, bad luck, divine judgement and spiritual danger. The museum’s treasures include ritual objects, religious images, inscriptions and archaeological finds from Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian and later Iraqi cultures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theiraqmuseum.com/pages/about-the-museum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theiraqmuseum.com">[theiraqmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theiraqmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span></span></span>

What makes these collections unusual is the way they blur boundaries between religion, medicine, folklore and early science. A carved figure, a written tablet or a small protective object was not merely decorative. For many ancient communities, words, images and rituals could create a relationship between humans and invisible powers. The Iraq Museum allows visitors to see these beliefs through physical evidence rather than later legends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theiraqmuseum.com/pages/about-the-museum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theiraqmuseum.com">[theiraqmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theiraqmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span></span></span>

## Ritual objects that reveal ancient protective beliefs

Mesopotamian “magic” was not a separate activity hidden away from ordinary life. It was closely connected with religion, healing and attempts to maintain order in a dangerous world. People used rituals and objects because they believed the correct words, symbols and ceremonies could restore balance when sickness, misfortune or supernatural threats appeared.

Among the most fascinating objects linked to this tradition are protective amulets and ritual pieces associated with demons and divine guardians. Ancient Mesopotamian beliefs included many harmful supernatural beings, but these were not simply monsters from stories. They were part of explanations for real human fears, including disease, childbirth complications and unexplained suffering.

A well-known example from Mesopotamian culture is the demon Lamashtu, associated with danger to mothers and infants. Protective objects sometimes showed Lamashtu alongside Pazuzu, a powerful demon figure whose image could be used against her. Modern museums interpret these objects as evidence of ancient protective practices rather than proof that supernatural forces existed. Their importance lies in showing how people in the ancient Near East explained and managed fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/326961" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Amulet with a Lamashtu demon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtAmulet with a Lamashtu demon - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

The Iraq Museum’s significance comes from keeping these traditions within their original Iraqi archaeological setting. Instead of displaying isolated “curiosities”, it presents the wider world that produced them: cities, temples, royal courts and households where beliefs about protection shaped daily life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theiraqmuseum.com/pages/about-the-museum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theiraqmuseum.com">[theiraqmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theiraqmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span></span></span>

## The strange world of incantation bowls and written protection

One of the most unusual categories of ancient magical objects connected with Iraq is the incantation bowl. These simple clay vessels appear ordinary at first glance, but their surfaces carry spiralling inscriptions containing protective formulas. Many were produced in Mesopotamia during late antiquity, especially between the sixth and eighth centuries CE.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumincantation bowl &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumincantation bowl &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

The bowls are sometimes called “[magic bowls]({{ 'magic-bowls/' | relative_url }})” because their texts were intended to provide protection. Some contain prayers, commands against harmful beings or references to supernatural threats. Archaeologists and historians understand them as evidence of religious and magical practice among communities living in Mesopotamia, including Jewish, Christian, Mandaean and other groups who used written ritual traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.iris.unict.it/handle/20.500.11769/53977" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iris.unict.it">[Iris]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iris.unict.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Iris Two Incantation Bowls from the Iraq Museum (Baghdad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Two Incantation Bowls from the Iraq Museum (Baghdad)January 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2005</span></span></span>

Their design adds to their mystery. The writing often spirals from the outside towards the centre, creating the impression of words physically enclosing or trapping danger. Some scholars have interpreted this layout as part of the ritual idea behind the objects: the harmful force was symbolically drawn into the centre and controlled by the inscription.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumincantation bowl &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumincantation bowl &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

The Iraq Museum holds a significant collection of incantation bowls, including examples studied by specialists in ancient languages and Mesopotamian culture. Research on these objects is valuable because the bowls reveal everyday beliefs rather than only official royal religion. They show ordinary people seeking protection for homes, families and personal problems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.iris.unict.it/handle/20.500.11769/53977" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iris.unict.it">[Iris]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iris.unict.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Iris Two Incantation Bowls from the Iraq Museum (Baghdad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Two Incantation Bowls from the Iraq Museum (Baghdad)January 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2005</span></span></span>

For modern visitors, these objects are among the clearest examples of why the Iraq Museum feels unusual. They are not mysterious because their purpose is unknown; they are mysterious because they preserve a worldview in which written words could act as a form of defence.

## Mythical beings hidden inside archaeological treasures

The Iraq Museum’s ancient collections also reveal a world filled with hybrid creatures and symbolic beings. Mesopotamian art frequently combined human, animal and supernatural features to express ideas about power, protection and divine authority.

A famous example is the tradition of protective guardian figures such as the lamassu, winged beings with human heads and animal bodies that stood at the entrances of important Assyrian buildings. These figures were not monsters in the modern sense. They were protective symbols designed to guard important spaces and communicate royal strength.

Although some of the best-known lamassu sculptures are connected with Assyrian palace sites such as Nimrud and Nineveh, the Iraq Museum places such imagery within the broader story of Iraqi civilisation. The museum’s collections include Assyrian, Babylonian and Sumerian material that shows how supernatural imagery was used across thousands of years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/re-opening-national-museum-bagdad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Re-opening of the National Museum of Bagdad &#124; UNESCO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Re-opening of the National Museum of Bagdad &#124; UNESCO...</span></span></span>

Another important treasure is the Warka Vase from ancient Uruk, one of the earliest surviving examples of narrative art. Found in a temple complex associated with the goddess Inanna, it shows scenes of offerings and ritual order rather than “magic” in the later occult sense. Its importance is that it reveals a world where religion, kingship, agriculture and divine relationships were closely connected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.worldhistory.org/image/10269/the-warka-vase-at-the-iraq-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldhistory.org">[World History Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World History Encyclopedia The Warka Vase at the Iraq Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World History EncyclopediaThe Warka Vase at the Iraq Museum - World History EncyclopediaMarch 21, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 21, 2019</span></span></span>

These objects demonstrate that ancient Mesopotamian beliefs were not divided neatly into religion on one side and magic on the other. Protection rituals, temple ceremonies and images of supernatural beings all belonged to a shared understanding of how the universe worked.

## Why the Iraq Museum’s survival matters


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The story of the Iraq Museum is also part of the meaning of its unusual treasures. The institution has survived war, political upheaval and major losses. Established in the 1920s, it became the central public home for Iraq’s archaeological heritage, preserving objects from thousands of years of Mesopotamian history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theiraqmuseum.com/pages/about-the-museum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theiraqmuseum.com">[theiraqmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theiraqmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span></span></span>

</div>

The museum suffered severe damage and looting after the 2003 invasion of Iraq, when many artefacts disappeared from its collections. International efforts later helped recover some stolen objects, while Iraqi specialists continued the work of documenting and protecting what remained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/re-opening-national-museum-bagdad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Re-opening of the National Museum of Bagdad &#124; UNESCO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Re-opening of the National Museum of Bagdad &#124; UNESCO...</span></span></span>

This history gives the museum’s ancient protective objects a modern relevance. Items once created to guard homes and communities now require protection themselves. The survival of tablets, bowls, statues and ritual objects allows researchers to study beliefs that would otherwise survive only through fragmented texts.

The museum’s collections therefore represent more than strange historical objects. They preserve evidence of how people thousands of years ago responded to uncertainty. Ancient Mesopotamian “magic” was a system of ideas about safety, health, fate and the relationship between humans and invisible powers. The Iraq Museum makes that world tangible through objects that still carry traces of the fears and hopes of their original owners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theiraqmuseum.com/pages/about-the-museum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theiraqmuseum.com">[theiraqmuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theiraqmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About the Museum &#124; The Iraq Museum</span></span></span>
